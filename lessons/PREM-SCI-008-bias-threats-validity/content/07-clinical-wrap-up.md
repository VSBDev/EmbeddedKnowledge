# Clinical wrap-up: search the dinner-timing study for missing evidence

This is how the dinner-timing study is searched for the evidence it might be missing.

**Illustrative teaching example—not medical advice.** This invented case is about study reasoning. It does not claim that dinner timing changes glucose.

**Target estimate:** the dinner-timing study now estimates the difference in recorded fasting glucose between its earlier-dinner and later-dinner groups during the stated study period.

## Follow the estimate through six gates

At each gate, name the mechanism before naming the bias. Then identify the estimate or conclusion at risk and choose a repair that reaches the same stage.

1. **Entry—scope and transportability.** The people who volunteered and then kept a dinner log may differ systematically from all eligible people the study meant to represent. That can limit how well the comparison applies beyond the studied volunteers, but it does not by itself show selection bias in the earlier-versus-later dinner comparison. That conclusion would require evidence that entry or log initiation depended on dinner-timing status and fasting-glucose values, or on determinants of both, in a way that changed the target contrast. Keep an eligibility, invitation, enrolment, and log-start record with a denominator at every step; compare what is known about people who entered with people who did not.
2. **Measurement—information bias.** Reported dinner times may be wrong, or the meter record may be misclassified. Either mechanism can place a night in the wrong dinner-time group or attach the wrong fasting-glucose record. Use timestamped dinner logs, retain the original meter records, and apply the same prespecified classification checks to both groups. Without an error pattern tied to group or glucose value, leave the direction open.
3. **Reading and coding—observer bias.** A person who knows the hypothesis might read a glucose record or decide whether a borderline dinner time counts as “late” in a way that favors the expected pattern. Mask the outcome reader and dinner-time coder to the hypothesis and comparison result, give both a prespecified rule, and check disagreements before the group summary is revealed.
4. **Retention—attrition bias.** Suppose participants in the later-dinner group with higher recorded fasting-glucose values leave more often, and the final analysis omits them. That mechanism removes higher values from the later-dinner group, so it threatens the earlier-versus-later difference and would likely pull the later-minus-earlier estimate downward. Keep the full enrolled-cohort denominator, reasons for leaving, and all available earlier records; show outcome availability by group instead of presenting retained participants as the original cohort.
5. **Dissemination—publication bias or selective reporting.** If an entire study with a null dinner-timing result is never written up, whole-study visibility depends on the result: publication bias threatens the conclusion drawn from the visible evidence base, not the arithmetic of this one completed study. If the study is written up but its null fasting-glucose comparison is omitted, the concern is selective reporting within the study. Match the repair to the level: search dated study-completion records for missing whole studies, and compare this study's report with its dated outcome plan so results are accounted for regardless of direction.
6. **Completion filter—survivorship bias.** An analysis restricted to participants who completed every study night admits only people who passed a persistence filter. This can also describe the analysis-stage result of attrition; the useful move is to name the filter and when it acted. Report the original enrolled denominator, preserve partial records, show how many nights and participants were excluded, and do not require perfect completion merely to enter the analysis.

## Finish with the strongest defensible statement

The attrition mechanism above supports a directional claim because it specifies which group lost which values. The other concerns identify routes that could alter the target estimate or the visible evidence, but their directions remain unknown until the corresponding entry, measurement, retention, completion, or dissemination records show what went missing.

This audit does not redraw a confounding map and does not design a replication. It follows only the evidence path, names the threatened estimate, and chooses stage-matched checks. The next lesson, **Replication, reproducibility, and transparency**, asks whether the finding holds up and can be checked.
