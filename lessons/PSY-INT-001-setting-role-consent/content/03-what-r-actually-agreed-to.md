# What R. actually agreed to, and when it changed

Nothing in R.'s appointment was signed. The agreement was made in speech, across five exchanges, and it was different at the end from what it had been at the start. Here is the whole of it, reconstructed from the record. Every line is invented for teaching.

> **Waiting area, before the appointment.** Receptionist: "There's a medical student sitting in this afternoon. Is that all right?" R., standing, coat on, student two metres away: "Yeah, that's fine."
>
> **Minute 0.** Clinician: "I'm a psychiatrist here. This is a final-year medical student, sitting in to learn. What I want to do in the next fifty minutes is understand what's been going on since the spring and work out with you what might help. I'll write to your GP afterwards. You can read that letter, and if there's anything in it you'd rather we talked about first, say so and we will. Some of what I ask will be personal. You can decline any question and it doesn't stop the rest."
>
> **Minute 24.** R.: "Can I ask — does she have to be here for all of it?" Clinician: "No. Would you rather she stepped out?" R.: "Yeah." The student left and did not return.
>
> **Minute 32.** R.: "What actually goes in the letter?" Clinician: "What we've talked about that your GP needs in order to help — how long this has been going on, the sleep, the weight, the drinking, what we agree to do next. Not everything you've said. And there's one thing I can't leave to you: if I thought you were in immediate danger, or someone else was, I'd have to act on that, and I'd tell you I was doing it."
>
> **Minute 45.** R.: "Does the drinking have to be in it?" Clinician: "Yes, because it's tangled up with the sleep and it changes what's worth trying. I'll write what you told me and not more than that."

:::{definition}
:id: definition-informed-consent
:label: Informed consent

**Informed consent** is a person's voluntary agreement to something proposed, given with enough information to make the choice mean something, by a person able to make it. It is a continuing arrangement rather than an event. A signature or a note records that the discussion happened at a particular moment and is evidence of the agreement, not the agreement itself.
:::

:::{worked-example}
:id: worked-example-consent-as-arrangement

**Task.** Decide what R. had agreed to after each exchange, identify where the agreement changed, and say which change would have been impossible under a different setup.

**What is given.** Five exchanges, four of them timed. R. is an adult, referred, attending voluntarily. The service is a clinic; the letter goes to one GP. What is not given: the jurisdiction, the clinic's policy on student observation, and whether the waiting-area question was documented anywhere.

**Principle selected.** Treat the agreement as a process with a state that changes, and read each exchange for three things: what was disclosed, whether the agreement was free, and what the person could still change afterwards. That is the model Canadian professional guidance on consent to psychiatric treatment sets out. Consent is described there as a process rather than an instant, to be reviewed, revised, and renewed as circumstances change, with the form or note as the record of a moment and not the consent itself. The same guidance holds that consent does not bind the person to proceed and does not remove their right to withdraw at any time.

**Plan.** Take the exchanges in order, treating minutes 32 and 45 as one move made twice. For each: name what was agreed, name what was disclosed to support it, and mark whether the agreement was free. Then look for the point where the state changed, and test it by altering one feature of the setup.

**Execution.**

*Waiting area.* R. agreed to a student being present. Disclosure: that a student existed. Not disclosed: who the student was, what she was there to do, whether she would ask questions, or whether declining would affect anything. Freedom: weak. R. was standing, in outdoor clothes, in front of the person being agreed to, seconds before an appointment R. had waited weeks for. The Canadian guidance lists institutional constraints, fear, and undue influence among the circumstances that compromise voluntariness, and a waiting-area question put in front of the observer carries at least the third. That the answer was yes tells you very little.

*Minute 0.* The clinician disclosed their own role, the student's role, the purpose of the appointment, what would be written and to whom together with R.'s freedom to read and query it, and that R. could decline any question. Requirements for informed consent to treatment include the nature and purpose of what is proposed, its likely benefits and probable risks, the alternatives, the consequences of declining, and the identity of the person providing it. The minute-0 statement covers purpose, identity, and consequences of declining. It does not cover risks or alternatives, which is defensible here because nothing has yet been proposed to consent to. The agreement at minute 0 is an agreement to have the conversation.

*Minute 24.* The state changed. R. withdrew the waiting-area yes. The clinician's answer did two things at once: it confirmed the withdrawal was available, and it made no request for a reason. Compare a plausible alternative reply, "she's only got two more weeks on this placement" — accurate, unremarkable, and enough to convert a withdrawable agreement into one that costs something to withdraw.

*Minutes 32 and 45.* R. tested the record twice, once in general and once on a specific item. The clinician answered specifically both times and, at minute 32, named the one limit that R. does not control. Notice what that disclosure is not: it is not "everything you say is confidential", and it is not a recitation of law. It states a boundary, in one sentence, in advance.

**Boundary conditions.** This reading assumes R. can make these decisions. Capacity is decision-specific and is assessed against a particular choice at a particular time: the ability to understand the relevant information and to appreciate what following or refusing would mean. A psychiatric condition does not by itself establish incapacity, and neither does disagreeing with the clinician or making a choice the clinician thinks unwise. Nothing in R.'s record raises the question, and this lesson does not teach how it is assessed.

**Independent check.** Remove the minute-0 statement and keep everything else. R. still tests the record at minute 32, but now for the first time, 32 minutes into a conversation, after the sleep and the weight and the friends. Everything R. said before minute 32 was said without knowing where it was going. R. cannot retrieve those 32 minutes, so the only available correction is to say less from here on. The minute-0 statement costs about forty seconds and buys the rest of the appointment.

**Interpretation.** By the end, R. has agreed to a conversation, agreed and then unagreed to an observer, and agreed to a specific description of that conversation going to one named reader with one stated exception. There is no document anywhere that says any of that. The agreement is real and the record of it is thin, which is the ordinary situation and the reason the discussion has to be deliberate.

**Self-explanation.** Of the five exchanges, one made the rest possible. Which, and what exactly did it supply that the others drew on?
:::

The minute-0 statement is the answer. It established that R. could decline things, which is what R. then did at minute 24, and it named the record as something open to discussion, which is what R. then used at minutes 32 and 45. R. did not have to work out that any of this was permitted; it had already been said.

The waiting-area question is worth one more look, because it is the version the survey numbers describe: asked about, and not introduced. In the Portuguese survey, 75% of psychiatry patients who recalled the encounter said the doctor had asked whether they minded students being present, while 31% said the students had introduced themselves as medical students and asked permission. R.'s waiting-area yes sits in the 75% and outside the 31%: R. was asked, and did not know who was being agreed to. That is exactly the gap that made the yes worth so little, and exactly the gap the minute-0 introduction closed.
