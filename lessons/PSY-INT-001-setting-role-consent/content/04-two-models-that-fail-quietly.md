# Two models of consent that fail quietly

Both of the models below are held by competent people, both produce encounters that look correct from outside, and both fail in ways that leave no complaint and no incident report. That is why they need a scene of their own.

:::{check}
:id: check-predict-the-failure
:kind: prediction

Commit to an answer before reading on. A clinic runs its first appointments like this: the referral letter is read out, the patient signs a one-page form headed "Consent to assessment and information sharing", and the interview begins. The form is complete, dated, countersigned, and filed.

1. Name one thing this clinic will find out later than R.'s clinic did.
2. Name one thing a patient here can do that R. could do, and one thing they cannot.
:::

## The first model: consent is the thing that was obtained at the start

:::{misconception}
:id: misconception-consent-as-event

**The model.** Consent is a discrete act. It happens near the beginning, it is recorded, and afterwards it is a fact about the encounter. Questions such as "was consent obtained?" have a yes or no answer, and the file settles them.

**Why it is held.** It is administratively true. Services audit whether the form exists, forms are countable, and a completed form is genuine evidence that a conversation happened at a moment.

**What it cannot explain.** It cannot explain minute 24. R. agreed to the student in the waiting area and reversed that agreement twenty-four minutes later. Under the event model, the reversal is either a nullity, because consent was already obtained, or an anomaly to be managed. Under the process model, it is the arrangement working as intended. The event model also cannot explain why R. asked at minute 32 what goes in the letter. If consent were settled at the start, that question would be closed; it was not closed, because R. had learned by then what there was to ask about.

**The better model.** Consent is a continuing arrangement, reviewed and renewed as the situation changes. The written record is evidence of the discussion at a moment and does not constitute the agreement. The person's right to withdraw survives the signature, and the state of the agreement at any time is what was last discussed and not what was first written.

**Where the event model comes from.** Partly from law, and law differs. Some Canadian provinces have legislated consent requirements that codify the common-law position while others have not, and Quebec's civil law provides different procedures again; a clinician is expected to know the requirements of the province or territory where they work. A form built to satisfy one of those regimes is a real legal instrument. It still is not the agreement, and a service that treats the two as the same has confused a filing requirement with a conversation.

**Comparing the two on R.'s case.** Ask both models the same question at minute 30: what has R. agreed to? The event model says: assessment and information sharing, as per the signed form. The process model says: to have this conversation, with the student out of the room since minute 24, with a letter going to one GP that R. may read, and with one stated exception R. does not control. Only one of those two answers is usable by the person who has to write the letter that evening.
:::

The failure is quiet because both clinics produce a completed assessment and a filed record. The difference shows up in what the second clinic never hears, and an absence does not audit.

## The second model: presence is not participation

:::{misconception}
:id: misconception-observer-is-neutral

**The model.** A learner who watches without speaking has not done anything to the patient, so the arrangements that govern doing things do not apply. Introductions are a courtesy; consent belongs to the examination and the treatment.

**Why it is held.** It follows from a reasonable premise. Consent attaches to interventions, and watching is not an intervention.

**What it cannot explain.** It cannot explain why patients behave differently when someone is watching. In the Portuguese survey, some psychiatry patients reported that a student's presence made talking about an intimate problem harder. That is a change in what the encounter produces, caused by a person who did nothing. If the observer changes the data, treating the observer as outside the arrangement puts the change beyond anyone's control.

**The better model.** Presence is part of the setting, so it is arranged like the rest of the setting: the person is introduced by name and role, the patient is asked in conditions where declining is easy, and the answer can be revised at any point without a reason. Where the encounter is being observed for teaching, the patient is entitled to know what the observer is there to learn.

**The contested part, stated as contested.** Whether a general consent signed at admission covers a student observer is genuinely disputed. A United States emergency-medicine review from 2006 records that many institutions treat the admission consent as sufficient while other writers hold that explicit consent should be sought for each observation, and states separately that visitors should be present only with the patient's permission. This lesson does not resolve that. It observes that the two readings produce different encounters and that a learner should know which one their institution uses before relying on either.

**Recheck in a new case.** A student sits in on a consultation, says nothing, and afterwards writes a reflective account for a portfolio, changing the patient's name. Did anything happen that the patient should have been asked about? Answer from the acts: someone was present who altered the conditions of disclosure, and a written account of a private conversation now exists in a second place with a second reader. Both are arrangements. Neither is an intervention.
:::

## The related error about role

The third failure follows from the second and is worth naming because it is the one a learner in this course is most likely to make. It is the belief that the boundary between studying psychiatry and practising it is drawn around subject matter, so that reading about suicide risk is bold, discussing it is bolder, and asking someone about it is the natural next step.

The boundary is drawn around acts and the people they land on. Reading R.'s record and arguing about which arrangement produced which fact is study, and it can go as deep as you can take it. Asking a real person a direct question about suicidal thoughts is an act with a person on the other end of it, and whoever asks has committed themselves to what happens next: to hearing the answer, to knowing what to do with it, and to being the person who was there. The evidence that asking does not plant the thought removes one objection to a clinician doing it. It supplies nobody with the **clinical authority**, the supervision, or the route onwards that doing it requires. That term keeps the meaning [Welcome to Psychiatry (PSY-WEL-001)](https://embeddedknowledge.io/psychiatry/lessons/read/?lesson=PSY-WEL-001) gave it: the jurisdiction-dependent responsibility and authorisation to act clinically with real people, which academic knowledge alone does not create.

:::{callout}
:kind: boundary
:id: callout-teaching-boundary-misconception

Teaching example, not medical advice. The clinic in the prediction task, the portfolio case, and R. are invented. Nothing here describes what any real service must do, and the legal position on observers, records, and confidentiality differs by jurisdiction and by institution.
:::

## Feedback on the prediction task

The signed-form clinic will find out later, or not at all, what its patients did not want written down. The form names information sharing in general and settles it; R.'s clinic named one reader and one exception and left the question open, which is why R. raised it twice. A patient at the signed-form clinic can still decline a question, because that right does not depend on being told about it. What they cannot easily do is revise something already agreed, because the arrangement has been recorded as complete and reopening it means contradicting a document with their own signature on it.
