# The sentence that is only there because someone asked

R. is 34, works rotating night shifts in a warehouse, and was referred by a general practitioner to a community mental-health clinic. R. is invented for this course, and every detail here and in the lessons around it was written for teaching. Nothing describes a real person.

The record of R.'s first appointment runs to about a page. One line near the end says that R. has had passive thoughts that life is not worth the effort, with no plan, no intent, and nothing done. R. did not raise it. It is in the record because a direct question was asked forty minutes in, after the medical student had left the room, and after R. had been told what would and would not go into the letter back to the GP.

Four arrangements stand behind that one line: how long the appointment was, who was in the room, what the appointment was for and therefore which questions belonged in it, and what R. had been told about the record. None of the four is a fact about R. All four changed what is now known about R.

That is the subject of this lesson. Someone arranges the conditions under which a person can speak before anyone describes what the person said. The arrangements are part of the clinical material, and an account that leaves them out cannot explain why some things are in it and other things are not. The next scene sets out the full list of six.

## People leave things out, and they can say what they were avoiding

Two large United States survey samples asked adults whether they had ever withheld information from a clinician. Among respondents who reported experiencing depression, suicidal thoughts, abuse, or sexual assault, 47.5% in one sample and 40.0% in the other said they had kept at least one of those from a clinician. The reasons endorsed most often were being embarrassed, not wanting to be judged or lectured, not wanting to be pushed into follow-up they did not want, and not wanting the information in their medical record. That last reason was named by 57.1% and 52.7% of them.

Those are self-reports from online survey panels, not observed encounters, and they carry all the recall and selection problems that go with the method. What they establish is narrower than a prevalence figure and more useful: people who withhold can name what they were avoiding, and more than half of them named the written record among their reasons. The record is something a clinician decides how to describe at the start of a conversation. It is one of the arrangements.

The other common fear belongs to the person asking rather than the person answering, and it is worth naming early because it decides whether the question in R.'s notes gets asked at all. In a two-year study, 170 adolescents who had been discharged from psychiatric hospital were asked about suicidal thoughts repeatedly, and being asked more often was not followed by higher scores; more than half the group scored lower at the end than at the start. That is one study, in adolescents, in continuing treatment, with no control group, so it cannot show that asking helps. It is evidence against a specific worry: that raising the subject plants the thought.

Put the two together and R.'s silence until asked stops looking like a fact about R.'s character. It is a property of an arrangement in which nobody had asked yet.

:::{check}
:id: check-description-inference-carryover
:kind: retrieval

Before reading on, sort these four statements about R.'s appointment into what a listener observed, what R. reported, and what a listener added. Write one line each.

1. R. said the thoughts come mostly on the drive home.
2. The appointment ran 50 minutes.
3. R. answered the direct question without hesitating.
4. R. found the question a relief.

Then say which of the four you would be least willing to write in a record, and why.
:::

Items 2 and 3 are things a person in the room could observe, though 3 already contains a judgement about what counts as hesitating. Item 1 is R.'s report, and it stays R.'s report however plausible it sounds. Item 4 is an addition: nobody has said it, and the only evidence for it is that the answer came easily. The one you should be least willing to write is 4, because writing it turns a listener's impression into a line in a document that R. and other clinicians will later read as though it came from R.

That sorting is the work of the preceding outcome, **Description, inference, and interpretation**. This lesson depends on it and does not repeat it. If the four items were hard to separate, the recovery route at the end of the practice scene gives a way back in that does not require the earlier lesson.

## What this lesson does, and what it leaves to other lessons

By the end you should be able to do four things.

- Trace a named fact in R.'s account back to the specific setup decision that made it available, and say what would have been different under a stated alternative.
- Distinguish an agreement that was negotiated and can be revisited from a signature on a form, and state what has to be disclosed for the agreement to mean anything, including the parts the person cannot control.
- Say, for a described encounter, who holds which role and which acts a person in a non-clinical role may and may not perform, justifying the answer from the act and not from the subject matter.
- Prepare a simulated encounter yourself: settle where it happens and who can hear, who is present and on what basis, who each person is and what the conversation is for, how long there is, what will be written and who reads it, and what has to be arranged for the person to take part at all. Then say what the person has agreed to, and what the limits of that agreement are.

Three things are deliberately absent. This lesson does not describe R.'s mental state, which is the mental-status observation outcome later in this module. It does not ask what might be going on with R. or weigh the possibilities, which the mood outcomes take up further along. And it does not teach how questions are put once the conversation has started, which is open and focused inquiry, the outcome directly after this one.

Teaching example, not medical advice. R., the clinic, the student, the appointment, and every quoted line are invented for this course. You are reading a record of a conversation you did not conduct and are not authorised to conduct. Studying how an encounter was arranged does not authorise you to arrange one with a real person, and nothing in this lesson is a basis for any decision about anyone's care.
