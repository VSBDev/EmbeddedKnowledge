# Six decisions, made before R. said anything

Someone made six decisions about R.'s appointment. Most were settled before R. arrived, and one was put to R. in the waiting area about thirty seconds before it started. Each has a consequence you can find in the record.

:::{definition}
:id: definition-setting
:label: Setting

The **setting** is the arranged conditions of an encounter: the place, the people present, the time available, the way information will be recorded and shared, and what each person is there to do. It is chosen, so it can be chosen differently, and it constrains what a person is able to say.
:::

## 1. Place: who could hear

R.'s appointment happened in a consulting room with a door that shut and a window onto a corridor. The blind was down. A colleague knocked once at minute 22 and was waved away.

The knock is the consequence. R. stopped mid-sentence, and the sentence did not resume in the same form. Nobody recorded what was lost, because a thing that was not said leaves no trace.

Auditory privacy is a property of the building and of how the service runs, and it is not always available. A review of privacy in emergency medicine notes that curtains give visual separation while leaving conversations audible, and that overheard conversation between staff is a routine way confidentiality is compromised without anyone intending it. The point for R.'s clinic is not that the room was adequate. It is that whether the room was adequate is a question with an answer, and someone should have asked it before the appointment instead of after the knock.

## 2. People: who else was there, and on what basis

A final-year medical student sat in for the first 25 minutes. R. was asked in the waiting area whether that was acceptable and said yes. R.'s partner, who drove R. there, waited in the car.

Two things about that arrangement are worth separating. The first is whether R. was asked at all. In a survey at a Portuguese university hospital, 185 psychiatry patients were asked about their experience of medical students in their care. Of those who recalled the encounter, 75% said the doctor had asked whether they minded the students being present, and 31% said the students had introduced themselves as medical students and asked permission themselves. Some respondents reported that a student's presence made it harder to talk about an intimate problem. The gap between those two figures is the interesting part: being asked about a student and knowing who the student is are different pieces of information, and patients received the first far more often than the second.

The second thing is contested, and this lesson does not settle it. A United States emergency-medicine review from 2006 records both readings of the question: many institutions treat the general consent signed at admission as covering a student observer, and other writers hold that explicit consent should be sought for each observation. The same review states that visitors should be present only with the patient's permission. Which reading governs depends on the institution and the jurisdiction, and a 2006 source cannot tell you what any current policy says.

R.'s clinic used the second reading, asked, and got a yes. That yes was later revised, which is the subject of the next scene.

## 3. Role: who each person was, and what each was there to do

The student was introduced by name and as a medical student. The clinician said what the appointment was for, in one sentence, before anything else: to understand what had been happening over the past few months and to work out together what might help.

:::{definition}
:id: definition-role-clarity
:label: Role clarity

**Role clarity** means every person in an encounter knows who the others are, what each is there to do, and which of them carries responsibility for any decision that follows. It is a property of what was said out loud, not of what was true.
:::

Role clarity is not politeness. It determines what a person's agreement is agreement to. Canadian guidance on consent to psychiatric treatment states that the identity of the person who will provide the treatment is part of what has to be disclosed, and that delegating the consent discussion to a different clinician risks missing concerns that matter to the person's decision. Someone who agrees to talk to a doctor has not thereby agreed to talk to a student, and someone who agrees to an assessment has not agreed to a treatment.

That has a direct consequence for you. You are reading this material the way the student sat through the first 25 minutes, and with less standing than she had: present at it, and authorised to act on none of it. The line does not run between easy topics and difficult ones. It runs between acts.

| Act | Where it sits | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reading R.'s record and saying which arrangements produced which facts | Study | The object is a fictional record. Being wrong changes an argument. |
| Asking a real person a direct question about suicidal thoughts because this lesson showed one being asked | Outside | The object is a person, and the act commits whoever asks to what happens next. |
| Rewriting a badly arranged fictional encounter into a better one | Study | The output is an argument about arrangements, inspectable and revisable. |
| Telling a real acquaintance what a clinic would or would not put in their letter | Outside | It is a statement about a service's actual practice and law, made without standing to know either. |

Notice that the topic is identical down the column. Every row is about suicide, records, and clinics. What moves an act across the line is who it lands on and what follows from getting it wrong.

## 4. Time: how much there was, and when

R.'s appointment was booked for 50 minutes at 4pm. R. had finished a night shift at 6am, slept badly, and asked twice how long this would take, because the next shift started at 10pm.

Fifty minutes is a choice a service made, and services choose very differently. A systematic review of 179 studies covering more than 28 million consultations in 67 countries found average primary-care consultation length ranging from 48 seconds in Bangladesh to 22.5 minutes in Sweden, with 18 countries containing about half the world's population at five minutes or less. Those are averages across whole health systems, from published data up to 2016, and none of them is a psychiatric assessment. They are enough to make one thing clear: an account of interviewing that assumes an hour is describing one kind of service, and the person reading it may work somewhere the number is five.

Time changes the order of a conversation, and the order changes what gets reached. In R.'s appointment the alcohol question came at minute 34 and the direct question about suicidal thoughts at minute 40. Either could have been the thing that did not fit.

## 5. Record: what would be written, and who would read it

Before the history began, the clinician said three things: a letter would go to R.'s GP, R. could read it, and R. could ask for anything in it to be discussed first.

Whether the clinician can promise that everything stays between them is a separate question, with an answer that depends on where the clinic is. In the United States, statutory duties to warn or protect a third party after a threat differ state by state: one review counts roughly 27 to 33 states with mandatory duties, 9 to 11 with permissive ones, and 6 to 13 with no statute, the ranges reflecting disagreement about how particular statutes should be classified. Other countries organise the question through professional regulation or general law instead. A clinician is expected to know the rule where they practise.

:::{definition}
:id: definition-confidentiality
:label: Confidentiality

**Confidentiality** is the duty to keep what a person discloses within the care of the people who need it for that person's care. It is strong and it is not absolute. Its limits are set by the law and professional regulation of a particular place, which is why a description of them is only accurate for one jurisdiction at a time.
:::

Saying this at the start rather than at the moment it becomes relevant is a setup decision with a cost and a benefit. The cost is that it introduces a caution into a conversation before there is anything to be cautious about. The benefit is that anything R. says afterwards was said knowing it.

## 6. Communication access: whether the conversation is possible at all

R. speaks the clinician's language, so nothing was arranged. That is itself a decision, and it is the one most often made by not thinking about it.

:::{definition}
:id: definition-communication-access
:label: Communication access

**Communication access** covers the arrangements that make an encounter usable by the person in it: a professional interpreter, a signed-language interpreter, longer appointments, written summaries, a hearing loop, an easy-read explanation, or a communication partner. It is arranged before the encounter, because it cannot be improvised inside it.
:::

A systematic review of 28 studies found that professional interpreters were associated with fewer communication errors and better comprehension, service use, clinical outcomes, and satisfaction than ad hoc interpreters, meaning family members, accompanying friends, or untrained bilingual staff. Most of those studies were observational and carried out in the United States, so the review reports an association across a varied literature and not the result of a trial. The practical reading is narrow and firm enough to act on: the person who came with the patient is not the arrangement, and using them as one degrades what the encounter can produce.

Had R. needed an interpreter and the clinic used the partner waiting in the car, the passive thoughts would probably not be in the record, and the reason would have nothing to do with R.
