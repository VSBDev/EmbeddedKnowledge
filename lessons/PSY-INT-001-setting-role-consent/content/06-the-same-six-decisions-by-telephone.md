# The same six decisions, by telephone

Two weeks later R. has a 15-minute telephone review. R. takes it in the warehouse car park at 9.40pm, twenty minutes before a night shift, with a partner in the passenger seat. The clinician has a phone, a screen, and no idea of any of that.

Everything arranged for the first appointment has to be arranged again, and three of the six decisions now behave differently. The point of this scene is that the six decisions are the transferable part and the specific arrangements are not; a rule learned as "check the room, introduce the student" transfers to nothing.

## What the telephone takes away

**Place becomes unverifiable.** In the consulting room, the clinician could see the door. On the phone, the only route to that information is to ask, and the question has to be asked in a form that a person sitting next to someone can answer safely. "Are you somewhere you can talk?" can be answered yes by a person who is not, without lying, because a car with the window up is somewhere you can talk. "Is there anyone with you?" is answerable with a single word in front of that person and cannot be. Whichever question is asked, the clinician has to expect the answer to be shaped by whoever can hear it, and to read a shorter conversation as evidence about the setting and not as evidence about R.

**People becomes invisible, and identity becomes a question.** In the room, the clinician knew who R. was because R. was sitting there. On the phone, the clinician has a voice and a number, and the person who answers a number is not necessarily the person the number belongs to. That check has no counterpart in the first appointment.

**Time is set by the person who has the shift.** Fifteen minutes was the clinic's figure; 9.40pm and a 10pm start are R.'s. The clinician has a slot and R. has a hard stop, and only one of them knows it. If nobody asks how long R. has, the call will end in the middle of something and the record will say the review was completed.

## What stays the same, and is easier to forget

The agreement from two weeks ago does not carry over by itself. R. agreed to a conversation in a room, and consent is renewed as the circumstances change; the last occasion does not supply it. A phone call from a car park with someone else present is a changed circumstance in every respect that matters, and the right to stop it is the same right R. used at minute 24 of the first appointment.

The record question returns in a new form. R. now knows that a letter goes to the GP and knows the exception. What R. does not know is whether this call is being recorded, whether a summary of it joins the same file, and whether anyone else has read the first letter since. More than half of the respondents in two United States surveys who withheld information from a clinician gave keeping it out of the medical record as a reason, and R. has already shown, twice, that this is a live question for R. specifically.

1. Which item on your list has no counterpart in the first appointment, and why does the telephone create it?
2. R. answers "yeah, fine" to your privacy question in a tone you would describe as flat. Name two different setups that produce that answer and say what you would do differently under each.

### Feedback after your attempt

A workable order establishes, first, who is on the phone; then how long R. has, in R.'s numbers and not the clinic's; then whether R. can speak freely where R. is and whether someone else can hear; then whether this call joins the same record and who reads it; then that R. can stop, reschedule, or decline any part without giving a reason. Only after all five does the review begin.

The item with no counterpart is identity. In the room it was settled by R. being in it. This is the clearest sign that the six decisions are the durable thing: the decision "establish who is here" survives into the new setting, while the arrangement that satisfied it does not.

A flat "yeah, fine" is produced by at least two setups: R. is alone and tired at the end of a bad fortnight, or R. is not alone and cannot say so. The two call for different responses, and the useful move is one that works under both without requiring you to know which. Offering a closed question that a person can answer safely in company, and offering a different time, costs little if R. is simply tired and matters a great deal if R. is not alone.

For the time question, the answer that transfers is to give up whatever can be done in writing or at a later appointment and keep whatever depends on this conversation happening now. What must not be given up is the ending: a call cut off by a shift leaves R. in a car park at 10pm holding the last minutes of a mental-health conversation with nowhere to put them.

## Accessibility and alternatives

Every task in this lesson can be answered in prose, in a list, or in a table, and typed, dictated, handwritten, or given aloud to a tutor. Nothing is timed and nothing depends on colour, position on a page, sound, motion, dragging, or a hover. The lesson contains two tables and no images, diagrams, charts, audio, or video. Each table row carries its own reasoning in words and reads as an ordinary sentence when the columns are taken in order, so a reader working through the text in a single column loses nothing. No task asks about your own health, treatment, or experience, and none requires you to disclose anything about yourself.

This lesson describes suicidal thoughts in one fictional person and discusses what makes them easier or harder to talk about. If that is difficult reading today, stop, and come back or do not; nothing here is assessed on speed and nothing is monitored. EmbeddedKnowledge is a set of documents and provides no care of any kind. For personal help, use a qualified local service or your area's current emergency route.
