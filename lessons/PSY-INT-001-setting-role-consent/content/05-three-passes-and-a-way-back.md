# Three passes, with the scaffolding taken away

Work each pass before opening its feedback. The first supplies the procedure, the second supplies only the question, and the third supplies neither.

## First pass: change one thing, find the consequence

The procedure has three steps. Name the setup decision that changed. Name what R. could no longer do, or could now do, as a result. Then name the specific line in the record that appears, disappears, or arrives at a different time.

:::{check}
:id: check-alter-the-setup
:kind: practice

For each change, run the three steps. Keep everything else about R.'s appointment as it was.

1. The appointment is 15 minutes instead of 50.
2. The student stays for the whole appointment and nobody offers R. a way to change that.
3. Nothing is said at minute 0 about the letter to the GP.
4. R.'s partner, who drove R. to the clinic, is invited in to help because R. seems tired.
:::

### Feedback after your attempt

1. The decision is time. At 15 minutes the order of questions becomes the whole of the interview, because the end arrives before the list does. In R.'s appointment the alcohol came at minute 34 and the direct question at minute 40; neither survives a 15-minute slot unless something else is dropped. Fifteen minutes is not an unrealistic figure: across 67 countries, average primary-care consultation length ran from 48 seconds to 22.5 minutes, and 18 countries holding about half the world's population sat at five minutes or less.
2. The decision is people, and the missing piece is the offer to change it. R. can still decline questions, so the likely effect is a shorter, flatter answer to whichever question R. would have preferred the student not to hear, and outright silence is the less likely outcome. In the Portuguese survey, some psychiatry patients reported that a student's presence made an intimate problem harder to discuss. The line that changes is the one about the friends R. has stopped seeing, or the one about the drinking, and you cannot tell which from the record afterwards.
3. The decision is the record. Everything R. says now travels to an unnamed destination. More than half of the respondents in two United States surveys who withheld something from a clinician named keeping it out of the medical record as a reason. R.'s two questions at minutes 32 and 45 do not disappear; they move later, or they turn into a shorter answer where the question would have been.
4. The decision is people, dressed up as communication access. A partner brought in to help is a third person in the room, and a companion is not one of the arrangements that communication access means. A systematic review found professional interpreters associated with fewer errors and better comprehension, outcomes, and satisfaction than family members and other ad hoc interpreters, and the same objection applies with more force to a topic the person may not want the accompanying adult to hear. If R. is tired, the arrangement to make is a second appointment at a different time of day.

## Second pass: start from the record, find the decision

Now the procedure is gone and only the question remains. Each line below is written as it might appear in R.'s record. For each, name the setup decision that produced it.

:::{check}
:id: check-infer-the-decision
:kind: practice

1. "Declined to answer further questions about family history."
2. "Patient became tearful; interview concluded shortly afterwards."
3. "Alcohol intake not established."
4. "Patient asked who else would see the letter."
5. "Collateral history not available at this appointment."
:::

### Feedback after your attempt

1. Someone told R. that declining was available, and R. used it. Absent that, the same silence appears in the record as a fuller answer given reluctantly, or as nothing at all.
2. Two candidates, and the record does not distinguish them. Either the appointment ended because it was finished, or it ended because the slot did. Which one it was is a fact about the setting, and whoever wrote the line knew it and did not write it down.
3. The item was reached and the answer was unclear, or the item was never reached. The second is a time decision and the first is not, and again the record does not say. A record that reported the minute at which the interview ended would settle it.
4. R. knew that a letter existed, so someone said so at the start. The question also tells you R. is thinking about readers beyond the one who was named, which the opening statement did not settle.
5. Nobody arranged for one. Whether a family member or a GP could add anything is a question that has to be raised, and consented to, before the appointment and not during it.

## Third pass: no support at all

:::{check}
:id: check-rebuild-the-encounter
:kind: practice

An invented case, unconnected to R. A community team offers a first appointment to a 27-year-old referred after two visits to an emergency department. As arranged, it runs like this: the appointment is in a shared open-plan assessment area with screens between bays; two team members attend, neither introduced by role; the slot is 20 minutes and a third of it is spent on a laptop entering details; the person's mother is in the bay because she brought them; the person is asked to sign a form headed "consent to assessment" without it being read out; nothing is said about who receives a summary.

Do three things.

1. Rewrite the arrangements. Change only the setup, and for each change say what it buys.
2. Two of the original arrangements are defensible under some conditions. Identify them and state the conditions.
3. Name two facts that the rebuilt encounter could produce and the original could not, and say why each one specifically depends on the change you made.
:::

### Feedback after your attempt

A strong answer changes at least these: a room where the conversation cannot be overheard; both team members introduced by name and role, with the second's reason for attending stated; the mother asked to wait outside for at least part of the appointment, with the person asked in private whether she should return; the laptop either used openly with the screen visible or set aside; a spoken account at the start of what will be written and who will read it, including the limits; and either a longer slot or an explicit statement of what a 20-minute appointment will and will not cover.

The two defensible arrangements are the second team member and the short slot. A second attender is defensible when the person has been told who they are and why they are there, and it is sometimes required, for example where a service's own safety policy calls for two staff. A 20-minute slot is defensible when it is named as a first of several and when what it is for is stated, so that the person is not left believing that everything had to fit.

For the third part, the two facts should each hang on one change. Anything the person did not want the mother to hear depends on the mother leaving, and no other change substitutes for it. Anything the person is only willing to say once they know where it goes depends on the account of the record, which is the reason more than half of the people in those United States surveys who withheld something gave for withholding it. A weaker answer names two facts and attributes both to a general improvement in atmosphere.

## Where to go next

- All three passes clean: continue to the telephone follow-up.
- Trouble in the first pass: you are probably naming the effect on the clinician's workload instead of the effect on what the person can say. Redo item 2 and write only sentences whose subject is R.
- Trouble in the second pass: for each item, write down the two setups that would produce the identical line, then say what would have to be added to the record to tell them apart.
- Trouble in the third pass: rebuild it as a list of the six decisions from the second scene, with the original in one column and yours in the other. If a row is empty, that decision was never made in either version.

## Recovery route

If sorting observation from report from addition in the first scene was where you came unstuck, that is the preceding outcome, **Description, inference, and interpretation**, and this lesson leans on it without teaching it. You can rebuild enough of it here: take any three sentences from a record and, for each, write who would have to be asked to check it. If the answer is the patient, it is a report. If the answer is anyone who was in the room, it is an observation. If there is nobody to ask, someone added it.

If the consent material read as familiar and you found the practice harder than expected, the gap is usually that consent is being held as a topic rather than as a state that changes. Take R.'s five exchanges and write the state after each one as a single sentence beginning "R. has agreed to". Five sentences. If any two are identical, look again at what changed.

If the role boundary is still doing the work of a warning instead of a test, apply the table from the second scene to three tasks you might plausibly do this week. For each, name who it lands on and what follows if you are wrong. The topic is not part of the test and should not appear in your answer.

If the six decisions do not come back from memory, do not reread the scene first. Write the list, then check it, then write the consequence each one had in R.'s appointment. Checking before retrieving is the one order that will not help.
