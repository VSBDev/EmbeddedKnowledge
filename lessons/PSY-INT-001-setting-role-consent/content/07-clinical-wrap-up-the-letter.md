# Clinical wrap-up: one sentence in a letter to a GP

:::{callout}
:kind: boundary
:id: callout-teaching-boundary-wrap-up

Teaching example, not medical advice. R., the clinic, the appointment, and the letter are invented for this course. This scene works through a documentation and consent question and gives no guidance on assessing or responding to suicide risk, which belongs to a later outcome and to supervised clinical training. Nothing here tells any clinician what to write, and nothing here is a basis for any decision about any real person.
:::

The appointment ended at 4.50pm. The letter to R.'s GP has to be written this evening, and the clinic has a template with headings for history, examination, impression, and plan. Everything in it is straightforward except one sentence. Does the line about passive thoughts that life is not worth the effort go into the letter?

This is a real clinical question with a real consequence for the person receiving the letter. It is also, and this is the point of the scene, a question that was mostly answered at minute 0.

## Work it with the lesson's own tools

**What did R. agree to?** Three statements, taken together. At minute 0: a letter would go to the GP, R. could read it, and R. could ask for anything in it to be discussed first. At minute 32: it would carry what the GP needs in order to help, and not everything R. said. At minute 45: R. asked about a specific item, was told yes, and was told why. R. therefore agreed to a described letter with a named reader, and R. agreed to it knowing that some things would be in it that R. had not chosen.

**Does the stated exception apply?** At minute 32 the clinician named the one thing not left to R.: immediate danger, and the promise to say so if it were being acted on. That exception governs a situation this letter does not describe. What is in the record is passive thought without plan, intent, or act. Writing to a GP as part of ordinary care is not the exception being invoked. Reaching for it here would use a rule meant for an emergency to settle a routine question about a letter, and a rule used that way stops meaning anything on the day it is needed.

**Who reads this, and can R.?** One named GP, plus whoever at that practice has access to the record. R. can read it, because R. was told so. That fact changes the writing. When patients at three German psychiatric outpatient clinics gained online access to their notes, documentation changed measurably: across 876 notes before and after, stigmatising, controlling, and incomprehensible language fell and comprehensible, personal, and resource-oriented language rose. The clinicians interviewed said the substance of what they documented was unchanged and described handling sensitive material through more general phrasing. That study covers three German outpatient clinics and compares two periods with no control site, so it does not establish that access causes the change. It does describe what changed where access arrived, and the shape of it is worth having: the readable version differed in how things were written far more than in whether they were there.

**What does each option cost?**

| Option | What the GP can do | What it does to the next conversation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leave the sentence out | Reviews R. without knowing there is anything to review against. Any later change has no baseline. | Nothing, until R. reads the letter and finds that the one thing hardest to say is the one thing missing. |
| Put it in without telling R. | Has what it needs. | R. reads a sentence R. did not know was going in. R. now has direct evidence that what is said in the room turns up in writing unannounced. |
| Put it in, having said in the room that it would be there | Has what it needs. | R. reads a letter that matches what R. was told. Nothing has to be repaired. |

The third option is the only one that costs nothing later, and it is only available if something was said earlier. That is the whole argument of this lesson in one row of a table. More than half of the people in two United States surveys who withheld something from a clinician named keeping it out of the medical record as a reason; the second option manufactures exactly that reason for R., and it does so at the moment R. has most to lose by acting on it.

**So what does the clinician do this evening?** Writes the sentence, in the terms R. used, in the section where a GP will find it, because the letter was described to R. as carrying what the GP needs. And the reason the clinician can do that without a phone call to R. first is that R. was told, in the room, what the letter was for and that R. could read it and query it. Had that not been said, the honest options this evening would be a call to R. or an incomplete letter, and both are more expensive than forty seconds at minute 0.

## What you should be able to reconstruct

Close this and answer from memory.

1. The six decisions, in any order, with the consequence each one had in R.'s appointment.
2. What changed about R.'s agreement at minute 24, and what made that change available.
3. One thing a person in a non-clinical role may do with this material and one thing they may not, justified from the act and not from the topic.
4. Why "was consent obtained?" is the wrong shape of question, and what to ask instead.

For the fourth: ask what the person has agreed to now, what they were told in order to agree to it, and what they can still change. A yes-or-no question about the past has no answer that is useful this evening.

## Where this goes next

The outcome that follows this one, open and focused inquiry, takes the arranged conversation and asks how the questions inside it are put. Mental-status observation, later in the same module, describes R.'s state without turning the description into a verdict, and it will keep running into this lesson: what a person shows you is partly a fact about the room they are showing it in. Further on, the mood outcomes ask what R.'s picture might be, and the alcohol, the weight, an episode six years ago that nobody assessed, and a blood test nobody has done all come back. Each of those facts is in the record because of a decision made before R. spoke, and none of them is more secure than the arrangement that produced it.
