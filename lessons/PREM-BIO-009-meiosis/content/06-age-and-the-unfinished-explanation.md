# A well-documented effect with an unfinished explanation

:::{callout}
:kind: boundary
:id: callout-boundary-age-scene

**Teaching example, not medical advice.** Every figure below is a population aggregate from a published study. None of them is anyone's personal risk, none is a threshold, and nothing here is a screening recommendation, a reproductive recommendation, or a statement about what any individual should do or expect. No individual is described. This scene is an exercise in reading evidence about a mechanism, and the mechanism is the subject rather than any decision.
:::

Errors of the kind scene 5 modelled are not rare curiosities. Aneuploid embryos account for at least 10 per cent of human pregnancies. About 0.3 per cent of newborn infants are trisomic or monosomic, and about 35 per cent of spontaneous abortions are. For women nearing the end of their reproductive lifespan the incidence of aneuploidy may exceed 50 per cent.

Set beside the checkpoint stringency of spermatogenesis, where errors are described as extremely rare, those numbers make meiosis in the human female look like an unusually error-prone process. It is. The interesting question is why, and this scene is an audit of how well that question has been answered.

## What is firmly established, and how firmly

One thing is established outright, and a second is established only in part. Separating those two is the work of this scene.

**Established: aneuploidy rises with maternal age.** This is about as well documented as anything in reproductive biology, from several independent kinds of study.

**Established in direction, not in magnitude: the rise is not uniform across the two divisions.** The 1996 study from scene 5 compared women aged 40 and over with women under 25, and reported the comparison as an **odds ratio**: a figure that sets the odds of the outcome in one group against the odds in the other, where 1 would mean no difference between them and a larger value means the outcome is concentrated in the older group.

For errors at the first maternal division the odds ratio was 5.2. For errors at the second maternal division it was 51.4.

That second pair of numbers is worth sitting with before drawing anything from it, because the *size* of the gap between 5.2 and 51.4 turns out to be much less secure than the fact that the two divisions behave differently.

## What that study can and cannot establish

:::{investigation}
:id: investigation-audit-the-age-evidence

**Audit the design before you accept the inference.**

That study assigned 170 trisomy 21 cases to a parental origin and a meiotic division using DNA sequence differences, then compared age bands. Ask three questions of it.

**How many cases sit behind the striking number?** Work it out. Of 170 cases, 86 per cent were maternal, which is about 146. Of those, 25 per cent were at the second division, which is about 37. That odds ratio of 51.4 rests on roughly 37 cases distributed across age bands. A large ratio computed from a small subgroup is a real finding and an imprecise one at the same time, and the size of the ratio is not evidence of the precision of the ratio.

**Is it retrospective?** Yes. It reads the DNA of children already born with trisomy 21 and infers backwards to a division that happened years earlier. It observes outcomes, not the meiosis itself.

**What does the study's own conclusion claim?** The authors state that the results support an association between advanced maternal age and errors at both divisions, that the association with the first division does not pinpoint when the error happened, and that the association with the second division implies at least one age-related mechanism acting around the time of conception.

Notice how carefully that is phrased. "Supports an association." "Does not pinpoint." "Implies at least one mechanism." The design can establish that age and stage travel together and can locate a mechanism in time. It cannot say what the mechanism is.
:::

## Three candidate mechanisms, and where each one stands

**Cohesion weakening over the decades of arrest.** This is the leading candidate and the one with the most direct evidence. The logic is attractive because scene 2 built it: cohesion is what holds sister chromatids together, and centromeric cohesion in particular has to survive from the first division to the second. In a female meiosis, scene 4 established, the cell has been holding that arrangement since before the woman was born. Protein glue asked to hold for four decades is a plausible thing to fail.

A 2019 study examined 218 oocytes from donors aged 9 to 43 and found that aneuploidy in eggs follows a U-shaped relation with age, high at both ends. Within that, it separated error patterns: precocious separation of sister chromatids increased linearly with age, while first-division non-disjunction actually *decreased* with age, and reverse segregation increased in the oldest group. The authors propose that cohesion weakening acts as a molecular clock limiting reproductive capacity, and they report a correlation between loss of centromeric cohesion and the pattern that rises with age.

**Recombination failure.** Studies in the 1990s identified failure to recombine, and crossovers in unhelpful positions, as prominent contributors to human trisomy. Scene 2 gives the mechanism directly: no crossover means no chiasma means nothing holding the pair. The scale is not trivial: over 10 per cent of all human oocytes contain at least one bivalent with no crossover.

**Weak checkpoints.** Scene 4's row about the poor checkpoint response in oogenesis belongs here too. A permissive checkpoint does not cause an error; it fails to catch one.

## Where the explanation stops

Now the part that a textbook summary usually omits.

The 2012 review that collects this literature reaches a deliberately unfinished conclusion. It notes that the age curve for trisomy in natural conceptions is J-shaped, with a slight increase at the youngest maternal ages as well as an exponential increase in the decade before menopause, and that the shape of the curve differs between individual chromosomes. Its reading of that is that ageing affects chromosome segregation by **multiple** mechanisms rather than one. On the leading candidate specifically, it concludes that loss of cohesin seems unlikely to be the only force driving the steep rise before menopause. Its overall summary is that human aneuploidy arises through a multi-step process, with errors at several distinct stages of oogenesis, made worse by the absence of efficient checkpoints.

The 2019 study is equally explicit about its own limit. Having linked the age-rising pattern to cohesion, it says the mechanism causing the elevated first-division non-disjunction seen in *young* females is currently unclear. The U-shape has two arms and the study accounts for one of them.

### Two studies that do not agree about the first division

There is a straight conflict between the two studies used in this scene, and it needs stating rather than stepping around.

The 1996 study reports first-division maternal errors becoming **more** common with age, at an odds ratio of 5.2. The 2019 study reports first-division non-disjunction **decreasing** with age. Both cannot be describing the same quantity.

They are not. Three differences are visible in what each one measured.

- **Different material.** The 1996 study read trisomy 21 in infants who were born. The 2019 study examined oocytes directly. Anything that affects whether an affected conception survives to birth sits between those two.
- **Different chromosomes.** The 1996 figures are for chromosome 21 alone. The 2019 figures are across all chromosomes, and scene 6's own review notes that age curves differ from chromosome to chromosome.
- **Different categories.** The 2019 study splits first-division errors into three patterns and reports the trend for one of them, first-division non-disjunction, separately from the other two, both of which rise with age. The 1996 marker method does not use those categories at all, so "first-division error" does not name the same set of events in the two papers.

Any of those could account for the disagreement, and this lesson cannot say which does. What follows is that the *direction* of first-division errors with maternal age is not settled by putting these two studies side by side, and the earlier statement that the two divisions behave differently rests on the 1996 study alone.

So the honest state of the question:

| Statement | Status |
| --- | --- |
| Aneuploidy in human eggs and conceptions rises with maternal age | Well documented, multiple independent designs |
| The two meiotic divisions behave differently with age | Observed in the 1996 study; rests on that one design here |
| The second-division odds ratio is about 51 | Direction supported, magnitude imprecise on roughly 37 cases |
| First-division errors become more common with age | Unresolved: the two studies used here report opposite directions |
| Cohesion weakening during prolonged arrest contributes | Leading candidate, with supporting correlation |
| Cohesion weakening is the sole or sufficient cause | Explicitly doubted by the reviewing authors |
| Failure to recombine contributes | Established as a contributor |
| One mechanism explains the whole age curve | Contradicted by the shape of the curve itself |
| The elevated error rate at young maternal ages is explained | Stated to be unclear |

A learner who can reproduce that table has understood this material better than one who can recite "cohesion degrades with age." That recitation is defensible as far as it goes. What it gets wrong is the confidence: it presents as closed a question the literature leaves open.

## A discrepancy this lesson could not resolve

One more piece of honesty, of the arithmetic kind.

Scene 3 reported that 2.60 per cent of bivalents in female material lacked a crossover marker. There are 23 bivalents per oocyte. If each were independent, the fraction of oocytes containing at least one such bivalent would be $1 - (1 - 0.026)^{23}$, which is about 45 per cent. The review quoted above puts the figure at over 10 per cent.

Those do not sit comfortably together, and this lesson does not know which is closer to the truth. Candidate explanations include the marker undercounting real crossovers, so that some bivalents scored as having none actually had one; the two figures coming from different populations, one of them fetal ovary material; and bivalents not being independent of one another. Both published figures are reported here as published, and the 45 per cent is what an independence assumption yields rather than a measured quantity.

:id: check-audit-the-evidence
