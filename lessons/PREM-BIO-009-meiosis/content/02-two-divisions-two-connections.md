# One replication, two divisions, and the reason there are two

Take a single chromosome pair and follow it. Everything here happens to all 23 pairs at once, but one pair is enough to see the logic, and 23 copies of the same logic is all the rest amounts to.

Some vocabulary first, because the argument cannot be made without it and lessons 01 to 07 did not supply it.

:::{definition}
:id: definition-machinery-vocabulary

The **spindle** is the apparatus of protein fibres a dividing cell builds to move chromosomes. It has two ends, called **poles**. Each chromosome is gripped at a single **attachment point**, a structure built on it that the spindle fibres catch hold of, and the spindle pulls each chromosome toward one pole or the other. **Anaphase** is the stage at which that pulling happens and the chromosomes actually move apart.

One division produces exactly two cells, and it is worth seeing why rather than accepting it. A division assembles one spindle; that spindle has two poles and no more; every chromosome is pulled to one pole or the other, so the contents end up gathered in two groups; and the cell then pinches into two between the groups. Two poles, two groups, two cells. A single division cannot make four.

Where a stage belongs to a particular meiotic division, a Roman numeral says which: **anaphase I** is the pulling stage of the first division and **anaphase II** the pulling stage of the second.
:::

:::{callout}
:kind: note
:id: callout-counting-convention

Three counts run through this scene and they do not move together. Watch all three.

When a chromosome is copied, the result is two identical **sister chromatids** joined at the **centromere**, a constricted region where the joining and later the pulling both happen. That joined object still counts as **one chromosome**. Each chromatid is a complete copy of that chromosome's DNA.

So a human cell that has just replicated its DNA holds 46 **chromosomes**, 92 **chromatids**, and two **DNA copies of every chromosome**. Replication changed the second and third counts and left the first alone.

Ploidy counts chromosomes. Diploid is 46, haploid is 23, and a replicated chromosome counts as one either way.
:::

## What is there to separate

Before meiosis begins, the DNA is replicated. Your chromosome 7 from your mother is now two identical sister chromatids; your chromosome 7 from your father is now two identical sister chromatids.

Then the two homologs pair along their lengths. Each duplicated chromosome pairs with its duplicated homolog to form a **bivalent**, which contains four chromatids. How the two homologs locate each other among all the DNA in a nucleus is a real question and this lesson does not answer it.

So one pair, at the start of meiosis, is four chromatids in a single paired structure: four copies of that chromosome's DNA, two from the mother and two from the father.

Two things hold that structure together, and both need naming before the argument.

**The exchange between homologs.** **Meiotic recombination** is the process in which meiosis deliberately breaks its own DNA and repairs each break using the homolog as the template. Most breaks are repaired with no exchange of arms. Where a repair does end with the two homologs having swapped the segments beyond the break, the outcome is **crossing over**, and one such reciprocal exchange is a **crossover**.

A swap sounds like something that would let two chromosomes come apart, so be explicit about why it does the opposite. The exchange is physical. Afterwards the DNA of one homolog's chromatid runs continuously into what was the other homolog's chromatid; along that chromatid they have become one molecule with a join in it. The visible connection this produces is a **chiasma**, and the chiasmata created by crossing over hold the maternal and paternal homologs together until the spindle separates them at anaphase I.

**The glue between sister chromatids.** **Cohesion** is a protein glue holding sister chromatids together, running along the chromatid arms and also through the centromere. It is one substance in two locations, and the cell releases it in two stages. Where the two need distinguishing, this scene calls them arm cohesion and centromeric cohesion.

## Why two divisions

Now the question in the scene title, and the answer is arithmetic before it is biology.

Stop at the end of the first division and look honestly at what you have. Each product holds 23 chromosomes, one from every pair. By the definition in scene 1 that is haploid: the chromosome number has been halved and one member of each pair is present. So why is meiosis not finished here?

Because the chromosome number is not the only thing that has to be halved.

Each of those 23 chromosomes still consists of two chromatids, and each chromatid is a complete copy of that chromosome's DNA. The cell holds 23 chromosomes and two DNA copies of every one of them. A gamete has to carry one copy of each chromosome, because fusion has to produce a cell with two copies and no more, one from each parent. A cell handing on two copies of its own chromosome 7 would be passing on both of the copies it made, and the child would receive two versions of that chromosome from one parent instead of one.

So there are two different halvings to perform, and they are halvings of different things.

- The first division halves the **chromosome number**, 46 to 23, by separating partners.
- The second division halves the **number of DNA copies per chromosome**, two to one, by separating copies.

Neither division can do the other's job, because they act on different pairings. That is the reason there are two.

The count for the single pair you are following makes the same point in one line. The bivalent holds four chromatids, so four copies of that chromosome's DNA. A gamete needs one. Each division halves. Four to two to one is two halvings, so two divisions, and since each division doubles the cell count the products go one cell to two to four.

:::{callout}
:kind: note
:id: callout-what-the-counting-does-not-settle

The argument above takes replication as given, and replication before meiosis is an observed fact: meiosis is a single phase of DNA replication followed by two cell divisions. Given the replication, two divisions follow from the counting.

What the counting does not explain is why meiosis replicates first at all. A cell that skipped replication would hold 46 chromosomes of one chromatid each, and a single division could in principle halve that to 23. Why the process instead copies everything and then divides twice is a question about how meiosis is coupled to the cell cycle and how it evolved, and this lesson does not answer it.
:::

## Which separation happens first, and why the order is fixed

Counting says there are two divisions. It does not say which separation each one performs, and that is the second question. The staging of cohesion release settles it.

At the start of anaphase I, the cohesion along the chromatid **arms** is degraded. The arms come unglued, the chiasma no longer holds the pair, and the two duplicated homologs move to opposite poles.

Count all three numbers. Each product now holds 23 chromosomes instead of 46, one from each pair. Each of those 23 is still two chromatids joined at the centromere, because the cohesion at the centromere was untouched. So the chromosome count per cell fell from 46 to 23, the chromatid count per cell fell from 92 to 46, and the number of DNA copies per chromosome is still two.

At the second division, the **centromeric** cohesion is degraded, held until then by cohesins specific to meiosis. Only now do the two sister chromatids of each chromosome come apart.

Count again. Each product holds 23 chromosomes, each a single chromatid, and one DNA copy per chromosome. That is a gamete.

So the first division separates partners and the second separates copies, and the ordering exists because arm cohesion goes first while centromeric cohesion is protected. Reverse the staging and the first division would pull sister chromatids apart while the homologs were still paired, which would sort no partners at all.

The vocabulary follows the arithmetic. The first division is **reductional**, because the chromosome number falls. The second is **equational**, because it does not.

## How the cell makes the first division grip differently

A fair objection: if the spindle machinery is much the same in both divisions, what stops the first division from simply pulling sister chromatids apart?

Part of the answer is the cohesion staging already described. The other part is geometry at the attachment point.

In mitosis, the attachment points of two sister chromatids face in opposite directions, back to back. Fibres from opposite poles catch them and the sisters are pulled apart. That arrangement is called bi-orientation, and it is why mitosis separates copies.

At the first meiotic division the arrangement is changed. The attachment points on one homolog's two sister chromatids are turned to face the same way, side by side, so that both are caught by fibres from the **same** pole. The two sisters are pulled as a unit, and what gets pulled apart is the pair of homologs. This side-by-side arrangement is what establishes a reductional division. At the second division the geometry reverts to back to back, which is why the second division separates sisters after all.

:::{callout}
:kind: note
:id: callout-mono-orientation-scope

A bound on that last paragraph. Which proteins hold the two attachment points side by side has been worked out mainly in yeast. The geometric contrast itself, side by side at the first meiotic division against back to back in mitosis, is the part this lesson uses.
:::

## One event, two jobs

Notice what has quietly happened to crossing over.

Crossing over is normally introduced as the mechanism that generates variety, and scene 3 counts how much. In this scene it turned up doing something else: making the chiasma that holds a pair together until the first division pulls it apart. One event, two jobs.

That has a consequence you can predict before anyone tells you. A pair of homologs that made no crossover has no chiasma, and nothing is holding it together. In mutant organisms with a reduced frequency of crossing over, some chromosome pairs do lack chiasmata; those pairs fail to segregate normally, and many of the resulting gametes end up with too many or too few chromosomes.

That is not confined to laboratory mutants. It happens in humans without any mutation at all: over 10 per cent of human oocytes contain at least one bivalent with no crossover, and scene 6 takes up what follows from that.

Hold on to it, because it is the hinge between the two halves of this lesson. The event that creates variation is the same event whose absence causes a specific failure.
