# Clinical wrap-up: in a crypt, a new combination is the other kind of problem

:kind: boundary
:id: callout-boundary-wrap-up

No dose, no regimen and no management plan appears anywhere in this lesson. This is a reasoning exercise about which division produces which kind of chromosome error, worked with the tools the lesson has already built.

A laboratory receives a colonic biopsy. Among the epithelial cells it reports a population with an abnormal chromosome number: cells carrying three copies of one chromosome instead of two. The requesting note asks a reasonable-sounding question: could this be meiotic non-disjunction?

You can answer that with scene 1 alone, and the answer is no.

## Working it

**Step 1. Which division does this tissue perform?** Meiosis is specialised for producing gametes. The crypt makes absorptive cells, goblet cells and their progenitors, and it makes them by somatic division. No cell in a colonic crypt is on its way to becoming a gamete, so no cell in a colonic crypt performs meiosis. Whatever produced those three copies, it was not a meiotic division of a crypt cell.

**Step 2. So what are the possibilities?** Two, and they are distinguished by *when*, not by where.

Either the error happened in a meiosis in a parent, or at fertilisation, in which case it is present in every cell of the body and the biopsy is showing you something constitutional that happens also to be in the colon. Or it happened in a mitotic division somewhere in that person's own lineage of crypt cells, in which case it is confined to the cells descended from the one that made the mistake.

**Step 3. What would distinguish them?** Whether the abnormal count is confined to a patch, and how far that gets you is worth stating carefully, because the clean version of this step is too clean.

The clean version says a meiotic or fertilisation-stage error is in every cell and a mitotic error is in one clone, so the distribution decides it. Two things break that. An abnormality that began in meiosis can be lost from some cells afterwards, by a postzygotic rescue or by a further mitotic error, and the result is a mosaic rather than a whole-body finding. And a mitotic error early in embryonic development lands in many tissues rather than one crypt patch, because everything descended from that cell carries it.

So distribution is real evidence and it is evidence about something slightly different: it supports constitutional against clonal, which is the useful clinical distinction. It does not by itself establish which division made the mistake. The reasoning that does hold entirely from this lesson is the narrower part: meiosis happens once, before the individual exists, and everything after that is mitosis, so a crypt cell cannot have done it.

**Step 4. Is the mitotic route actually real?** It is, and scene 5 already showed the evidence in passing. In that population study of trisomy 21, 5 per cent of cases arose neither in maternal nor in paternal meiosis but by a mitotic error after fertilisation. Mitotic divisions do miscount chromosomes. The crypt performs an enormous number of them.

So the answer to the requesting note: the mechanism in scene 5 is the wrong mechanism for this tissue, and the question to ask instead is whether the finding is constitutional or clonal.

## The reason this tissue must not do what meiosis does

Now the closing contrast, which is what this lesson has been building toward since its first paragraph.

Meiosis exists to produce new combinations. Scene 3 counted them: over eight million combinations of whole chromosomes from assortment alone, and many orders of magnitude more once crossing over is included. In the germ line, novelty is the product. The process is built to generate it, it breaks its own DNA on purpose to do so, and a meiosis that produced a gamete genetically identical to the parent cell would have failed at its job.

A crypt needs the opposite. A stem cell at the crypt base has to make daughters that carry what it carried. The tissue works because the cells are the same. Genetic novelty in a crypt is not a product; it is damage.

And the crypt's exposure to that damage scales with exactly the thing lesson 01 opened with: how much dividing it does. Across tissues, cancer risk is correlated with the number of stem-cell divisions a tissue requires to maintain itself, because more divisions carry more opportunity for random mutations to accumulate. The colon renews every three to five days for a lifetime. The bill for that comes due in lesson 10.

## The two divisions side by side

Everything needed for the comparison is now in place, so here it is in one piece. Read the last row first: it is the row the whole lesson has been about.

| | Mitosis | Meiosis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Do homologs pair? | No. They behave independently and are never matched up. | Yes. Each pairs with its partner into a bivalent of four chromatids, tied by a chiasma. |
| What separates? | Sister chromatids, in one division. | Homologs at division I, then sister chromatids at division II. |
| Chromosome number | Unchanged: 46 in, 46 in each product. | Halved, and only at division I: 46 to 23. |
| DNA copies per chromosome | Two before, one in each product. | Two after division I, one after division II. |
| Product | Two cells matching the parent, still diploid. | Four haploid products in spermatogenesis; one egg plus polar bodies in oogenesis. |

The two middle rows are the whole difference. Mitosis has one separation to make and meiosis has two, and it is the extra one, the separating of partners, that halves the chromosome number and reassorts the parents' contributions on the way.

So the same word, variation, names an achievement in one lineage and a hazard in the other, and the difference is not in the chromosomes. It is in what the tissue is for.

## What this lesson did and did not settle

Three things it established. Meiosis halves the chromosome number by separating homologs at its first division and sister chromatids at its second, in that order, because two different connections have to be undone. The variation it produces is countable, and assortment alone gives 2 to the 23. A failure at each division leaves a different signature, and that signature can be read backwards from a centromere marker.

Three things it did not. It did not establish the mechanism of the maternal-age effect, because the literature has not; scene 6 records what is documented and what remains open. It did not resolve the discrepancy between two published figures for how many oocytes carry a crossover-less bivalent. And it did not teach mitosis or the cell cycle, which belong to PREM-05.08, or chromosome structure and genome organisation, which belong to PREM-06.02.

Lesson 10 of this block takes up cell death, senescence and cancer, and returns to the crypt when the climb stops ending.
