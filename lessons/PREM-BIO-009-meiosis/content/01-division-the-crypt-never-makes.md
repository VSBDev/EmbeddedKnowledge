# A tissue that divides for a lifetime and never does this

Somewhere in your descending colon, at the base of one of the tubes you have been reading about since lesson 01, twelve to sixteen stem cells are dividing. Each divides about once every twenty-four hours. Their daughters climb, differentiate on the way up, work at the surface for a few days, and are shed into the lumen. Much of the colonic lining renews on a three-to-five-day cycle, and the absorptive cells are replaced every four or five days.

One division a day, every day, for decades. Eighty years is about twenty-nine thousand days, so a single lineage at the crypt base runs of order tens of thousands of divisions.

Not one of them is a **meiosis**, which is the division this lesson is about.

That is worth sitting with, because meiosis is usually taught as though it were the interesting kind of cell division and mitosis the routine one. In your body the ratio runs the other way. Almost every division that has ever happened inside you has been the ordinary kind. Meiosis happens in one lineage only, and the colonic crypt is not it.

So this lesson opens with a question that comes before the mechanism: what job is meiosis doing that a crypt has no use for?

## Two cells, and an arithmetic problem

Start from the thing meiosis exists to make possible.

A new human begins when two cells fuse. Those two cells are the **gametes**: a sperm cell from one parent and an egg cell from the other. The single cell that results from their fusion is the **zygote**, and every cell in the resulting body descends from it by ordinary division.

Now count chromosomes. An ordinary body cell carries 46 of them, arranged as 23 pairs. Suppose the sperm and the egg each carried 46 too. The zygote would carry 92. That person's own gametes would then carry 92, so their children would start from 184, and their grandchildren from 368. Three generations and the number has octupled.

So the gametes have to carry half. That much is obvious. What is not obvious, and what the rest of this lesson turns on, is that halving is a much harder operation than it sounds.

Halving does not mean discarding 23 chromosomes at random. A cell that kept both versions of chromosome 7 and no version of chromosome 12 would have 23 chromosomes and would be useless. What is needed is exactly one from each of the 23 pairs. Every pair contributes one member, and which member it contributes does not matter.

Read that requirement again and notice what it demands. The cell has to treat chromosome 7 from your mother and chromosome 7 from your father as *a pair*, and send one of them to each side. It has to sort partners.

:::{definition}
:id: definition-homolog-and-ploidy

A **homologous pair** is the two versions of one chromosome that a person carries, one inherited from each parent. They are very similar in DNA sequence without being identical. Each member of the pair is a **homolog**, and the two homologs of a pair are partners rather than copies of each other.

A cell holding both members of every pair is **diploid**: 46 chromosomes in a human, as 23 pairs. A cell holding one member of every pair is **haploid**: 23 chromosomes. A gamete is haploid. The cell at the base of your crypt is diploid, and stays diploid every time it divides.

Ploidy counts chromosomes, and not copies of DNA. The distinction looks like pedantry here and turns out to be the whole of scene 2's argument.
:::

That last sentence is the whole of the contrast, so here is the vocabulary for it. A **somatic cell** is any cell of the body that is not on the path to becoming a gamete. Your crypt cells are somatic. The lineage that does produce gametes is the **germ line**, and it is the only lineage in which meiosis happens.

## What an ordinary division separates, and what it does not

An ordinary division cannot sort partners, and this is where the crypt makes the point for you.

Before any cell divides, its DNA is copied, so that each chromosome becomes two identical **sister chromatids** joined together. An ordinary division then pulls those sister chromatids apart, sending one to each daughter cell. Each daughter ends up with 46 chromosomes, exactly as the parent cell had.

During that division the maternal and paternal members of a pair are never brought together, never matched up, never treated as partners. They behave independently of one another. Each simply splits into its two sister chromatids.

That is exactly right for a crypt. A stem cell at the crypt base has to produce two cells carrying what it carried. Faithful copying is the entire requirement. A division that halved the chromosome number would leave 23 after one day and fewer than a dozen after two, and a lining whose cells were shedding chromosomes at that rate would not last the week.

Meiosis is the other kind of division. It is specialised for producing gametes, and in it the homologs of a pair *are* brought together and physically joined to each other before they are separated. Pairing partners is the operation an ordinary division never performs, and it is what makes halving possible instead of merely destructive.

Scene 3 makes you count the variation meiosis produces, because "enormous" is not a number and this one is computable.
