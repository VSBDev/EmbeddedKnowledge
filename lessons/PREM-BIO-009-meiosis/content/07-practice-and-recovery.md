# Practice: trace it, count it, then say what failed

Five tasks, with the support falling away as you go. Task 1 tells you which method to use. By task 5 you are choosing the method yourself.

## Task 1: trace one pair, with the steps named

A cell entering meiosis carries chromosome 15 from each parent. Replication has already happened.

Answer each part in one line, and the scene-2 headings tell you where to look for each.

1. How many chromatids of chromosome 15 does the cell contain at this point?
2. What is the paired structure of the two homologs called?
3. At the first anaphase, which of the two connections is released, and what moves apart?
4. Immediately after the first division, how many chromosome 15s does each product hold, and how many chromatids does each of those consist of?
5. At the second anaphase, what is released and what moves apart?
6. State which division was reductional and give the reason in terms of what it separated.

## Task 2: the same counting, a different organism

A hypothetical animal has a diploid chromosome number of 16. A study counts crossover markers in its meiotic cells and reports a mean of 20 per cell.

1. How many chromosome pairs does it have?
2. How many combinations of whole chromosomes can independent assortment alone produce in one of its gametes?
3. How many crossovers does that give per chromosome pair on average?
4. Compare that per-pair figure with the human male figure from scene 3. Which of the two averages is lower?
5. A colleague concludes from part 4 that the species with the lower average must therefore have more chromosome pairs that end up with no crossover at all. Scene 3 gives you a specific reason to refuse that inference, and a specific pair of human numbers that demonstrates it. State both, and say what you would need to measure instead.
6. Quite apart from variation, why does it matter whether a pair receives a crossover?

## Task 3: read a marker result

Three findings, each in a different individual with a trisomy. For each, name the division at which the error occurred, or say the result does not distinguish them. Use the two-case model from scene 5 and assume the marker sits at the centromere.

1. At the centromere of the tripled chromosome, the individual carries two different maternal sequence variants and one paternal variant.
2. At the centromere, the individual carries two identical copies of one maternal variant and one paternal variant.
3. At a marker two-thirds of the way along the chromosome arm, the individual carries two different maternal variants.

## Task 4: audit a sentence

Here is a sentence of the kind that appears in course notes:

> "Cohesion between sister chromatids degrades over the decades an oocyte spends arrested, and this is why aneuploidy increases with maternal age."

Using scene 6 only, answer:

1. Which part of that sentence is well supported?
2. Which word in it goes beyond what the cited literature concludes?
3. Name one published observation the sentence cannot accommodate.
4. Rewrite the sentence so that it says only what the evidence supports.

## Task 5: no method named

A colleague argues that because meiosis produces such large numbers of genetically distinct gametes, a tissue that divides as often as the colonic crypt must also be generating large genetic diversity among its cells over a lifetime.

Say whether the conclusion follows, and identify the specific step where the reasoning goes wrong. You are given no framework for this one; choose the parts of the lesson that apply.

## Accessibility and alternatives

Every number and relationship in this lesson is available as prose, and no task requires seeing a picture.

The diagram in scene 2 has a long description carrying the same five stages, the same links and the same conclusion. The argument in that scene is complete without the picture: the section headed "Which separation happens first, and why the order is fixed" states the whole sequence in words, with all three counts at each step. If the diagram does not render or is not visible to you, read that section together with the long description.

Six tables appear in the lesson, in scenes 4, 5, 6 and 8, and each is restated in the sentences around it. The five rows of scene 4's comparison are discussed one group at a time in the paragraphs that follow it. The two product tables in scene 5 are derivable from the M and P notation described in prose immediately above them, and that notation uses subscripts which read aloud as "M one" and "M two". Scene 5's two-case summary, scene 6's status table and scene 8's meiosis-and-mitosis table all restate judgments that appear as full sentences nearby.

Every calculation is written out as an explicit arithmetic step with its result, including the powers of two, which are built up from 2 to the 10 instead of being assumed. A calculator is fine. Nothing is timed and no mental arithmetic is required. Nothing anywhere in the lesson depends on colour, motion or sound to carry its meaning.

## Recovery route

If the two divisions keep collapsing into one another, stop trying to hold the sequence and hold one fact instead: there is only one glue, and the cell cuts it in two places at two times. Cohesion holds sister chromatids together along their arms and at their centromeres. Division one cuts the arm cohesion, and because the swapped arms beyond a crossover were being held by exactly that cohesion, cutting it also lets the chiasma go, so the homologs come apart. Division two cuts the centromeric cohesion, and only then do the sisters come apart. One glue, two locations, two times, in that order. Everything else in scene 2 hangs off it.

If you can say what each division separates but the *arithmetic* will not stay put, run one pass counting only DNA copies of a single chromosome. Start at four for the bivalent, go to two after the first division, and one after the second. Then check the chromosome count separately: 46 to 23 at the first division, and unchanged at the second. Two counts, falling at different steps, is the thing to hold.

If task 2 went wrong, the problem is probably that you used the diploid number where the number of pairs belongs. Independent assortment counts pairs, so the exponent is the count of pairs and never the count of chromosomes. Redo part 2 with 8 in the exponent and check it against the human case: 23 pairs gives 2 to the 23, and not 2 to the 46.

If the combinatorics in scene 3 did not land, the ratio work in PREM-QNT-005 on powers and orders of magnitude is the direct prerequisite, and lesson 01 of this block used the same arithmetic on cell sizes. Then re-run only step 1 of scene 3, which needs nothing but repeated doubling.

If task 4 felt like a trick, it was not. Separating a documented association from a proposed mechanism is a skill the earlier blocks teach directly: PREM-SCI-007 on correlation, causation and confounding, and PREM-SCI-011 on mechanistic and convergent evidence, both apply to it. Scene 6's status table is the worked version of that separation.

If task 5 went wrong, check whether you carried "variation" across from one setting to the other without noticing that it means two different things. Scene 8 is where that gets resolved, so reading it and returning to task 5 is a legitimate route rather than a shortcut.

If a whole scene refused to make sense on two passes, the likeliest reason is one of the two prerequisite gaps named at the end of scene 1. Neither is your fault and neither is recoverable by rereading this lesson. Ask your course text or instructor for the chromosome material rather than pressing on.
