# Four products, and the meiosis that keeps only one

Almost every account of meiosis you will read says it produces four gametes from one cell. Two divisions, one cell becoming two becoming four, each haploid. The arithmetic is clean and the diagram is easy to draw.

It is also only half true, and the half that is false is the half that matters for the rest of this lesson.

The two processes need names. **Spermatogenesis** is the production of sperm cells, which happens in the testis. **Oogenesis** is the production of egg cells, which happens in the ovary. Both run meiosis. They run it very differently.

:::{misconception}
:id: misconception-four-gametes

**The claim.** One meiosis produces four gametes.

**Where it holds.** In spermatogenesis it is accurate. Both divisions are symmetrical, the cytoplasm is split roughly evenly each time, and four haploid products result from one starting cell.

One qualification, because it is the same kind of precision this scene is about. Those four products are spermatids. Meiosis has finished with them and they are not yet sperm cells; differentiation follows, and a mammalian sperm cell has only begun that process when meiosis ends. So even in the sex where the count is four, meiosis does not hand over four finished gametes. It hands over four cells that go on to become them.

**Where it fails.** In oogenesis both divisions are deliberately lopsided. The first division produces one large cell and a much smaller **first polar body**. The second division produces the egg and a **second polar body**. Half the genome is segregated into the polar bodies, and only a single viable gamete comes out of the whole process.

**Why the error is not harmless.** Four equal products and one product plus discards are different processes with different failure behaviour. A cell that is going to keep only one of its four chromosome sets, and that has been holding those chromosomes in a paused state for decades, is running under constraints that a symmetrical division does not face. Scene 6 is about what those constraints do, and the four-gamete picture hides all of it.

**The corrective test.** Ask how many functional gametes come from one meiosis and in which sex. If the answer is "four" without a qualifier, the answer is describing spermatogenesis and calling it meiosis.
:::

## A note on how many polar bodies

You may meet the figure of three polar bodies instead of two, on the reasoning that four products minus one egg leaves three. Both figures are in circulation, and the count is two or three depending on whether the first polar body goes on to divide again. This lesson says two, which is what the source it follows describes.

The number that carries the teaching is not two or three. It is **one**: one functional gamete per meiosis in the female, against four in the male.

## The other asymmetry, which is time

The second difference between the two processes is more extreme than the first, and it is easy to miss because it is not visible in any diagram of chromosomes.

Spermatogenesis runs throughout life. It starts and it continues, producing cells continuously.

Oogenesis does not work like that at all. Oocytes are formed in the ovary during fetal development, before birth. They enter meiosis and then stop, arrested in late **prophase** of the first division. Prophase is the long preparatory stage of any division, the part where chromosomes condense and, in meiosis, where homologs pair and cross over; it comes well before the anaphase pulling scene 2 described. Oocytes are maintained in that arrested state for decades. A cell that completes its first meiotic division in a woman of thirty-five started that division roughly thirty-five years earlier and spent the interval paused partway through it.

Then, after the first division finally completes, the cell arrests a second time, at **metaphase** of the second division. Metaphase is the stage at which the chromosomes have finished lining up on the spindle and are ready to be pulled, so the cell is poised one step short of anaphase. It waits there. Fertilisation is the event that triggers completion of the second division.

So the two divisions of one female meiosis can be separated by decades from its own beginning, and the second division does not finish unless a sperm arrives.

## Checkpoints, and a difference in stringency

One further contrast belongs here, because scene 6 needs it.

Cells have surveillance mechanisms that hold a division until the chromosomes are properly attached to the spindle. In oocytes, during the first meiotic division, that surveillance appears weak and ineffective at detecting misalignment compared with its performance in mitosis. Looking across the two sexes, the checkpoints operating during spermatogenesis are described as stringent enough that errors are extremely rare, while the checkpoint response to errors during oogenesis is described as poor.

Put the three asymmetries side by side and a pattern appears that you can reason from instead of memorising.

| | Spermatogenesis | Oogenesis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Functional gametes per meiosis | four | one |
| When it happens | throughout life | begins before birth, then arrests for decades |
| Checkpoint response to error | stringent; errors extremely rare | poor |
| Crossovers per meiosis (scene 3) | about 49 | about 69 |
| Bivalents with no crossover marker | 0.38% | 2.60% |

Read the rows carefully, because they do not all do the same work.

Three of them predict more segregation errors in the female: the decades of arrest, the poor checkpoint response, and the seven-times-higher rate of bivalents with no crossover marker. Those three point the same way, and if you were asked to predict which sex misses more chromosomes before seeing any data, those are the rows that would tell you.

One row makes no prediction at all. Four functional gametes against one is a difference in yield, and nothing about yield says anything about how often a chromosome goes to the wrong pole.

And one row points the other way. More crossovers per meiosis should mean *fewer* pairs left without a chiasma, so the female's 69 against the male's 49 predicts that the female should do better. She does worse, by a factor of about seven, and that is exactly the failed prediction scene 3 spent a step on: an average does not fix a failure rate, and the resolution was in the spread and in the proportion of crossovers that never finish.

So the table is a genuine forecast from three rows, a silence from one, and a warning from one.
