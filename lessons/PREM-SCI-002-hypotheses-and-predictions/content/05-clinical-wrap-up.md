# Clinical wrap-up: split one diabetes question into testable expectations

This is how one clinical question splits into a hypothesis and a prediction you could check.

**Teaching example—not medical advice.** The people, records, and proposed relationships in this case are invented for reasoning practice. The example does not claim that dinner timing affects glucose in real patients and must not be used to guide care.

The bounded question from the previous lesson is:

> In adults with type 2 diabetes, is a later dinner time associated with higher next-morning fasting glucose?

Keep that question separate from this candidate hypothesis:

> **Candidate hypothesis:** Among the adults in this fictional teaching record, later dinner time is associated with higher next-morning fasting glucose.

The question asks what relationship the record may show. The hypothesis proposes one relationship that could answer it; it does not claim that dinner timing causes the difference.

## Derive an observable prediction

Name the conditions before the expected record: compare later-dinner days with earlier-dinner days across the adults in the fictional record, with the other recorded conditions similar. For this simplified exercise, choose the group average before inspecting the result and treat the supplied record as the complete teaching record rather than as a sample from which to estimate a real clinical effect.

If the candidate hypothesis holds under those conditions, then:

> **Prediction:** Across those adults, the average next-morning fasting glucose reading is higher on the later-dinner days than on the earlier-dinner days.

The prediction translates the proposed relationship into the prespecified record to compare.

The prediction must stay at the level of the hypothesis. The hypothesis concerns a pattern across the fictional group, not a rule that must hold for every person on every day. One adult's reading could be lower after a later dinner while the later-dinner group average is still higher. A prediction about one adult on one pair of days would therefore test a different, stronger claim.

## Name a result that would count against it

Suppose the teaching record instead shows the average next-morning reading on later-dinner days is the same as, or lower than, the average on earlier-dinner days, under the named conditions. That result would count against the candidate hypothesis as written.

One adult reading lower after a later dinner would not, on its own, count against the group-level hypothesis. Individual records can vary while the prespecified group average follows the predicted direction.

A competing hypothesis could propose that the later- and earlier-dinner days have the same average next-morning reading in this fictional record. Under the same named conditions, that hypothesis predicts equal group averages. The two hypotheses therefore disagree about whether the later-dinner average is higher.

Stop at that reasoning comparison. This scene does not choose variables, controls, measurements, sampling, or a study design, and it does not establish a clinical conclusion.

The next lesson, **Variables and controls**, turns the prediction's terms into independent, dependent, and controlled variables.

This scene is illustrative and makes no factual medical claim about diabetes.
