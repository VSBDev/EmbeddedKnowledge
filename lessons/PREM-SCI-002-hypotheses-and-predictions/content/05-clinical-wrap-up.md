# Clinical wrap-up: split one diabetes question into testable expectations

This is how one clinical question splits into a hypothesis and a prediction you could check.

**Teaching example—not medical advice.** The people, records, and proposed relationships in this case are invented for reasoning practice. The example does not claim that dinner timing affects glucose in real patients and must not be used to guide care.

The bounded question from the previous lesson is:

> In adults with type 2 diabetes, is a later dinner time associated with higher next-morning fasting glucose?

Keep that question separate from this candidate hypothesis:

> **Candidate hypothesis:** In the adults described by this teaching example, eating dinner later tends to raise next-morning fasting glucose.

The question asks what relationship the record may show. The hypothesis proposes one relationship that could answer it.

## Derive an observable prediction

Name the conditions before the expected record: compare the later-dinner days with the earlier-dinner days across the adults in the teaching record, holding the other recorded conditions similar.

If the candidate hypothesis holds under those conditions, then:

> **Prediction:** Across those adults, the average next-morning fasting glucose reading is higher on the later-dinner days than on the earlier-dinner days.

The prediction names what the record would show. It does not repeat the proposed relationship in different words.

It also has to stay at the level the hypothesis was pitched at, which is the step most easily fumbled. The hypothesis says dinner timing *tends* to raise the reading in a group of adults: a statement about a group, and about a tendency within it. It does not say that any one adult's reading must be higher on any one later-dinner day. A prediction about a single adult on a single pair of days would be a different claim -- stronger, and about a smaller thing -- so testing it would not test what was proposed, and it could fail while the proposal held.

## Name a result that would count against it

Suppose the teaching record instead shows the average next-morning reading on later-dinner days is the same as, or lower than, the average on earlier-dinner days, under the named conditions. That result would count against the candidate hypothesis as written.

One adult reading lower after a later dinner would not, on its own. A tendency in a group allows individuals to run against it, so a single case that goes the other way is consistent with the proposal rather than counting against it -- which is why the record to compare is the group's, and why naming that before you look is the whole discipline here.

A competing hypothesis could propose that dinner time has no consistent relationship with the next-morning reading in this teaching case. Under the same named conditions, that hypothesis predicts the two averages will not separate. The two hypotheses therefore differ on whether the averages separate at all.

Stop at that reasoning comparison. This scene does not choose variables, controls, measurements, sampling, or a study design, and it does not establish a clinical conclusion.

The next lesson, **Variables and controls**, turns the prediction's terms into independent, dependent, and controlled variables.

This scene is illustrative and makes no factual medical claim about diabetes.
