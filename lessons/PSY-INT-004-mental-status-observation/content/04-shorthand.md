# What "poor historian" costs

Every clinical document is compressed. "4/12 low mood" is compression, and it is fine: four months of low mood is what it says, and a reader can unpack it exactly. "Poor historian" is a different operation. It cannot be unpacked, because the observation it replaced is no longer in the building.

The distinction is worth stating once, plainly. Compression keeps the information and shortens the writing. Shorthand of the second kind shortens the writing by throwing information away, and usually replaces it with a judgement about the person. This scene works through the first note phrase by phrase and puts back what the room supported.

:::{misconception}
:id: misconception-shorthand-is-decompressible

**The wrong model, stated fairly.** Clinical shorthand is a professional compression code. An experienced reader decompresses "flat affect, poor historian, poorly motivated" back into roughly the right picture, so writing it out longhand is a courtesy to junior readers and costs time that busy services do not have.

**Commit first.** Cover the observation log from the last scene. Read this line: *flat affect, poor eye contact, poorly motivated.* Write down what you think the room looked like.

**What the model cannot explain.** Compare your reconstruction with the log. The two expression changes are almost certainly missing, because "flat" excludes them. The direct-question pattern in the eye contact is missing, because "poor" is a grade and grades have no shape. The bus at 15:10 is missing, and without it "poorly motivated" has nothing behind it at all. The code did not decompress, because there was nothing left inside it to decompress. Add to that the agreement figures from earlier in the lesson: experienced psychiatrists watching the same interview agreed on the range of affect under half the time. A code that senior readers encode differently cannot be decoded reliably by anyone.

**The better model.** A phrase in a mental-status description earns its place when a reader who was not present can say what happened from it. Descriptive words are welcome next to the events they summarise, where they act as a heading. Alone, they are the only copy, and they are a lossy one.

**Recheck in a new case.** A colleague writes: *agitated, uncooperative with examination.* Say what you now know about the appointment. You know that somebody formed two impressions and you know nothing about the room. Then write the two or three sentences that would have made those impressions checkable, and notice that they are not much longer.
:::

## The first note, line by line

Each row takes one phrase from the first note, sets the room beside it, and names what the phrase costs the reader who inherits it.

| Wrote | What was in the room | What the phrase costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| poor historian | Gave the same four-month account twice with the same dates; added nothing when invited to expand | A consistent, thin account is evidence about the account. "Poor historian" converts it into a defect in R. and deletes the consistency, which is the useful half |
| flat affect | Face still for most of forty minutes; two changes, both tracking the conversation | Contradicts the observation on the standard vocabulary, and hides that expression was reactive when it came |
| poor eye contact | Floor for most of the interview; met the examiner's eye on direct questions | The pattern. A grade with no shape cannot be compared with anything at the next appointment |
| psychomotor retardation | Rose slowly; five to ten second pauses before four-month questions; ordinary pace on other topics; hoarse voice | That the slowing was topic-bound, and that it sat in the same room as a hoarse voice and a request to close the window |
| denies SI | Asked directly; described thoughts that life is not worth the effort, no plan, no intent, nothing acted on; said evenly; answered the follow-up questions | What R. actually said, how R. said it, the period asked about, and the fact that R. answered the follow-ups. The phrase records a negation and discards everything that made it checkable |
| refused water | Water was offered and declined | Water was offered and declined. *Refused* is not wrong, but *declined* is the plainer word and does not invite a reader to picture resistance |
| poorly motivated | Checked the clock twice near the end; said the bus was at 15:10 | A bus |
| difficult to get a history from | Answered every question asked; elaborated little | Whether the difficulty was in R., in the questions, or in the forty minutes available |
| Alcohol + | Most evenings, more over the last two months, by R.'s account to help with sleep | Amount, direction of change, and R.'s own reason, all three of which a later reader will need |

Three of these rows are worth a second look.

*Psychomotor retardation* is not rude and it is not lazy. It is a real technical term, used correctly by many people, and it still fails the source test. It names a slowing of movement and thought as a feature of the mental state, which is a conclusion, and it absorbs the hoarseness and the cold into a psychiatric account before anyone has looked anywhere else. The last scene of this lesson returns to exactly that.

*Denies SI* is the phrase most likely to survive review, because everybody writes it. In clinical use it is a conventional way of recording that something was asked about and reported absent, and the reference chapter this lesson cites writes it exactly that way. The problem is not that the word is false. The problem is that it is a container with almost nothing in it. It does not say what was asked, in what words, over what period, or what R. said back.

R. was asked directly and described thoughts that life is not worth the effort, with no plan, no intent, and nothing acted on, answered evenly, and answered the follow-up questions too. *Denies SI* records none of that. A clinician reading the two versions a week apart is not reading the same clinical picture, and only one of them can be checked.

There is a second cost, smaller and worth knowing. Outside the ward the word still carries its ordinary sense of disputing a charge, and R. is among the people who can now read this record. A phrase whose technical sense has worn smooth for you has not worn smooth for them.

*Refused water* has the same shape. Water was offered and declined, and *refused* is not a wrong description of that. But *declined* is the plainer word for what happened, and *refused* invites the reader to picture a resistance that nobody in the room saw.

## This is measurable, and it has been measured

The case for writing the second note is usually made as a matter of respect. It is also a matter of what happens next.

Four hundred and thirteen medical students and residents were randomly given one of two chart notes about the same hypothetical patient, a young man with sickle cell disease in a pain crisis. The clinical information in the two notes was identical. One was written neutrally; the other cast doubt on the patient's pain, carried unnecessary markers of lower socioeconomic status, and implied that he had been uncooperative. Readers of the stigmatising note scored the patient lower on a validated attitude scale, and the residents among them also chose less intensive pain management. The point estimates are not quoted here: the article carries a published correction whose text is not reachable through this project's permitted routes, and the figures on the article of record cannot be confirmed as the corrected ones. The direction and the fact of both differences are what the study supports, and they are what this scene needs. Nothing about the patient differed. Only the previous clinician's sentences did.

A second study read 600 outpatient notes written by 138 physicians and sorted the ways negative feeling actually shows up in a record. Five recurred: questioning the patient's credibility with words such as *claims* and *insists* or with quotation marks placed around their account, disapproving of their reasoning or self-care, quoting their speech in a way that marks them out, portraying them as difficult, and emphasising the writer's own authority over them. Look back at the first note. In twenty-eight words it questions credibility with *denies*, disapproves with *poor historian* and *poorly motivated*, and marks R. as difficult twice over, which is three of the five. The writer would almost certainly say they were being efficient.

A third study counted stigmatising descriptors across 48,651 hospital admission notes at one centre in a single year. Two and a half per cent carried at least one term from the study's restricted descriptor lists. After adjustment, the share was 0.67 percentage points higher in notes about Black patients than in notes about White patients, with a confidence interval running from 0.15 to 1.18. The habit is uncommon, and it is not evenly distributed.

Put the three together. The wording of a note changes what the next clinician thinks and does, negative wording has recognisable recurring forms, and it lands more often on some patients than others. Avoiding dehumanising shorthand is a documentation skill with measurable consequences, and it belongs in the same part of your training as taking a blood pressure properly.
