# Eleven minutes, and a letter R. will read

Two settings that are not the forty minutes. Both are ordinary parts of a service, and both bend the method in a way worth working out before you meet them.

## Eleven minutes in a waiting room

A support worker sat with R. from 14:04 to 14:15 and has been asked to add something to the record. What they have: R. arrived early and stood by the window instead of sitting; declined a chair twice, politely, then sat when the worker sat; watched the corridor door; said nothing except in reply; answered a question about the journey in a full sentence at ordinary pace. The worker asked R. nothing about how R. was.

:::{check}
:id: check-transfer-eleven-minutes
:kind: transfer

Work all four before reading on.

1. Write the two or three sentences this worker can honestly contribute.
2. Say which of the description's lines they belong on, and which line the worker cannot write at all.
3. State what changes about the clock test when the period is eleven minutes instead of forty.
4. The clinician's note says the pauses arrived with one topic. Can the worker's eleven minutes confirm or contradict that? Say why.
:::

**One and two.** Something close to "Seen in the waiting area 14:04 to 14:15. Stood by the window on arrival, declined a chair twice and sat when I did. Watched the corridor door. Spoke in reply to questions, at ordinary pace and in full sentences." Appearance and behaviour takes all of it, and speech takes the last clause.

The line the worker cannot write is mood. Mood is what the person tells you about their own state, and nobody asked. An observer with eleven minutes and no question has observations of expression and no report to compare them with, so a mood line from this encounter would be the worker's impression wearing R.'s voice. Writing "seemed low" here is exactly the migration the second scene was about, running from affect to mood.

**Three.** Nothing changes about the test and everything changes about what passes it. The eleven minutes are as real as the forty, and eleven minutes cannot support a statement about how much of anything appeared. *Narrow range of expression* needs enough time for a range to have had a chance to show. The worker can write what happened; the summarising words wait for someone who was there longer.

**Four.** The eleven minutes can neither confirm nor contradict it. R. was not asked about the four months, so the topic that produced the pauses never came up. This is the part people get wrong: a second observation only speaks to the first if it covered the same ground. Two descriptions of the same person taken at different times and on different subjects sit side by side without ever meeting.

## The letter R. will read

Many services send the person a copy of the clinic letter, and R.'s service is one of them. R. will read the description you wrote in the third scene.

:::{check}
:id: check-transfer-letter
:kind: transfer

1. Go back through the three lines from the worked example and mark any sentence whose meaning changes when R. is the reader.
2. Decide, for each one you marked, whether it should change, and say what a change would cost the clinician who reads the letter next.
3. Write the one sentence you would add to the letter that would not have been in the notes.
:::

The observational sentences do not change. "Looked at the floor for most of the appointment and met my eye when asked a direct question" says the same thing to R. as to a colleague, which is a fair test of whether it was ever a description.

The judgements change, and they change in a useful direction. "Congruent with what was being discussed" reads to a colleague as a technical statement and to R. as a stranger's verdict on their face. The repair is the one the worked example already applied: keep the judgement and keep the two events attached to it, so R. can read what was seen and disagree with the reading. A judgement with its evidence in view is answerable by the person it describes. A judgement alone is not.

The move to avoid is softening. "R. seemed a little subdued" reads gently and tells the next clinician nothing, and the next clinician is the person who has to notice if R. is different in October. A letter that has been made comfortable by removing its events has traded a real cost to R. later against an imagined one now.

The sentence worth adding is the one that says what the description is for: that it sets out what was seen on one afternoon, so that a later appointment has something to be compared against. R. is entitled to know that the document is a record of forty minutes and not a verdict. The randomised study in the fourth scene, where the wording of a note moved 413 trainees' attitudes towards a patient and the pain management the residents among them chose, is the reason it matters that the next reader knows it too.

## Mastery check

The assessment holds four untimed, retakeable items worth twenty-four points. Attempt each before opening its reasoning. Mastery means all twenty-four: six for rewriting a shorthand description as a time-bound one, six for separating mood from affect in a supplied encounter, six for identifying what specific phrases discard, and six for an integrated rewrite of a description you have not seen. A missed criterion routes you to a different task, not to the same one again.

## Accessibility and alternatives

Every task in this lesson can be answered in prose, in bullets, or in a table, whichever suits you, and you may type, dictate, or use assistive technology. No image, audio, video, upload, or clinical software is required, and nothing depends on colour, position on a page, dragging, sound, motion, or timing. The tables are read in ordinary reading order and every relationship they carry is also stated in the prose beside them. No task asks about your own health, mood, medication, or experiences, and none requires contact with a patient, a service, or a real record.

## Recovery route

If the clock test keeps passing sentences that still feel wrong, the source test is the one doing the missing work. Take the sentence and ask what a stranger could reconstruct from it. *Appeared distressed* survives the clock and reconstructs nothing.

If mood and affect keep swapping places, go back to the sorting task at the start of the second scene and ask one question of each sentence: did this reach me through R.'s mouth or through my eyes.

If you can see that a phrase is shorthand and cannot produce the replacement, the observation is probably missing rather than hidden. That is worth knowing, and it is a reason to write during the appointment or immediately after it.

If Setting, role, and consent (PSY-130.01) is not yet familiar, some of the letter task will float, because who reads the record and what the person was told about it is settled there.
