# Clinical wrap-up: the observations that belong to the body

:::{callout}
:kind: boundary
:id: callout-wrap-up-boundary

Teaching example, not medical advice. R. is invented, and so is every observation in this lesson. Nothing here diagnoses R., proposes a test for R., or tells any reader how to interpret their own symptoms. Weighing what R.'s picture might be is the work of a later outcome in this course and is deliberately not attempted here.
:::

Three observations from the room have been sitting in the corner of this lesson, and a handful of complaints from the history sit next to them.

From the room: the work fleece stayed on and the window was closed twice; the voice was hoarse throughout, and R. said it had been for a while; R. rose from the chair slowly. From the history, taken before the appointment: R. described feeling cold, being constipated, and being tired past what the broken sleep would explain, along with about six kilograms of weight loss over four months that R. was not trying to lose. No thyroid result is available.

The clinical question is short. **Does the mental-status description leave a body question askable, or has it already answered it?**

## Run the lesson's own tools over the three observations

**Apply the clock test.** "Kept a work fleece on throughout and asked twice for the window to be closed" passes: it is a statement about the forty minutes and a reader can picture it. "Cold intolerance" is already a small conclusion, and it belongs in the note with its basis attached, because the fleece and the window are the whole of what was seen.

**Apply the source test to the first note.** *Psychomotor retardation, flat affect, poorly motivated* reconstructs nothing. Read that line as the only record of the appointment and the fleece is gone, the window is gone, the hoarse voice is gone, and the slow rise from the chair has been renamed as a feature of R.'s mental state. The line has not lost the observations by accident. It has absorbed them.

**Ask which line each observation belongs on.** The fleece and the window are appearance and behaviour. The hoarseness is speech. The slow rise is behaviour. None of the three is a mental-state finding on its own, and writing them on their own lines keeps them available to whoever reads the record with a different question in mind.

**Now name what the absorbing costs.** The United States National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases lists fatigue, trouble tolerating cold, and depression among the common symptoms of an underactive thyroid. That is a general fact about a common condition and it says nothing about R. What it does say is that a fleece kept on indoors, a request to close a window, and unexplained tiredness are the kind of observation a clinician somewhere downstream will want, and that a thyroid result would speak to. A note that has renamed them *psychomotor retardation* has made them invisible to that clinician, and nobody will know to look, because the record no longer says there is anything to look at.

## What the description decides and what it does not

The description does not decide anything about R.'s thyroid. It does not rank explanations, it does not order a test, and it does not say whether the low mood, the sleep, the alcohol, or the body is the more likely account. Those comparisons belong to the mood differential and longitudinal course outcome later in this course, and they need the history, a physical examination, and results that do not exist yet.

What the description decides is whether that later work is possible. Four months from now, someone will read this note. If it holds the fleece, the window, the hoarseness, the slow rise, the topic-bound pauses, the two expression changes, and R.'s own sentence about watching it through glass, then every question anyone might want to ask is still on the table. If it holds *flat affect, psychomotor retardation, poor historian*, then the appointment has been converted into a verdict about a person, and the verdict is all anyone inherits.

That is the argument of the whole lesson in one line. A mental-status examination describes a period of observation so that other people can go on thinking. Shorthand that grades the person instead ends the thinking early, and it ends it in the record, where nobody can see that it happened.

## Where this goes next

Mood phenomenology and function, later in the course, takes a description like the one built here and assembles the mood picture from it. Mood differential and longitudinal course, the outcome after it, sets this appointment beside a second one and asks what time reveals. Both are only as good as what this document kept.

Problem representation, further on, is the general form of the same skill: getting a case into terms that make the right question available before anyone answers it.
