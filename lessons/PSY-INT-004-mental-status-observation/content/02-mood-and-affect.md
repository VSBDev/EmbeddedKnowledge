# Two words the room uses interchangeably

R. said "flat". The first note says "flat affect". The same word is in both, and it changed jobs on the way across.

In ordinary speech, mood and affect are near-synonyms and nobody is harmed by the overlap. In a psychiatric description they name two different things with two different sources, and a note that lets one slide into the other reports something nobody saw.

:::{check}
:id: check-sort-mood-affect
:kind: retrieval

Four items from the appointment. Sort each one into *what R. reported* or *what the observers saw*. Write your answers down before reading on.

1. "Flat. Like I'm watching it through glass."
2. A brief smile while describing the forklift joke.
3. Four months of low mood, by R.'s account.
4. Eyes filling while talking about friends.
:::

Items 1 and 3 came from R. Items 2 and 4 came from the room. That sorting is the distinction, and the two words mark it.

:::{definition}
:id: definition-mood-and-affect
:label: Mood and affect

**Mood** is the person's own account of their sustained emotional state. It comes from what they tell you, it covers whatever period they are describing, and it is written in their words wherever they have given you words.

**Affect** is the emotional expression the examiner observed during the encounter: the face, the voice, the posture, and how those moved as the conversation moved. It covers the period you were in the room.

One is reported. The other is watched. Neither is evidence for the other.
:::

## Describing an affect instead of grading one

Affect has parts, and naming them keeps a description from collapsing into a single adjective.

- **Range** is how much of the available expression appeared. The conventional words run from flat, through blunted and restricted, up to full and broad.
- **Intensity** is how strong a change was when it came.
- **Reactivity** is whether the expression moved with what was being discussed.
- **Congruence** is whether what appeared fits the mood the person has reported and the subject they are on.

Now R., in those terms.

R.'s mood, as R. gave it: "Flat. Like I'm watching it through glass." That goes into the note inside quotation marks. A paraphrase would replace R.'s account of R.'s state with the observer's, which is the one substitution this line exists to prevent.

R.'s affect across the forty minutes: the face was still for most of the appointment; it changed twice, once towards a smile and once towards tears; both changes tracked what was being talked about; neither was strong or long. Written out: narrow range, low intensity, reactive to content, congruent with the mood R. described.

## Where the first note went wrong

"Flat affect" fails twice over.

Flat, used technically, names expression that barely moves at all. This face moved twice in forty minutes, and both times it moved in the direction the conversation was going. On the standard vocabulary the word is simply the wrong one, and *narrow range with preserved reactivity* is the description that fits.

The second failure is the interesting one. The word arrived in the affect line because R. used it about mood. One person's word for how they feel inside became the examiner's word for what their face did, and the note now carries a single observation twice while appearing to carry two.

That migration runs in both directions and is worth watching for. An observer who has watched a still face for half an hour and writes "mood: flat" has done the same thing backwards, replacing what R. said with what the observer saw.

## Why a description outlives a label

Five senior psychiatrists once rated affect from the same recorded interviews, each seven to ten minutes long, with 25 inpatients. Their mean agreement was 54% on the quality of the affect, 43% on its range, and 25% on a finer subtype. That was one small pilot at one hospital with one patient group and very short interviews, so the figures themselves belong to that study and travel nowhere.

The direction travels. The further a term sits from what was in the room, the less of it survives the trip to the next reader. *Blunted* has to be reconstructed by whoever reads it, and reconstruction is where the agreement went. "Two expression changes in forty minutes, both fitting the content" arrives intact, and anyone can apply their own word to it afterwards.

This is the argument for describing rather than grading, and it is an argument about information, not about manners. The manners argument comes later and turns out to be about information too.

:::{source-note}
:claims: claim-mood-is-reported-affect-is-observed, claim-affect-descriptors, claim-affect-ratings-vary-between-clinicians
:sources: source-statpearls-mse, source-barzilay-affect-rating

A clinical reference chapter on the mental-status examination supports the split between mood as the person's own reported state and affect as the examiner's reading of observed expression, and it supplies the conventional descriptors including range and congruence. The agreement figures come from a single pilot study of five raters, 25 male inpatients with schizophrenia, and interviews of seven to ten minutes; the authors themselves name the small sample and the narrow patient group as limits. Take the figures as evidence that these judgements vary, and not as an estimate of how much they vary anywhere else. R.'s words and expressions are invented for this course.
:::
