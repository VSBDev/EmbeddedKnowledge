# Two notes, one afternoon

Two people sat in on R.'s first appointment. It ran from 14:15 to 14:55 on a Thursday. R. is 34, works rotating night shifts in a warehouse, and had come to the clinic from a shift that finished at seven that morning. Afterwards both observers wrote up what they had seen.

Here is the first.

> 34yo, night shifts, poor historian. 4/12 low mood. Flat affect, poor eye contact, psychomotor retardation. Denies SI. Poorly motivated, refused water. Difficult to get a history from. Alcohol +.

Here is the second.

> Seen 14:15 to 14:55, first appointment, having finished a night shift at 07:00 the same morning. Kept a work fleece on throughout and asked twice for the window to be closed. Sat leaning forward, forearms on knees. Looked at the floor for most of the interview, met my eye when asked a direct question, then looked away. Speech at ordinary volume and pace while describing the warehouse; pauses of five to ten seconds before answering about the last four months. Voice hoarse throughout, and R. said it had been like that "for a while". Face still for most of the forty minutes, with a brief smile while describing a colleague's joke about a forklift and eyes filling while talking about not having seen friends since spring. Asked how R. felt in themselves: "Flat. Like I'm watching it through glass." Gave the same four-month account twice with the same dates and added nothing to it when invited. Asked directly about not wanting to be alive: said there had been thoughts that life is not worth the effort, no plan, no intent, nothing acted on; said this evenly, without changing posture, and answered the follow-up questions. Offered water and declined. Rose from the chair slowly. Checked the clock twice near the end and said the bus was at 15:10.

Both writers were in the room for the same forty minutes.

:::{check}
:id: check-what-was-lost
:kind: retrieval

Before reading on, write two lists.

1. Everything the first note tells you that you could not work out from the second.
2. Everything the second note tells you that the first has discarded.

Keep both. The rest of this lesson is an argument about the second list.
:::

The first list has one honest entry. "Alcohol +" appears in the first note and nowhere in the second, and it is a real piece of information, badly stored. Everything else in the first note is a conclusion about R. that the observations in the second note would have had to earn.

The second list runs to a page. That asymmetry is the whole subject here.

## The examination has a clock on it

A mental-status examination describes how a person looked, spoke, moved, and responded during the evaluation itself. It covers a stretch of time with a beginning and an end. The history covers R.'s four months; the examination covers the forty minutes.

That reads like a filing convention until you look at what the second note put in its opening sentence. R. had been awake since the previous evening. *Slow to answer, still face, low energy* describes a person eight hours off a night shift about as well as it describes anything psychiatric, and no amount of care in the wording will separate those two readings on its own. A reader who has the shift time can hold both open. A reader who does not will hand the whole picture to R.

So every sentence in this document has to survive the addition of *during those forty minutes*.

- "R.'s face was still for most of those forty minutes" survives, and says something a reader can use.
- "R. is flat" does not survive, because it was never a statement about the forty minutes in the first place.

Call it the clock test. It costs a second per sentence, and it catches most of what goes wrong here. A sentence that fails it has stopped describing a period and started describing a person.

## What you are bringing with you

Description, inference, and interpretation (PSY-101.02) separated three things a listener does with an encounter: report what was said and seen, draw a conclusion from it, and give that conclusion a meaning. This lesson does not rebuild the separation. It applies the separation to one written document, where losing it costs a later reader the ability to tell which of the three they are holding.

Setting, role, and consent (PSY-130.01) settled the room, the purpose of the appointment, what R. agreed to, and what R. was told about the record. Take all of that as done.

## Where this goes

By the end of the lesson you should be able to:

- write a mental-status description that names the period observed and holds every sentence inside it;
- keep mood and affect apart, attributing one to what R. reported and the other to what was seen, and describe affect by its range, its intensity, and its fit with what R. said;
- find the shorthand in a written description that has discarded information, and put back what the observation supported.
