# Writing three lines from a page of observation

The second note in the first scene is a running account of the appointment. A mental-status description is the organised version of that account, written in lines that a later reader can find. This scene builds three of those lines out loud, so that the decisions are visible while they are being made.

Nothing new is added. Everything below comes from the same forty minutes.

:::{worked-example}
:id: worked-example-three-lines

**Task.** From the observations of R.'s appointment, write the appearance-and-behaviour line, the speech line, and the mood-and-affect line. Show every decision that could have gone the other way.

**What you have.** The observation log, in the order things happened.

| Time | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| 14:15 | Collected from the waiting area, having arrived about ten minutes early |
| Throughout | Work fleece kept on; asked twice for the window to be closed |
| Throughout | Sat leaning forward, forearms on knees; floor for most of the interview; met the examiner's eye on direct questions, then looked away |
| Throughout | Voice hoarse; R. said it had been like that "for a while" |
| 14:20 onward | Ordinary volume and pace about the warehouse; five to ten second pauses before answering about the last four months |
| 14:26 | Brief smile while describing a colleague's forklift joke |
| 14:31 | Asked how R. felt in themselves: "Flat. Like I'm watching it through glass." |
| 14:38 | Eyes filled while talking about not having seen friends since spring |
| 14:44 | The four-month account repeated, same dates, nothing added when invited |
| 14:50 | Water offered and declined |
| 14:53 | Rose from the chair slowly; checked the clock twice; said the bus was at 15:10 |

**What you do not have.** Why R. moved slowly. Whether R. looks at the floor in every conversation or only in this one. What R.'s face does on a day off. Whether the hoarseness is old. None of that was in the room, and none of it can appear in a document about the room.

**Principle selected.** Two tests, applied to each sentence in turn. The clock test asks whether the sentence still holds when you add *during those forty minutes*. The source test asks whether a reader who was not present could say what happened from your sentence alone. A conclusion may go in when it earns its place, on the condition that it is written as a conclusion and carries what it rests on.

**Plan.** Draft each line from the log only. Run both tests on every sentence. Repair, then read the three lines as a stranger would.

**Execution.**

*Appearance and behaviour.* "Attended alone, arriving about ten minutes early, in work clothing kept on throughout the appointment, and asked twice for the window to be closed. Sat leaning forward with forearms on knees. Looked at the floor for most of the appointment and met my eye when asked a direct question. Rose slowly from the chair at the end."

Three decisions are buried in that. *Work clothing kept on* passes the source test; *dressed inappropriately for the weather* would not, because it grades R. against a standard the reader cannot see. *Looked at the floor for most of the appointment and met my eye on direct questions* is longer than "poor eye contact" and carries the pattern, which is the part a second reader can compare against next time. *Rose slowly from the chair* stays a movement; whether the slowness belongs to a mood, to a night shift, or to something in R.'s body is a question this line has no business settling.

*Speech.* "Ordinary volume and rate while describing work. Pauses of five to ten seconds before answering questions about the last four months. Voice hoarse throughout; R. reports it has been hoarse for some time."

The interesting decision is the contrast. Writing only "slow, long pauses" would have been true of part of the appointment and would have hidden the fact that the slowing arrived with a topic. The hoarseness sits in the speech line because that is where a later reader will look for it, and it is attributed to R.'s report where R. supplied it.

*Mood and affect.* "Mood, in R.'s words: 'Flat. Like I'm watching it through glass.' Affect: narrow range, low intensity; two changes in forty minutes, a brief smile and eyes filling, both congruent with what was being discussed and with the mood described."

R.'s sentence goes in whole. Two changes in forty minutes is a count, so a reader three weeks from now can compare a count with a count. *Congruent with what was being discussed* is a judgement, and it is written as one, with the two events attached so the reader can disagree.

**Assumptions and limits.** All three lines assume the observer's own attention was steady, which nobody checks. The affect line assumes the standard vocabulary means the same to the reader, which the last scene showed is only partly so, and that is why the count and the two events travel with it. Nothing in the three lines establishes that any of this is stable, characteristic of R., or present outside the appointment.

**Independent check.** Give the three lines to someone who was not there and ask them to say what happened in the room. If they can describe the fleece, the pauses, the two expression changes, and the bus, the lines are doing their work. If they come back with an impression and no events, something was compressed away, and the compressed part is exactly what the next appointment would have been compared against.

**Interpretation.** Together the three lines support a small set of statements: that R. was cold and stayed dressed for it, that slowing appeared with one subject and not others, that expression was narrow and still moved, and that R. gave a consistent, thin account. They support nothing about why. Why belongs to the history, to a physical examination, to tests, and to lessons after this one.

**Self-explanation.** One decision in the three lines does more work than the rest. Find it, and say what a reader loses if it goes the other way.
:::

The decision worth naming is the one in the speech line: recording that the pauses arrived with a topic. Everything else in the three lines describes a state. That one sentence describes a pattern, and a pattern is the only thing here that a later observation can confirm or contradict. Drop it, and R. becomes a person who is slow. Keep it, and R. becomes a person who was slow about one thing on one afternoon, which is a claim somebody can go and check.

The line structure, the treatment of mood as reported and affect as observed, and the descriptive vocabulary of range and congruence come from a clinical reference chapter on the mental-status examination. The pilot study of affect ratings is the reason the affect line here carries a count and two named events alongside the descriptive words. Services and record systems organise these lines differently and use different headings; what does not vary is that the document reports the evaluation period. R.'s appointment and the whole observation log were written for this lesson.
