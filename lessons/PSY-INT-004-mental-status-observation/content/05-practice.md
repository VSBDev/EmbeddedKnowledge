# Three passes at the same forty minutes

Three tasks, with less help each time. Attempt each one in writing before reading the response underneath it. Writing the sentence out is the point; recognising a good sentence when you see one is a weaker skill and it is not the one being built here.

## First pass, with the pattern in front of you

:::{check}
:id: check-practice-supported
:kind: practice

The first note says: **"Poorly motivated, refused water."**

The log holds three relevant entries: water was offered and declined at 14:50; R. checked the clock twice at 14:53; R. said the bus was at 15:10.

Do three things.

1. Name every claim the note makes that the log does not support.
2. Write the replacement sentence for the description.
3. Say which line of the description your sentence belongs on.

Use the two tests from the worked example: add *during those forty minutes*, then ask whether a reader who was not present could say what happened.
:::

The note makes two unsupported claims and one distortion. *Poorly motivated* attributes a disposition to R. from an event that has an ordinary explanation sitting next to it in the log. *Refused* reports resistance to an offer that was simply declined. The distortion is the pairing itself: putting the two phrases in one sentence invites the reader to treat the declined water as evidence for the motivation.

A replacement: "Declined water when offered. Checked the clock twice in the last few minutes and said their bus was at 15:10." It belongs on the appearance-and-behaviour line, and it is one word longer than what it replaces.

If you wrote something like "appeared eager to leave", run the clock test again. It survives the test, which is why it is tempting, and it still fails the source test: a reader cannot get from *eager to leave* back to a bus.

## Second pass, with the sentences supplied

:::{check}
:id: check-practice-faded
:kind: practice

A third person also sat in and wrote this paragraph.

> R. presented as a guarded 34-year-old who was clearly reluctant to be there. Kept their work fleece on for the entire session. Was adamant that the sleep problem started in March. Gave very little away. Some psychomotor slowing was evident. Denied any thoughts of self-harm on direct questioning.

Label each of the six sentences as one of: an observation, a conclusion written as a conclusion, or a conclusion written as an observation. Then rewrite every sentence in the third category using only what the log supports, or mark it as unrecoverable if the log does not support it.
:::

Sentence by sentence. Five of the six are conclusions written as observations, and the middle category is empty, which is the finding. Nowhere in the paragraph does the writer say that something is their reading of what they saw.

*Guarded and clearly reluctant to be there* is a conclusion written as an observation, and the log does not support it. R. arrived early. Rewrite it out of the note, or attach what it rests on if the writer has something the log does not record.

*Kept their work fleece on for the entire session* is an observation. It is also the one sentence in this paragraph that a later reader could use, and the paragraph gives it no weight at all.

*Was adamant that the sleep problem started in March* is a conclusion written as an observation. R. gave the same account twice with the same dates. Consistency became stubbornness on the way onto the page. This is the fourth of the five recurring forms that a study of six hundred real outpatient notes found: portraying the patient as difficult, usually through one loaded adjective. Rewrite: "Gave the same account of onset on two occasions, with the same dates."

*Gave very little away* is a conclusion written as an observation, and it is recoverable: "Answered the questions asked and added little when invited to expand."

*Some psychomotor slowing was evident* is a conclusion written as an observation, and *was evident* is doing the disguising. Rewrite: "Pauses of five to ten seconds before answering questions about the last four months; ordinary pace on other subjects; rose slowly from the chair."

*Denied any thoughts of self-harm on direct questioning* is a conclusion written as an observation and it is also wrong. R., asked directly, described thoughts that life is not worth the effort, with no plan, no intent and nothing acted on. The paragraph has converted a described experience into a denial. Anyone reading this note next month believes R. said no.

## Third pass, on your own

:::{check}
:id: check-practice-independent
:kind: practice

The last two minutes of the appointment were left out of the earlier log. Here they are.

At 14:54 R. pulled the hood of the fleece up before opening the door and said "thanks for listening" at ordinary volume while looking at the floor. R. then stopped with a hand on the handle and asked whether the letter would go to their GP. Told that it would, R. said "fine" after a pause of about three seconds, and left at 14:55.

Write the sentence or two that belong in the mental-status description. Say which line each belongs on. Then name one conclusion you were tempted to write and did not, and say what it would have cost.
:::

There is more than one defensible answer. A workable version: "At the end of the appointment, asked whether the letter would go to their GP and paused for about three seconds before accepting that it would." That belongs on the appearance-and-behaviour line, or on a line about how R. engaged with the appointment if the record has one.

The hood, the floor, and the volume all belong to lines that already exist and should not be repeated. "Thanks for listening" is a social remark at the door and carries nothing a reader can use, though it costs little to include.

The conclusion most people reach for is that R. was worried about the letter. It may well be right and it is not observable. Written plainly it becomes the observer's reading of a three-second pause, and the next reader inherits an anxiety that nobody witnessed. If it matters, write the pause and the question, then ask R. at the next appointment. The description's job is to leave the question available.

## Where you go if a pass did not work

Missing the first pass usually means the clock test is doing all the work and the source test is doing none. Reread the two tests in the worked example, then redo the first pass asking only whether a reader who was not present could say what happened.

Missing the second pass usually means the disguised conclusions are hard to see, which is normal, because they are written in the grammar of observations. Take any paragraph you have written about anyone and underline every adjective that grades a person. Each one is a candidate.

Missing the third pass is the least worrying of the three. Independent writing from a raw observation is the hardest thing in the lesson, and the third pass has no single right answer. Compare yours with the version above and ask which of you a stranger could follow.

You will use all of this again. Mood phenomenology and function, later in the course, builds the mood picture out of a description like the one you have just written, and the outcome after it sets this appointment beside a second one months later. Both depend on the description having kept its events.
