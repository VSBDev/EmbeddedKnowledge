# Sixty numbers and no summary yet

The dinner-timing study has finished collecting. On the screen is a column of sixty fasting glucose readings, one per adult with type 2 diabetes: 137, 118, 155, 142, 129, and on down the list. Nobody can hold sixty numbers in mind at once, and no clinician, journal reader, or ethics committee wants to. They want two things. What is a typical reading in this group? And how far apart are these people?

Those two questions are what this lesson answers. By the end you will be able to pick a summary of the centre, calculate a summary of the spread, and say which pair of numbers tells the truth about a particular column of data. You will also be able to say when a perfectly correct average is a misleading thing to publish.

A fasting glucose reading is not an arbitrary number. It is the concentration of glucose in a blood sample drawn after the person has gone without food or drink other than water for at least eight hours, which is why a study can compare one person's Tuesday morning with another's Thursday morning at all. The overnight fast is the shared condition that makes the sixty readings comparable.

## Check the two prerequisites

Answer both before reading the routing feedback.

1. In this study, fasting glucose is recorded in mg/dL and can in principle take any value in its range, while an adherence rating takes only the labels 1, 2, 3, and 4 in a fixed order. What kind of data is each?
2. A colleague adds up the sixty glucose readings and divides by sixty. What quantity have they calculated, and what does its unit have to be?

Fasting glucose is continuous quantitative data; the adherence rating is ordinal. The colleague has calculated the arithmetic mean, and because every reading is in mg/dL the total is in mg/dL and dividing by a plain count leaves it in mg/dL. Your wording need not match.

## Recovery route

If either answer was unavailable, take this bridge before continuing.

- For data kinds, write each study column on its own line and ask two questions of it: can a value sit between two other values, and is the distance between neighbouring values fixed and meaningful? Continuous quantitative data answers yes to both. Ordinal data answers yes to the first and no to the second.
- For units, redo one small division out loud: 710 mg/dL shared among 5 people is 142 mg/dL each. The count carries no unit, so it cannot change the unit of the answer.

Then reread the two questions. Continue when you can name the kind of each column and state the unit of an average without hesitating.
