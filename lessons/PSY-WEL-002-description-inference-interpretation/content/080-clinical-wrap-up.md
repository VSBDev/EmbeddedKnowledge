# Clinical wrap-up: one sentence in R.'s referral letter

:::{callout}
:kind: boundary
:id: callout-wrap-up-boundary

Teaching example, not medical advice. R. is invented, the letter is invented, and nothing here says what is happening to R. or what should be done about it. The general facts about thyroid disease below are sourced and are used to show why a description has to survive in a record; they are not a rule for interpreting anyone's symptoms.
:::

Six weeks after the first appointment, a referral letter goes out. Two lines of R.'s note have been compressed into a single sentence, and a third line has not made the journey at all:

> Fatigue and constipation secondary to depression.

Six words. Sort them with the tools from this lesson before reading on.

## The sort

*Fatigue and constipation* began as line 9 of the note, which was a person's report and a good one: R. said he feels cold, constipated, and tired beyond what the sleep loss would explain. The letter has kept two of the three, dropped the cold, dropped R.'s comparison, and stripped the attribution. It now reads as a fact about R. Nothing marks it as something R. said.

*Secondary to depression* is a causal explanation, and it is doing what line 11 did with the alcohol: two words assert a direction and no rival is named.

*Depression* is a classification, imported from line 12 and now used as the explanation of something else.

And line 10, which recorded that no thyroid function result was available, has not been carried into the letter at all. It was the one line in the note that named something nobody had done yet, and it is the line that did not survive.

## Why this particular loss matters

Fatigue, lethargy, cold intolerance, weight gain and constipation are the commonest symptoms of hypothyroidism in adults. They are also strikingly non-specific. A clinical review reports a study in which none of thirty-four hypothyroidism-related symptoms could identify who had the condition, and notes that 70% of people with normal thyroid function have one or more thyroid-associated complaints. That is why the diagnosis rests on measuring thyroid-stimulating hormone in blood and not on how convincing a history sounds.

Notice that R.'s weight went the other way. That is exactly the sort of detail a description keeps and an explanation drops, and it is not this lesson's business to say what it means. It is this lesson's business that it should still be legible to whoever asks.

Follow that through with the lesson's own machinery. A report of feeling cold and tired is compatible with several accounts, so the report on its own settles nothing in any direction. Its value lies entirely in being a report that somebody can still act on. The letter has converted it into an explanation, and an explanation cannot be acted on: nobody can order a test against *secondary to depression*.

This is the failure from the fourth scene arriving at its destination. The compression looked harmless, the letter will be copied forward, and the version that gets carried is the one with the description removed. It is also the shape of what the triage note did to M.: a psychiatric category placed in front of a physical complaint, with the complaint's description thinned out behind it.

## Write the letter sentence properly

Three lines instead of one:

> R. reports feeling cold, constipated, and tired beyond what the disrupted sleep would account for.
>
> No thyroid function result is available.
>
> Whether these physical features belong with the mood picture has not been established. They are recorded here as reported, because they are non-specific and a thyroid result would speak to them.

Nothing in that version is a claim about R. that the note could not support. It costs three lines and it leaves every question open to the next person, which is the entire point of writing a description down.

## What this lesson does not do

It does not tell you what is happening with R., and it has not weighed one reading of this case against another. That work belongs to the mood differential and longitudinal course lesson later in this block.

What this lesson does is keep the weighing possible until that lesson arrives. A record that kept the descriptions separate from the conclusions can be re-opened. A record that merged them has decided the question by deleting the alternatives, and it did so without anybody noticing.

## Recovery route

Take the assessment attached to this lesson before looking back. Then use whichever of these applies.

- If the six kinds do not come back, reread the table in **What each kind of statement commits you to**, cover it, and re-sort the three lines in that scene's prompt from memory.
- If you can name the kinds but not the checks, work the twelve-line note again and fill in only the check column.
- If the two reports keep collapsing into one, redo the change in the worked example where the partner's line becomes a report about the phone, and say why the same speaker produced better evidence.
- If a classification and a causal explanation still feel like the same move, go back to the three fictional record sentences in **The two that get believed hardest** and name which words carry which claim.
- If you can sort but cannot repair, do the completion problem again and write only the material line, with no conclusion attached to it.

Retake with a different fictional record. Sorting the same twelve lines a second time tests your memory of the answers rather than your grip on the method.
