# Two shortcuts, and what they cost the next reader

:::{check}
:id: check-predict-the-shortcuts

Commit to an answer before reading on, in one line each.

1. Rank the six kinds from strongest evidence to weakest, or say why they cannot be ranked.
2. A colleague writes "poor concentration" and leaves the observation it came from three lines away, unlinked. Name one thing this costs, or say that it costs nothing.
:::

**Feedback on the ranking.** If you wrote one, keep it beside you: the next section is the reply to it, and there are three separate objections. If you said the six cannot be ranked, write down your reason now, because most people find one of the three and the other two are the interesting ones. The second question is answered further down this scene.

## The first shortcut: a ladder of reliability

:::{misconception}
:id: misconception-reliability-ladder

The shortcut sounds like this: direct observation is the hard evidence, a person's report is soft because people are unreliable narrators of themselves, and an informant's account is hearsay at one remove. Rank them in that order and you have a rule for whose word to take.

The intuition is imported from the rest of medicine, where a ranking often does useful work, because a number off an analyser and a report of feeling weak really are different grades of evidence there. Three things stop it working here.

An observation's check usually dies with the appointment. Nobody can go back into that room. A report's check is alive for as long as the person is: R. can be asked again tomorrow, in different words, and the answer compared with the first one. On the criterion of what can still be tested, the supposedly softest category outlasts the supposedly hardest.

The ranking also gets the informant backwards. Which account is more accurate depends on what is being described. For outwardly visible behaviour, the person who watches R.'s week is better placed than R. For what four in the morning feels like, nobody is better placed than R. A fixed ranking has to be wrong for one of those.

And the ladder assumes that observation in psychiatry is a firmer kind of evidence than report. Most of what is registered in the room is expressive, so reading it takes the same interpretive work as listening to an account, and the boundary between watching and concluding is genuinely soft.

Replace the ladder with something more useful: the six are differently checkable, and each is strongest where its own check is available. That is why they are worth keeping apart, and why none of them is the good one.

Test the replacement on a new question. Suppose you want to know how much R. actually slept last Tuesday. R.'s report is available and re-askable, and R. was awake in the dark without a clock, which is not a good position from which to time anything. The partner's report can cover when the light went off and when R. got up. An observation from a clinic room on Thursday says nothing about Tuesday at all. The best-placed source changed as soon as the question changed, which no fixed ranking can do.
:::

## The second shortcut: the harmless summary

The second shortcut is the one with teeth. "Poor concentration" compresses an observation into two words, everyone reading it understands, and nothing seems lost.

Compression is not the problem. Detachment is. The compressed line does not sit beside the material it came from, does not say whose conclusion it is, and does not say what else went into it. Line 6 of R.'s note sits directly above it, and even at that distance nothing on the page says the two are related.

Two facts about records turn that into a durable problem. Clinical notes are largely made of earlier notes: in one study of 23,630 inpatient progress notes written by 460 clinicians at a single centre over eight months, 18% of the text in a typical note had been entered by hand, 46% was copied, and 36% was imported from elsewhere in the record. A line does not get read once and discarded. It gets carried, and each carry makes it look more like something that was established somewhere.

The second fact is about what a carried label does to a reader. Work on diagnostic overshadowing, the misattribution of physical symptoms to an existing mental illness, names the psychiatric history recorded in the chart as one of the things that predisposes a clinician to explain new symptoms by it. The label does not sit inertly in the file. It arrives before the patient does.

Put the two together and a two-word summary written on a Tuesday evening can be read as an established finding a year later, by someone who has no way of knowing that it was one person's conclusion drawn from a few pauses in a conversation.

## The same line, written twice

Take line 7 from R.'s note and write it both ways.

> **A.** Poor concentration.

> **B.** Poor concentration, my judgement, from the pauses recorded above. R. did not raise concentration himself.

Version B is thirteen words longer and does three things A cannot. It marks the conclusion as one person's. It points at its material, so a reader can accept the pauses and reject the conclusion. And it records who raised concentration: the interviewer did, and R. never mentioned it, which is the kind of fact that changes what a later conversation opens with.

Both versions carry forward equally well, and only one of them can be argued with.

:::{check}
:id: check-recheck-the-repair

A different fictional record contains this line, and nothing else about the observation:

> Agitated.

Write the version B of it: the conclusion marked as somebody's, the material it rests on, and whether the person raised it themselves. You will have to invent the material, which is the point. Notice how much you had to supply that the original line did not contain, and that a real reader has no way to supply it.

Then answer: which of the six kinds is "agitated" as written, and which kind was it probably meant to be?
:::

**Feedback.** Whatever you invented, whether pacing or a raised voice or an inability to stay seated, the original line contains none of it and a later reader cannot reconstruct it. This is the harder case than line 7, because line 7 at least left its material somewhere in the note. Here nothing was written down at all. As written, "agitated" is an inference with its material gone. It was probably meant as an observation, and it could have been one if it had named what was seen. Compare your prediction at the top of this scene: if you said the summary costs nothing, this is the cost.
