# Six sentences about the same forty minutes

R. is 34 and works rotating night shifts in a warehouse. On a Tuesday evening R. spent forty minutes in a clinic room with someone whose job was to find out what had been happening. Six sentences from that conversation reached the record.

1. R. said he wakes around four most mornings and cannot get back to sleep.
2. R.'s partner said R. has not been himself since the winter.
3. R. answered slowly and twice stopped mid-sentence, looking at the floor for several seconds.
4. R. is having difficulty concentrating.
5. The picture meets the requirements for a depressive episode.
6. The night shifts wrecked R.'s sleep and the mood followed.

One person, one evening, six sentences. They can sit in a single paragraph with nothing marking them apart, and each one answers to a different test.

1. Ask R. again, and keep the words R. used.
2. Ask R.'s partner, and note which parts of R.'s life the partner actually sees.
3. Only somebody else in the room at the time could have contradicted this, and forty minutes later there is nothing left to look at.
4. Show what the sentence was drawn from and see whether it carries. The record does not say, so the test cannot be run.
5. Hand the same information to a second clinician and see whether they land in the same category.
6. Find evidence that tells this account apart from a rival one. No rival is named anywhere.

The fourth sentence is the interesting one. Nobody saw R. having difficulty concentrating. Somebody heard R. answer slowly, watched R. lose the thread twice, and drew a conclusion. Written in that form, the conclusion looks like something that was noticed.

## Why this is the method lesson

Psychiatry reaches most of what it studies through what a person says and through how they say it. There is no reading to take instead. What an examiner registers in the room is mostly expressive: speech, movement, facial expression, how easily a conversation gets going. Reading those takes much the same interpretive work as listening to an account.

General medicine has a fairly clean split between a symptom, which the patient reports, and a sign, which the examiner finds. A swollen ankle is a sign whatever the patient says about it. Psychiatry cannot copy that split cleanly, so it has to be careful about a different one: the difference between what was said, what was seen, and what was concluded.

A record that keeps those apart lets a later reader go back to the description and ask a new question of it. A record that merges them keeps only the conclusion, and a conclusion cannot be re-examined by anyone who no longer has the material it was drawn from.

Six categories have to stay apart: a person's report, another person's report, direct observation, inference, classification, and causal explanation. The first three are description. The fourth is inference. The last two are most of what people mean when they say interpretation. Every later lesson in this block runs on this sort, so it is worth getting slow and exact about it once.

## What you will be able to do

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- take each statement in a clinical extract, say which of the six kinds it is, and name what would check it;
- rewrite an entry that has buried a conclusion inside a description, so that both are visible and both can be questioned;
- say what a classification and a causal explanation each add to a record, and what neither of them settles.

You need PSY-WEL-001. Two words used here, *inference* and *model*, are already defined in the premed course; both are explained again as they come up, so you do not need to have taken it. Every case is fictional, no personal disclosure is asked for, and you may answer in writing, by speech-to-text, in a transcribed recording, or with a tutor.

**Feedback.** Sentence 6 is the one the warning is aimed at: it takes a question worth asking about night work and states it as a settled cause. Sentence 5 is a near miss, because a category can also be treated as more than a question, though it claims to explain nothing. Your second answer is doing the real work of this lesson. Slow answers, lost threads, a request to repeat a question: all of that is description. The sentence in the record is a conclusion drawn from it. If what you wrote down was "R. said he cannot follow things at the moment", you have found something better still, because in that case sentence 4 is not a conclusion at all, and the wording gives a reader no way to tell.
