# Practice on a record you have not seen

Three tasks, with the support falling away between them.

:::{callout}
:kind: boundary
:id: callout-practice-boundary

T. is invented, and so is anything you write here. These tasks are about how a sentence is written down and what would check it. None of them is a method for interviewing a person, deciding what to ask, or judging anyone's state, and nothing below should be carried into a conversation with a real person.
:::

## Completion problem

A line reads:

> Anxious in the interview.

Take it that you wrote the line, and complete the repair by filling the four blanks:

"Anxious in the interview" is: ______. What I actually saw or heard was: ______. The repaired line has two halves and two checks. The check on the material is ______, and the check on the conclusion is ______.

Then write the repaired line as one sentence.

**Feedback.** The first blank is *an inference*. The second has to contain something a second person in the room could have agreed or disagreed with: a shaking hand, a rate of speech, a question asked three times, an answer given before the question had finished. The third blank is another person present at the time, and that check has already expired. The fourth is different in kind: display the material and see whether it carries the conclusion, which anyone can still do from the page. A repaired line might read: *Anxious in my judgement: spoke quickly throughout, asked twice whether the door was closed.* If your second blank contained "seemed nervous", you have written a second inference where the material should be; try again with something a video would have caught.

## Supported problem

T. is fictional, 58, a retired teacher, seen at home by a community team. Six lines from the visit:

1. Daughter phoned: T. has not been out of the house for three weeks.
2. T. says the tablets make her feel "like cotton wool".
3. House cold; curtains closed; post unopened on the mat.
4. Low mood, likely bereavement-related.
5. Denies any thoughts of self-harm.
6. Poor self-care.

For each line, name the kind and the check. Two of the six are genuinely arguable; find them and say what the argument is about. Then name the pair of lines where one is the material for the other and the note has not connected them.

**Feedback.** Line 1 is another person's report, checked by asking the daughter, and it asks her about exactly the kind of thing a family member is well placed to see. Worth also recording is how often she visits, because that sets what "three weeks" is based on.

Line 2 is a person's report and the strongest line in the extract, because it kept T.'s own words. "Like cotton wool" cannot be recovered from any paraphrase of it, and a paraphrase would have to guess whether she meant sedation, slowed thinking, or something about her body.

Line 3 is direct observation, and it is well written: cold, closed, unopened are all things a second visitor could have contradicted. It is also the kind of observation whose check does not expire on the day, because a cold house and unopened post are still there next week. Notice that the line stops before drawing anything from what it describes.

Line 4 is two moves at once. "Low mood" may be T.'s report or the writer's compression, and the note gives no way to tell, which is the first arguable line. "Likely bereavement-related" is a causal explanation with no rival named; the word *likely* signals uncertainty about the answer while leaving the question itself unexamined.

Line 5 is the second arguable one. It reports an answer to a question, so it belongs with a person's report, and the word *denies* is doing work that a record should not do quietly. Denial implies something there to be denied. The line records neither what was asked nor how T. answered. *Asked directly about thoughts of self-harm; T. said no* is the same information with the implication removed.

Line 6 is an inference, and its material is sitting three lines above it in line 3. The note contains both halves of an argument and never joins them, so a later reader sees a conclusion and a description and no stated relation between them.

If more than two of your six answers landed in the wrong row, go back to the table in **What each kind of statement commits you to**, cover the check column, and re-sort R.'s twelve lines before attempting the next task. The sort becomes a habit before it becomes a rule, and the habit is built on repetition against different records.

## Independent problem

Invent a short fictional situation of your own, in two or three sentences, borrowing nothing from a real person's life. Then write eight lines of record about it that include at least one of each of the six kinds:

- a person's report with the person's own words kept;
- an informant's report with the vantage point named;
- an observation tight enough for a second person to contradict;
- an inference with its material pointed at and its owner named;
- a line that names a category and is written so a reader can see it is a category and not a description;
- a causal explanation with its rival named and something that would separate them.

Now hand your own eight lines to the checks:

- for each line, name what you would do to test it;
- find the line that will be least useful in a year, and say why;
- find any line that is two kinds joined by a comma, and split it;
- find any word that carries an implication you did not intend, as *denies* does;
- for your category line, say what a reader still does not know about how reproducible that category is.

If every line passes, make it harder: write the version of your record that a hurried clinician would actually produce, then repair it. The compressed version is the one that gets written in real life, and the repair is the skill.

:claims: claim-informant-discrepancy-normal, claim-vantage-point-asymmetry
:sources: source-delosreyes-informants, source-carlson-asymmetry

T. and the six lines are invented for this lesson. Two sources support the reasoning applied to line 1: that a person and someone close to them commonly give different accounts without either being at fault, and that an informant's account carries more weight for outwardly visible behaviour while the person's own carries more for internal states. The second source studied personality characteristics, so it supports a principle for choosing whom to ask, not a figure for how much better either source is in a clinical record.
