# Transfer: nine words in an emergency department

The sort was built for a psychiatric first appointment. Take it somewhere else.

M. is fictional, 41, and arrives at an emergency department at two in the morning with abdominal pain. The triage entry reads:

> Known anxiety disorder. Abdominal pain, likely functional. Obs stable.

Nine words. They will be the first thing every clinician who meets M. tonight reads, and in a busy department some of them will read nothing else before walking into the cubicle.

## Do the sort first

Work through it before reading on. Name the kind of each of the four statements in that entry, and the check that goes with each.

*Known anxiety disorder* is a classification, and it is not this department's classification. It was made somewhere else, at some time, by someone the note does not name, and it has been carried here. Its check is a second clinician applying the same guideline, and nobody in this department is going to run that check tonight.

*Abdominal pain* is a person's report, and it is the reason M. is here. Its check is asking M., which is available immediately and costs nothing.

*Likely functional* is a causal explanation. It asserts that the pain is produced by something other than a disease process in the abdomen. Its check is evidence that separates it from a rival, and the rivals here are the physical causes of abdominal pain. The note does not name one, and no such evidence is in it.

*Obs stable*, meaning the routine observations of pulse, blood pressure, temperature and the rest, is a compressed observation. It is the exception the second scene named: the numbers behind it are stored elsewhere in the system, so its check has not expired.

## What the sort changes

The problem is not that any of the four statements is false. Each of them may be exactly right. The problem is the order and the register: a classification made elsewhere sits in front of a report, and a causal explanation about tonight's pain has been written before anyone examined tonight's abdomen.

This is the shape of diagnostic overshadowing, which the fourth scene introduced. Work on the concept names a psychiatric history recorded in the chart as one of the things that predisposes a clinician towards that misattribution, and reports consequences that include worsening physical illness and death. The same work notes that people with mental illness die around a decade earlier than others, with most of that gap accounted for by physical disease such as heart disease and cancer; overshadowing is one contributor to that gap among several, and the size of its share is not established.

Notice also what the earlier scene predicted. *Likely functional* is three words, it is easy to carry, and every subsequent note that copies it makes it look more like a finding. Nothing in the copied version records that it was a guess made at triage before an examination.

## The task

Rewrite the entry so that a clinician arriving at the cubicle at 3am gets the same information without the conclusion arriving first. Your rewrite should:

- put M.'s report before anything else, in M.'s terms;
- keep the psychiatric history and label it as past history, so it cannot be read as an account of tonight;
- either delete the causal explanation or write it with its rival named;
- say what would separate the two.

Then answer two questions. Which single word in the original does the most damage, and why? And what would you want the note to contain that the original does not mention at all?

## Guidance after your attempt

A workable rewrite runs something like: *Abdominal pain since around 10pm, described as constant and worse on movement. Past history of anxiety disorder, diagnosed elsewhere. Not yet examined. Observations stable.* It is longer, and it leaves the question open for the person who has to answer it.

The word doing the most damage is *likely*. It has the texture of caution and the effect of a conclusion: it commits the writer to nothing while telling the reader what to expect. A note that said *possible functional pain, abdomen not yet examined* would be doing the opposite job with almost the same words.

The thing missing from the original is the examination, and after that, time. The original says nothing about when the pain started, and duration is the sort of description that later readers most often need and most often cannot recover.

## What successful transfer looks like

You have transferred the model if you can take a record from a setting the lesson never described, sort it without the categories being announced, and say what each statement's check would be in that setting. The sort itself did not change between a psychiatric clinic and an emergency department. What changed is which check is cheap: in the clinic, asking again is easy and the observation window is gone; in the department, the observation is continuous and the classification came from a system nobody present has access to.
