# What each kind of statement commits you to

Grammar will not sort these for you. "R. is exhausted", "R. looks exhausted" and "R. says he is exhausted" have almost the same shape and three different origins.

Two questions do the sorting, and they have to be asked together. Where did this come from? And what would have to happen for it to be shown wrong?

| Kind | Where it comes from | What writing it commits you to | What would check it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A person's report | R. | that R. said this, in something close to these words | ask R. again; keep R.'s own wording |
| Another person's report | an informant: someone who knows R. and is speaking about R. | that this informant said this, from where they stand in R.'s life | ask that informant; record who they are and what they get to see |
| Direct observation | the interviewer's eyes and ears, inside one bounded stretch of time | that this happened in the room during those minutes | a second person who was present at the time |
| Inference | none of the above; it is drawn from them | that this follows from material already stated | display the statements it rests on and see whether they carry it |
| Classification | a published set of requirements | that this picture meets the requirements of a named category | apply the same guideline and see whether a second clinician reaches the same category |
| Causal explanation | a model of how the parts act on one another | that one thing is producing or maintaining another | evidence that separates this account from a named rival |

The bottom four rows are told apart by their checks. The top two share a check of the same shape and are told apart by their origin, because the check leads to a different person and which person you go to turns out to matter more than anything else on either row.

Sort claims, not sentences. A single sentence can carry two kinds at once, and several in this lesson do. When that happens the sentence gets two entries and two checks.

One thing the table cannot show. A clinical record also holds lines that are not statements about the person at all: a missing result, a phone number, the date of the next appointment. Those belong to none of the six, and one of them turns up in the worked example.

## The two kinds of report

The first instinct when a person and an informant disagree is to treat the gap as an error and pick a winner. Across clinical research, the two accounts overlap only weakly. Where the overlap is expressed as a correlation, values around 0.2 to 0.4 are typical, on a scale where 1 would mean the two accounts rise and fall together exactly and 0 would mean they carry nothing in common. Low agreement is closer to the norm than to the exception.

Some of that gap is error. Much of it is information, because informants see different stretches of a life and behaviour genuinely differs across them.

Which account is closer to the mark depends on what is being described. For internal states that cannot be watched from outside, the person's own account carries more weight. For outwardly visible and evaluative characteristics, the informant's account carries more. R.'s partner is the better source for whether R. has stopped answering the phone. R. is the better source for what four in the morning feels like. A record that keeps only one of the two has thrown away the comparison, which is why these are two kinds and not one.

Write the source into the sentence. "R.'s partner said R. has not been himself since the winter" is a usable line. "R. has not been himself since the winter" is the same content with the vantage point deleted, and nobody can now tell whether to go back to R. or to the partner.

## Observation, and how little of it there is

Direct observation is what happened between two people inside a bounded stretch of time, written so that another person in the room could have agreed or disagreed.

"R. paused for around five seconds before answering three of the questions" is an observation. "R. was slowed" sits on the edge, because *slowed* is a comparison and the record has not said with what: R.'s own usual pace, or people in general. Those are different claims and only one of them is available to somebody meeting R. for the first time. "R. is withdrawn" has left the category altogether; withdrawal is a pattern across weeks, and nobody watches a pattern across weeks in forty minutes.

The check on an observation is unusually strong and usually short-lived. Another person present at the time could have contradicted it. Once the appointment ends that check normally expires, so the wording chosen in the room is the only version anybody will ever have. The exception is an observation that leaves something behind: a pulse written into a chart, or a house that will still be cold next week. Where a trace survives, so does the check.

## Inference

An inference is a conclusion drawn from statements already made. That is the same meaning the premed science-method lessons give the word: a conclusion drawn from observations and reasoning instead of something recorded directly. Those lessons also let *interpretation* stand in as a second name for it. Here *interpretation* is reserved for the last two rows, classification and causal explanation, so *inference* stays narrow and keeps a row of its own.

The check is to display the material. An inference with its material attached is an argument a colleague can take apart: they can accept the pauses and the slow answers and reject the conclusion, or add a fact that changes it. An inference whose material has been deleted is beyond reach. Nobody can get back to what it was built from, so nobody can do anything with it except believe it or ignore it.

## Classification

"The picture meets the requirements for a depressive episode" is a statement about a rule set. It says that somebody compared this person's picture with a published description and found that it fits. It says nothing about what produced the picture, how it will move, or what would help.

The check on a classification is agreement between clinicians, and agreement can be measured. The usual measure is a **kappa**: the proportion of cases on which two independent raters assign the same category, adjusted downwards for the agreement that guessing alone would produce. A kappa of 1 means they always agree; a kappa of 0 means they agree no more often than chance would arrange.

In the World Health Organization's field study for the ICD-11 chapter on mental disorders, 339 clinicians assessed 1,806 patients across 28 sites in 13 countries. Two clinicians sat in on the same interview and then classified the person independently. Kappa ran from 0.45 for dysthymic disorder up to 0.88 for social anxiety disorder.

Those two numbers came from one study, one guideline and one design, and a record writes both categories in the same font. A reader holding a category name has no way of knowing which end of that range it sits at, which is worth saying out loud whenever the name is used to decide something.

## Causal explanation

"The night shifts wrecked R.'s sleep and the mood followed" is the strongest of the six and usually has the least behind it.

It commits you to a model, which is the premed course's word for a representation that keeps some features and relationships, drops the rest, and explains within stated limits. The model inside this sentence is small: it picks night work and sleep, treats four months of R.'s life as background, and asserts a direction of travel. Say that out loud and the check becomes obvious. What would separate this from the account in which the sleep loss and the mood are both downstream of something else, or from the account in which four months of low mood made the night shifts unmanageable?

A causal explanation with no named rival has nothing to be checked against, so naming the rival is most of the work.

:::{check}
:id: check-sort-three-lines

Cover the table. Here are three lines from three different fictional records. For each one, name the kind and the check, and say which words told you.

1. "Sister says he has been spending money he does not have."
2. "Tearful throughout."
3. "Symptoms began after the redundancy."

Then answer: which of the three could still be tested a year from now, and which one has already run out of time?
:::

**Feedback.** The first is an informant's report, checked by asking the sister, and *says* is the word that marks it; notice that the sister is well placed for this, since spending is visible from outside. The second is direct observation, checked by a second person who was in the room, and *throughout* dates it to the appointment. The third is a causal explanation, checked by evidence separating it from a rival, and *after* is doing the work: a sequence has been stated and a cause has been implied without either being argued. On the last question, the sister can be asked again a year from now, the redundancy question stays open and unanswered, and the tears are gone: nobody can go back into that room, and nothing was left behind.
