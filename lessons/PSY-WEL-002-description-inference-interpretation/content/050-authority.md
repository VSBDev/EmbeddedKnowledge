# The two that get believed hardest

Four of the six kinds are modest about themselves: a report says who said it, an observation says when, and an inference written properly shows its working. The remaining two are the ones this lesson calls interpretation. They arrive with more authority than they have earned, and they arrive in the sentences that change what happens next.

## One word to pin down first

*Depressive episode* has appeared twice already, in the first scene's fifth sentence and at the foot of R.'s note, and the word inside it does two jobs in English. In ordinary speech, depression means feeling low. In psychiatry it names a syndrome defined by a published set of requirements, which is a claim about a rule set and not a description of a mood. This lesson uses only the second sense.

Turn that test on R.'s note. Line 12 reads *Impression: depressive episode*, and *Impression* marks it as the writer's judgement without saying which guideline was applied or which requirements were met. A reader cannot tell whether the second sense was meant carefully or the first sense slipped into a category's clothing. That is worth catching, because a letter that slides has turned a mood into a category with nobody deciding to. The mood lessons later in this block take the technical sense apart properly.

## What a classification is doing

A category name says one thing: someone compared this picture with a published set of requirements and found that it fits. That is genuinely useful. It lets two clinicians on different continents mean the same thing, it connects a person to a body of evidence about people described the same way, and in most systems it is what opens a door to a service.

It says nothing about what produced the picture, how it will move over the next year, or what will help. Those are separate questions with separate evidence, and a category name answers none of them by containing them.

The ICD-11 field-study kappas from the previous scene are the reason to keep saying so. Two clinicians hearing the same interview agreed at 0.45 for dysthymic disorder and 0.88 for social anxiety disorder, under one guideline and one design. A record writes both category names in the same font, and neither the record nor the reader carries the number that belongs to it. Those figures also came from specialist mental-health services in the participating countries. The same categories get applied in general practice, in emergency departments, and by clinicians who reach for them far less often.

## What a causal explanation is doing

"The night shifts wrecked R.'s sleep and the mood followed" is the sentence a reader remembers, and the one with least behind it. The previous scene set out the small model inside it: night work and sleep selected, four months of R.'s life left as background, a direction asserted.

What the sentence *adds* is the reason it survives every compression. A cause is the only one of the six that tells a reader what to do next. A report invites another question, an observation invites a second observer, a classification invites a service; a cause invites an action, and it is the sentence a busy reader acts on.

What it does not settle is almost everything a reader will take from it: whether the association is real at all, whether it holds for this person or only on average, and whether removing the cause would change anything. None of those follows from writing the sentence down.

Comparing whole explanatory models against each other, biological against psychological against social, is the next outcome in this module and is not the work here. The work here comes first: recognising a causal sentence when you see one, and knowing that it owes you a rival and something that would tell the two apart.

## A causal sentence that outran its evidence

The clearest illustration comes from outside the case note altogether: the sentence that depression is caused by a chemical imbalance, and specifically by low serotonin.

In 2022 a systematic umbrella review in *Molecular Psychiatry*, meaning a review that gathers up the existing reviews rather than the original studies, examined six lines of serotonin research. It reported no consistent evidence of an association between serotonin and depression, and no support for the claim that depression is caused by lowered serotonin activity or concentration. The same review cites surveys in which 80% or more of the general public believe it is established that depression is caused by a chemical imbalance.

In 2023 the same journal published a reply from a different group, who argued that the review had applied its methods inconsistently, reported the evidence selectively, and misread the brain-imaging studies, and who held that the serotonin system is implicated in depression.

The dispute is live and this lesson does not settle it. What the episode shows is the shape of the problem. A causal sentence reached near-universal public standing while the evidence behind it was under specialist dispute, and the places it travelled to, from a leaflet to a conversation to a letter, did not carry the dispute with it.

## What to write instead

A causal explanation earns its place when it names three things: the model, the rival, and what would separate them.

> **Before.** The night shifts wrecked R.'s sleep and the mood followed.

> **After.** R. works rotating nights and reports waking at four and not returning to sleep. One reading is that the shift pattern disrupted the sleep and the mood followed. Against it: the low mood is recorded as four months old, and the drinking has increased over the last two months by R.'s own account to help with sleep, which could account for the early waking on its own. The note dates neither the shift change nor the onset of the early waking, and that dating is what would separate these.

The second version is longer and is doing something the first cannot: it can be wrong in a way a reader can find. It is also honest about the fact that the record does not yet contain what the question needs.

Three sentences from three different fictional records. For each, say which of the six kinds it is, and what would check it. One of them is two kinds in one sentence.

1. "Meets requirements for generalised anxiety disorder."
2. "Symptoms are driven by the housing situation."
3. "Panic disorder, precipitated by the bereavement."

Then answer: which of the three could two clinicians disagree about while both applying the guideline correctly?
:::

**Feedback.** The first is a classification, checked by a second clinician applying the same guideline. The second is a causal explanation, checked by evidence separating it from a rival, and it names no rival. The third is two kinds joined by one word: a classification and a causal explanation, whose checks have nothing in common even though they share a comma. The last question points at the first sentence and at the classification half of the third. Those are the statements a guideline governs, and a kappa of 0.45 is exactly what two clinicians applying one guideline correctly and landing apart looks like. Nobody is applying a guideline to the second sentence, so the question does not attach to it at all.
