# Twelve lines from R.'s note, sorted

Here is the note from R.'s first appointment, as a clinician might actually write it: short, telegraphic, and mixing all six kinds without marking any of them.

:::{worked-example}
:id: worked-example-sorting-the-note

Your job is to assign each line to a kind, name the check that goes with it, and work out which lines a reader six months from now will be unable to do anything with.

1. Four months of low mood and loss of interest.
2. Wakes at 4am, does not return to sleep.
3. Has stopped seeing friends.
4. Partner: "not himself since February".
5. Weight down about 6 kg, unintentional.
6. Speech slow; pauses of several seconds before answering.
7. Poor concentration.
8. Alcohol most evenings, increased over the last two months, to help with sleep.
9. Feels cold, constipated, tired beyond the sleep loss.
10. No thyroid function result available.
11. Sleep disturbance secondary to alcohol.
12. Impression: depressive episode.

**Start with what is missing.** Twelve lines and one interviewer. The lines are all that survives; the forty minutes are gone. Nothing in the extract says which words were R.'s, which were the partner's, and which were the writer's, and recovering that is most of the task.

**Sort by the check, not by the wording.** Two lines with identical grammar can belong in different rows, and a line that reads like a plain fact can turn out to be a conclusion. So for each line, ask where it came from and what would have to happen to show it wrong. Two of the lines carry two kinds at once, and their rows say so.

| Line | Kind | Check | What it has lost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | A person's report | Ask R. again | R.'s own words. *Low mood* and *loss of interest* are the writer's vocabulary, and asking again next month gets a fresh account, not the one R. gave that evening. |
| 2 | A person's report | Ask R. again | Nothing. Specific, dated, and answerable. |
| 3 | A person's report | Ask R. again | Only the explicit attribution, which a reader can safely supply here. In a note where the partner also spoke, that would be a real ambiguity. |
| 4 | Another person's report | Ask the partner | The partner's material. *Not himself* is the partner's own inference, and the note kept the conclusion without what produced it. |
| 5 | Two candidates and no way to choose | None, until the note says which it was | Its source. If R. was weighed, this is an observation made by whoever weighed him; if R. said so, it is a report. *Unintentional* can only have come from R. |
| 6 | Direct observation | A second person in the room, at the time | Nothing, and it is the only line whose check has already expired. |
| 7 | Inference | Show the material | Its attribution and its working. Line 6 is plainly part of what it was drawn from, and the note never says so, never says what else went into it, and never says whose conclusion it is. |
| 8 | A person's report, with R.'s own causal account attached to it | Ask R. again | Nothing. *To help with sleep* is R.'s explanation of R.'s drinking, and the line correctly marks it as R.'s. |
| 9 | A person's report | Ask R. again | Very little. *Beyond the sleep loss* is a comparison added by the writer, worth noticing and stated in the open. |
| 10 | None of the six | Order the test | Nothing. It records a gap, which is why the test can still be ordered. |
| 11 | A category word plus a causal explanation | Two different checks, and neither is available | The two descriptions it replaced, which are lines 2 and 8, and the rival it never names. |
| 12 | Classification | A second clinician applying the same guideline, if the note says which guideline | The requirements it was measured against, and any sense of how reproducible that category is. |

**Four lines deserve the extra minute, and a fifth will not sort at all.**

Line 3 looks like the most factual line in the extract and is a report. The test is simple: who would you go to in order to check it? You would go to R. If the partner had said it, the same words would be an informant's report and would carry a different vantage point.

Line 4 has two layers and the note kept one. The partner said something, which is checkable by asking the partner. The partner also concluded something, and what the partner actually noticed has not been written anywhere. Ask the partner what changed in February and the line moves into usable territory.

Line 7 is the failure this lesson exists for. Some of its material did survive, in line 6, and nothing on the page joins them. The note gives a reader a conclusion and a description on consecutive lines with no stated relation between them, no indication of whose judgement the conclusion is, and no way to know what else fed it. Repairing that on the day costs a dozen words. Repairing it in six months is impossible, because only the writer ever knew what else went in.

Line 11 does two things in five words. *Sleep disturbance* replaces line 2, which was specific and dated, with a category word that has no published rule set behind it, so there is nothing for a second clinician to re-apply. Then *secondary to* asserts a direction: the drinking came first and the sleep followed. Those two words are the whole causal claim, and they are the reason the line is more dangerous than it looks. Line 8 has R.'s account of the same two things pointing the other way.

Line 5 is the one that will not sort. The note never says whether R. was weighed or whether R. said so, and a line that could be either is usable as neither. Failing to place a line is a legitimate result: it locates a defect in the note.

**A note on what the sort does and does not decide.** The sort is about the statement as written, not about whether it is true. Line 2 could be sorted correctly and still be wrong; line 7 could be sorted as an inference and still turn out to be a good one. Line 12 is an impression recorded by the writer, and whether it fits R. is work for a later lesson in this block.

**Change one thing and sort again.** Suppose line 4 read: *Partner: R. has stopped answering the phone since February.* The kind has not changed and the value has. The partner is now reporting something they are placed to see, which is where an informant's account carries more weight than the person's own. The original line asked the partner for a judgement about R.'s inner state, which is the direction in which R.'s own account carries more. Same category, same speaker, better evidence, because the vantage point changed.

Now change line 6 to *R. was slowed*. Still an observation by intent, and no longer checkable, because *slowed* compares R. with a baseline the note never states. The category survived the change; the check did not.

**What survives.** Ask which lines a reader can still work with six months from now.

Lines 2, 3, 8 and 9 can be re-asked of R., and the answers will still be about the same things. Line 4 can be re-asked of the partner. Line 10 names something nobody has done yet, so the test can still be ordered; nothing on the page says that line 9 is why it matters, and whoever reads the note next has to make that connection again. Line 6 is fixed forever and was written tightly enough to bear it.

Four lines have gone dead. Line 1 is re-askable but its wording is lost, so nobody will ever know how R. described the four months. Line 5 cannot be sorted at all. Line 7 has lost its attribution and everything that fed it except line 6. Line 11 has neither its descriptions nor its rival. Line 12 rests on all of them, and nothing in the note announces which four are gone.

**One last question.** Go back to the answer you kept in the first scene, where you wrote what someone would have had to see or hear to write "R. is having difficulty concentrating". Compare it with line 6, and notice how much of what you wrote is not in this note at all. Then: if you could rewrite exactly one line of this extract, which one recovers the most for a later reader, and what makes it worth more than the others?
:::

A strong answer names line 7 or line 11 and explains the choice by what the rewrite restores. Rewriting line 7 as *poor concentration, my judgement, from the pauses in line 6* gives the next reader an argument they can accept or reject. Rewriting line 11 as *R. reports the drinking increased to help with sleep; whether the drinking or the early waking came first is not established* restores two descriptions and admits that a direction was asserted without evidence. Choosing line 12 is a weaker answer, because the impression sits downstream of everything else: repair the material and the impression becomes checkable on its own.

The note extract, its twelve lines, and R. are invented for this course. The three sources support only the general points used in the sort: that psychiatric material is reached through report and expressive behaviour, that a person and an informant commonly give different accounts without either being an error, and that which account is more accurate depends on whether the characteristic is internal or outwardly visible. The asymmetry evidence comes from research on personality, so it is a principle to reason with here and carries no measured effect size for clinical records.
