# A cell with two outsides

Pick one absorptive cell near the top of a colonic crypt and stand it up the way it actually sits. Above it is the lumen: water, salts, whatever the bacteria down there have finished fermenting, and the bacteria themselves. Below it is the tissue, the blood supply, and the rest of you. The cell is the join.

Anatomy calls this cell a colonocyte, and it is the absorptive kind of enterocyte that makes up most of the colonic lining. It is polarised, meaning its two ends are built differently and do different work. The end facing the lumen carries **microvilli**, fine finger-like projections packed so densely that they form a single fuzzy border under a light microscope. A mature absorptive cell of this kind carries around a thousand of them. They multiply the area available for taking things up, and water absorption from the gut contents happens across them. The other end faces neighbouring cells and the sheet of material the epithelium sits on, and it is where the cell hands its cargo onward.

Two names to keep straight, because the rest of the lesson uses them constantly. The lumen-facing end is **apical**. The other end, along with the sides in contact with neighbours, is **basolateral**. They are not interchangeable. A pump that belongs on one of them is useless on the other, and a great deal of what this cell does is a matter of getting the right protein to the right face.

## The division you cannot see from outside

So far this is a division of the cell's *surface*. The more interesting division is inside.

If you could open the cell, you would not find one bag of soup with machinery floating in it. You would find membranes running all through the interior, folding into stacks and tubes and closed sacs, marking off spaces that are chemically nothing like the fluid around them. Taken together, those membrane-enclosed compartments occupy close to half the volume of a cell. Each one holds its own distinct set of proteins, which is another way of saying each one does a job the others do not.

That should look expensive, because it is. Membrane costs lipid and protein to build. Anything the cell wants to move from one compartment to another now has to cross a boundary, which needs machinery. And every product now has an address problem: made in one place, needed in another.

So the question this lesson answers is the one a sceptical engineer would ask.

**Why would a cell pay for internal membranes at all?**

The answer has three parts, and they are not obvious until you see them working. A compartment lets the cell run chemistries at the same time that would ruin each other in one shared volume. It lets the cell hold a local concentration far away from the concentration outside it. And because compartments sit in a fixed order along a route, passing through them imposes a fixed order on what gets done to a molecule.

Against those three purchases there is a standing bill, and the last scene of the lesson adds it up.

## One sentence about emergence, then onwards

[Cell theory, scale, and emergence (PREM-BIO-001)](https://embeddedknowledge.io/premed/lessons/read/?lesson=PREM-BIO-001) defined an emergent property as one belonging to an organisation of components and not to any component alone, and gave you a two-part test for it. Compartmentalisation is that same idea one level down: the cell is the organisation, the compartments are the components, and an acid interior or a proton gradient is a property of the arrangement and of nothing in it. Apply the test where it helps and do not make a mystery of it.

Then a harder one. If both kinds of cell run the same basic chemistry, what does that tell you about whether compartments are *necessary* for life?

Eukaryotic cells are the compartmentalised ones. Bacteria and archaea run most of their chemistry in a single internal volume, bounded by the plasma membrane and whatever surrounds it.

The second question is the one worth sitting with. Bacteria are alive, they are numerous, and they manage without an endomembrane system, so compartments are plainly not a requirement for being a cell. That reframes the whole question. Compartments are an investment a eukaryotic cell makes, and an investment is only sensible if it buys something. Everything below is an answer to what.

## What this lesson leaves alone

Two neighbouring questions are deliberately not answered here. What a membrane is actually made of, and why its behaviour changes with its composition, belongs to the lesson on membrane structure. How a specific molecule is moved across a membrane, against a gradient or down one, belongs to the lesson on membrane transport after it. This lesson treats a membrane as a boundary that can hold a difference, and asks what a cell does with that.
