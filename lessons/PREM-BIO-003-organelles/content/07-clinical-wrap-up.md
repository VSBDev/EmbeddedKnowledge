# Clinical wrap-up: everything was built, and none of it arrived

:::{callout}
:kind: boundary
:id: callout-teaching-boundary

Teaching example, not medical advice. The infant below is invented. The condition described is real and the findings quoted come from published review, but nothing here is a diagnostic criterion, a threshold, a test performance, a management plan, or a statement of what any finding would mean in any real patient. This is a reasoning exercise about compartments and delivery.
:::

An invented newborn, a few days old, has watery diarrhoea that does not stop and does not respond to withholding feeds. The infant is losing weight and losing water faster than it can be replaced by mouth. A small-bowel biopsy is taken.

Under the microscope the epithelium is there. The cells are present, they are alive, they are the right shape and in the right place. What is missing is the brush border: the apical surface has few microvilli, or abnormal ones, or none. Staining for a protein that belongs on the apical membrane does not find it on the surface. It finds it in a band just underneath, inside the cell. There are vesicle-like structures in the cytoplasm, and in some cells there are microvilli sitting *inside* an intracellular inclusion, pointing into a space that has no business existing.

This is microvillus inclusion disease, and everything you need to reason about it is in the fourth scene of this lesson.

## First, note where we have gone

This case leaves the colon. Microvillus inclusion disease presents as a severe neonatal enteropathy and its biopsy findings are described in small-bowel tissue, where the same absorptive architecture appears on villi instead of around crypts. The absorptive cell, its apical brush border and its basolateral face are the ones you have been working with all lesson. Take the case, then come back: the colonocyte is the running example for the rest of this module.

## Work it with the lesson's own tools

**Which face does the job, and what is on it?** The absorptive cell's uptake happens across the apical surface, and the microvilli of the brush border are what make that surface large enough to be worth using. Around a thousand of them on a mature cell, packed together. The basolateral face, doing a different job, is not affected in the way the apical one is.

**Is this a failure of manufacture or of delivery?** Pass three of the practice scene asked exactly this question, and the answer here is delivery. The disease is caused by mutations in genes for myosin Vb, syntaxin 3 or syntaxin binding protein 2. Those are not genes for brush-border proteins. They are genes for the machinery that moves vesicles and fuses them with the plasma membrane, so what fails is vesicle trafficking or vesicle fusion, and the consequence reported is that vesicles carrying apical proteins accumulate subapically.

Read that against the diagram. Manufacture is intact: transcription, translation, entry into the endoplasmic reticulum, folding, modification through the Golgi, sorting in the trans-Golgi network. The cargo is made, addressed and packaged. The last leg fails, and the cargo stops one step short of where it belongs.

**Does the model predict what the pathologist sees?** It predicts three things, and all three are what is reported. Apical proteins should be found subapically instead of on the surface, and they are. The brush border should be absent or abnormal, because it is built from material that has to be delivered to that face, and it is. Vesicles that cannot fuse where they were meant to should be visible as accumulated intracellular structures, and they are, along with the inclusions the disease is named for.

**Does the model predict the clinical picture?** The absorptive surface of the intestine is not being built, so the intestine cannot absorb across it, and what is not absorbed stays in the lumen and leaves with the stool. Extremely watery diarrhoea from the first days of life, with dehydration and weight loss, is what published description reports, and it is what a failure to build the absorptive surface would produce.

## Why this belongs in a lesson about organelles

Nothing has gone wrong with any organelle's condition. The lysosomes are still acid. The mitochondria still hold their gradient. The endoplasmic reticulum still folds proteins and the Golgi still modifies them. Every compartment in this lesson is working.

What has failed is the fourth item on the bill: the system that gets each product to the compartment or the face where it works. The first scene said that internal membranes create an address problem that an undivided cell does not have, and that solving it is part of the price. A disease of the delivery machinery is what that line item looks like when it is not paid.

That is also why "organelle" is a poor unit of understanding on its own. A list of organelles and their functions has no place to put this case. A cell described as a set of maintained conditions connected by routes has exactly one place to put it, and it is the arrow between two boxes.

## The thread back to the crypt

The absorptive cell in a colonic crypt lives about four to five days, and the whole lining is rebuilt continuously. Every one of those cells has to construct its own brush border, its own apical and basolateral protein sets, and its own complement of compartments, from scratch, within a few days of being born at the crypt base. The delivery machinery in this lesson is not doing a job once. It is doing it in every cell of a tissue that never stops replacing itself.

The next lessons take the parts of that apart. What a membrane is made of, and why the apical membrane of this cell is built differently from its basolateral membrane, is the lesson on membrane structure. How sodium and glucose actually cross it, and why that matters for treating the dehydration in the case above, is the lesson on membrane transport after it. Both are questions this lesson deliberately left standing.

## What you should be able to do now

Three things, and each has an assessment item.

Given a stated cellular job, name the condition it requires and the compartment that maintains it, and justify the pairing by the condition rather than by the organelle's name. Compute a concentration ratio between two compartments from their pH values, check it against an independently reported figure, and say what holding that difference costs. Predict where a product ends up, and what stops working, when a named step of the route from gene to working location fails, separating a fault of manufacture from a fault of delivery.

The mastery check holds four untimed, retakeable items worth twelve points. Attempt each before opening its reasoning. A missed criterion routes you to a different case, never the same one twice.
