# Where the compartment is the machine

Everything so far treats a compartment as a place with conditions: somewhere to put chemistry, or somewhere to put it in order. The mitochondrion is different, and the difference is worth a scene of its own, because getting it wrong is what makes "the powerhouse of the cell" such a useless thing to have memorised.

Here is the mechanism in one pass. Oxidising fuel releases energy, and a mitochondrion does not use that energy directly to make ATP. It uses it to pump protons across a membrane, building an electrical and chemical potential difference between the two sides. Then it lets the protons flow back, and the returning flow turns the rotor of an enzyme, ATP synthase, which makes ATP as it turns. The energy takes a detour through a gradient before it becomes chemistry.

Now ask what that arrangement requires. A gradient is a *difference between two sides*. It does not exist in a substance; it exists across a boundary. There is no such thing as a proton gradient in an open volume, in the same way there is no such thing as a pressure difference in a room with no wall down the middle.

So the membrane here belongs to the mechanism. Oxidative phosphorylation requires a membrane to couple the transport of protons to the synthesis of ATP; take the membrane away and you have not removed a wrapper, you have removed the machine. Chemical uncouplers make the same point experimentally: they let protons cross the membrane freely, which dissipates the proton potential, and respiration then proceeds while ATP synthesis falls to nothing. Fuel is still being burnt. Nothing is stored, because there is nowhere for the difference to sit.

That is why the label is unhelpful. A power station is a building containing generators, and you could in principle move the generators outdoors. You cannot move this one outdoors. Say instead that a mitochondrion is a compartment whose boundary holds a gradient, and the mechanism follows from the sentence.

## What this particular cell is burning

Ask what a mature colonocyte is actually burning, and the crypt argument closes on itself. Seventy to eighty per cent of its energy comes from **butyrate**, a short-chain fatty acid, oxidised to carbon dioxide by the mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation system. That is the machinery just described, running at high load.

Where does the butyrate come from? It is made by the bacteria in the lumen, on the other side of the apical membrane, as a product of their fermentation. The cell described at the start of this lesson takes up a fuel manufactured by the population it is keeping out of the tissue.

That metabolism has a measurable consequence. Mature colonocytes running this way consume oxygen heavily, which holds the oxygen partial pressure at the epithelium below 7.6 mmHg, under one per cent oxygen. The surface of the colonic lining is a hypoxic place, and it is hypoxic because of what is happening inside the mitochondria of the cells that make it up.

Stop there instead of going one step further. Whether that epithelial hypoxia is what maintains the anaerobic community in the lumen is an active research question and a claim this lesson does not make. What is established is the direction of the arrow so far: bacterial fermentation supplies the fuel, mitochondrial compartments burn it, and the oxygen tension at the tissue surface is a consequence.

Note also what kind of property that is. No single mitochondrion has an oxygen tension. Epithelial hypoxia belongs to a sheet of cells all consuming oxygen at once, which is the emergence test from the first lesson applied at tissue scale to a process that starts inside an organelle.

## Adding up the bill

The purchases are on the table. Here is the invoice.

**Building material.** Every compartment needs a boundary, and boundaries are built from lipid and protein that the cell has to synthesise. Membrane-enclosed compartments take up close to half a cell's volume, so this is not a marginal expense.

**Standing energy.** Each maintained condition leaks. The V-ATPase pumps protons into the lysosome for as long as the lysosome exists, using ATP to do it, because a five-hundred-fold gradient runs down the moment you stop. That cost never ends and it never falls.

**Crossing charges.** In an undivided cell a molecule made in one place is already everywhere it needs to be. Once you have boundaries, everything that has to get from one side to the other needs a transporter, a channel, or a vesicle, and each of those is a protein the cell has to make, place and regulate.

**Addressing.** Every product needs a destination encoded in it and a receptor able to read the code: signal sequences read by the signal recognition particle, mannose 6-phosphate read by mannose 6-phosphate receptors, and a separate apparatus again for sorting to the apical face against the basolateral face.

Set that against what it buys. Incompatible chemistries at once, which is how a cell can hold a compartment at pH 5 and another near 7.2 and let both work. Local concentration, which is how hydrogen ions can be five hundred times more concentrated in one small volume than a nanometre away. Controlled sequence, which is how a fixed order of compartments gives a fixed order of modifications without needing a controller. And a fourth thing this scene added: a boundary that is itself the working part of an energy-converting device.

Bacteria pay none of these costs and buy none of these things, and they are doing fine. The investment is real and it is optional, and everything a eukaryotic cell does with it is what the rest of this module is about.

Two hypothetical interventions on the same colonocyte, each affecting one compartment.

1. An agent makes the mitochondrial inner membrane freely permeable to protons.
2. An agent stops the V-ATPase in the lysosomal membrane.

For each, say which of the three purchases is being attacked, and predict what stops working. Then say what the two cases have in common that a list of organelle names would not have told you.
:::

The first attacks the gradient itself. Protons stop accumulating on one side, the potential difference collapses, and ATP synthesis stops even though fuel is still being oxidised and every protein in the mitochondrion is intact. The second attacks a maintained condition. Protons stop being driven into the lysosome, the interior drifts towards the cytosolic pH, the acid hydrolases move away from their optimum, and digestion inside an undamaged lysosome full of undamaged enzymes slows.

What the two have in common is the shape of the failure. In neither case is a component broken. In both cases a *difference* has been lost, and everything that depended on the difference stops. That is what it means to say a compartment is a maintained condition rather than a container, and it is not a fact you can reach by learning which organelle is which.
