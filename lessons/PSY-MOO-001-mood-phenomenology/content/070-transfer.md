# Reading a description you did not write

Everything so far assumed you were in the room. Most of the time you are not. A document arrives about somebody you have never met, and you have to work out what it actually establishes before you can add anything to it.

P. is 61 and spent eight days on a general medical ward after a fall. This is the mental-health content of the discharge summary, quoted in full.

> Low mood noted by nursing staff. Patient reports poor appetite. Slept poorly on the ward. Agitated overnight on day 2, settled with reassurance. Historically had a breakdown in P.'s 30s. Weight on admission 61 kg. Declined food on three occasions (nursing notes). Cognitive screen not performed. Seen by liaison psychiatry day 5: no acute mental health needs.

## Your task

Working only from that paragraph:

1. Write the elements it genuinely supports, each with its source and whatever time course it carries.
2. Name every place where the document withholds the source.
3. Say which of the nine elements you cannot populate at all, and separate "the document says it was absent" from "the document is silent".
4. List, in order, the three things you would ask for first.

Write your answers before reading on.

## What the paragraph supports

Three lines carry a source and can be used as they stand. *Declined food on three occasions* is an other-person report of a countable observation, and it is the strongest item in the document because somebody counted. *Agitated overnight on day 2* is an other-person report of an observation with a date attached, and the document does not say whether it recurred on any other night. *Weight on admission 61 kg* is a measurement, so it is usable as a baseline for the next weighing while establishing nothing at all about change so far, because no earlier weight exists to set it against. A number with nothing behind it is a starting point and not a time course.

Two lines look like findings and are not. *Low mood noted by nursing staff* has a source and no date, no duration, and no description of what was noted; "noted" could cover a single remark or eight days of observation. *Seen by liaison psychiatry day 5: no acute mental health needs* is a conclusion, correctly dated and correctly attributed, which is a different kind of thing from a finding and cannot substitute for one. It tells you what another team decided on day 5 about the question they were asked. It does not tell you what they saw.

One line has had its source removed. *Historically had a breakdown in P.'s 30s* could be P.'s own account, a relative's, or a sentence copied from an old letter, and the three would carry very different weight. "Breakdown" is a lay word covering a wide range of events, and by itself it names nothing. This is the same defect that turned R.'s five days into a history of hypomania, arriving from the other direction: there, a weak source was written strongly; here, the source has simply been deleted.

## What the document cannot tell you

| Element | State |
| --- | --- |
| Depressive (low mood) | Reported by staff, undated, undescribed |
| Elevated, irritable, mixed | Silent |
| Psychotic | Silent |
| Cognitive | Explicitly not assessed, and the document says so |
| Sleep | Poor on the ward; direction and pre-admission pattern silent |
| Energy | Silent |
| Functional | Silent apart from the fall |

Not one row in the document records an established negative. The cognitive row comes closest, because somebody wrote down that the screen was not performed, and recording a gap that way is better practice than most of the paragraph. Every other silence is unreadable. Nobody can tell whether P. was asked about unusual experiences and said no, or was never asked. That distinction is not academic: in one well-characterised sample of people with psychotic depression, 27% of diagnoses were initially wrong, and what the assessing clinicians had missed was the psychosis rather than the mood disorder. A silence in a record is precisely how that happens twice.

The three things to ask for, in order: who said "breakdown", and what happened; what the nursing notes actually describe under "low mood", with dates; and whether anyone has weighed P. before. The first is first because it is the only line that could change the shape of the picture, and because it is the cheapest to establish. Establishing a past period from the patient's account alone is unreliable, so the question runs past what P. says now to who else was there at the time.

## Accessibility and alternatives

Every task in this lesson is text in and text out, and nothing depends on seeing a layout, a colour, or a diagram. The tables restate information that the surrounding prose also states, so a reader using a screen reader loses nothing by reading the prose alone. Answers can be written, dictated, recorded, or worked through aloud with a tutor; the assessment rubric scores the reasoning and does not reward a particular format. If reading a long quoted document is difficult, work through the discharge paragraph one sentence at a time against the three questions in the first section, since each sentence is independently assessable.

Nothing in this lesson requires you to disclose your own health, mood, sleep, or history, and no task asks you to describe a real person.
