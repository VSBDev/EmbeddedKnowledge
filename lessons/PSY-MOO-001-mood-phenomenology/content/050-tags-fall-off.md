# When the tags fall off

Here is R.'s presentation written the way most records are written. It is fluent, it is quick to read, and a competent clinician produced it.

> 34-year-old warehouse worker with a four-month history of low mood, anhedonia, poor sleep, low energy, weight loss and reduced function. History of a hypomanic episode six years ago. Drinking to sleep. Family history of bipolar disorder. Passive suicidal ideation. No psychosis.

:::{check}
:id: check-what-the-paragraph-lost
:kind: prediction

Before reading on, commit to an answer. Name two things in that paragraph that are now stated more strongly than the evidence behind them, and one thing that a clinician in three months' time can no longer check.
:::

## The model that produces it

The paragraph comes from a defensible idea: a description is a piece of clinical writing whose job is to convey the picture, and provenance is bookkeeping that a careful reader can reconstruct or that can be filled in later if it turns out to matter.

That idea is held widely, and it is not stupid. Clinical time is short, and the paragraph does convey a picture. What it cannot do is survive contact with a second reader.

Two of its lines have changed status on the way in.

**"History of a hypomanic episode six years ago."** Nobody assessed that period. The raw material was one person's memory of five days, offered as a good patch. The paragraph has turned a retrospective self-report into a clinical history, and the next clinician has no way to tell, because the sentence that would have told them was the sentence that got trimmed.

**"Family history of bipolar disorder."** R. reported that a parent had "nerves" and took lithium for years, with the indication unknown to R. The paragraph has converted a second-hand memory of a medicine into a diagnosis in a relative.

Two more have lost a qualifier. "Poor sleep" no longer says that R. wakes at about 04:00 and stays awake, so a clinician in October cannot tell whether R.'s sleep improved, worsened, or changed shape. And "passive suicidal ideation" no longer says that R. raised none of it until asked directly, which is the part of that finding a duty clinician at two in the morning would most want to know.

## Why the first of those is the expensive one

There is a real finding underneath R.'s "a good patch", and the paragraph destroys the ability to use it.

A Dutch general-population study re-interviewed 40 adults with a confirmed lifetime bipolar diagnosis and asked what they thought had happened to them. 82.5% recognised having had a depressive episode. 22.5% recognised having had a hypomanic or manic one, and 17.5% had ever consulted a health professional about it. The people who did not recognise those periods explained them as pleasant and productive stretches, as part of their personality, or as the result of circumstances.

Read R.'s wording against that list. "It was a good patch" is close to the first explanation, and that is a reason to take the account seriously and to write it down in full.

The same finding is why the account cannot be promoted. In that study the episodes were established by a structured diagnostic interview, and roughly three in four participants did not recognise the raised-activity periods at all. R. has had no such interview. What exists is the account, and an account is a real piece of evidence: work on retrospectively reported depressive episodes shows that periods people later name as episodes were genuinely worse at the time by contemporaneous measurement, though only about half of those contemporaneous reports reached the measure's clinical threshold. Real signal, soft edges. A record that says "reports about five days" preserves both facts. A record that says "history of hypomania" preserves neither.

## The repair

:::{misconception}
:id: misconception-strength-drift

**The incorrect model.** A description is a fluent summary of the clinical picture; where each part came from is bookkeeping that can be reconstructed by the reader or added later.

**Where it fails.** Provenance cannot be reconstructed downstream. Once "reports about five days six years ago, never assessed" becomes "history of hypomania", every subsequent reader inherits the stronger claim, and nothing in the record signals the loss. The same trimming turns a remembered medicine into a diagnosed relative, and a described sleep pattern into an undirected complaint.

**The better model.** The strength of a written statement is set by its source and its time course, and you write it at that strength. Where the source is thin, the sentence gets longer, and that is the sentence doing its job.

**The same five days, three ways.**

| Version | What it asserts | What a later clinician can do with it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "History of hypomania." | That an episode occurred and was identified as such | Act on a diagnosis nobody made, and never learn that it was not made |
| "No history of mania." | That the question was asked and settled in the negative | Stop asking, on the strength of an established negative that was never established |
| "Reports about five days six years ago of markedly reduced sleep with increased activity, several purchases R. could not afford, and talkativeness noticed by friends at the time. Never assessed. R. describes it as 'a good patch'." | That R. gave this account, with this content, about this period | Seek an informant who was there, ask what the friends noticed, and compare any future period against a described baseline |

**Recheck.** R. also said: *"My mum had nerves. She was on lithium for years. I don't know what for."* Write the version of that line which asserts exactly what is known. Then say what a later clinician can do with your version that they could not do with "family history of bipolar disorder".
:::

A workable answer: *R. reports that a parent had what R. calls "nerves" and took lithium for a number of years; R. does not know the indication.* A later clinician can now ask R.'s family, or request the parent's records with consent, because your line makes visible that there is something still to find out. The shorter version hides the question by appearing to have answered it.

:::{source-note}
:claims: claim-hypomania-self-recognition, claim-retrospective-report-is-evidence
:sources: source-regeer-self-recognition, source-birk-retrospective-validity

The Dutch reappraisal study supports the recognition figures, the consultation figure, and the explanations participants gave for periods they did not recognise. Its sample is 40 adults drawn from one general-population survey, so the percentages describe that sample rather than any clinic population, and the design compares self-recognition against a structured research interview. The Oregon cohort study supports the point that periods later reported as depressive episodes were measurably worse at the time, and that fewer than half of the contemporaneous reports inside those periods reached the measure's clinical cut-off; it studied depressive episodes and does not measure recall of raised activity. R.'s wording and the three versions of the five days are this lesson's own.
:::
