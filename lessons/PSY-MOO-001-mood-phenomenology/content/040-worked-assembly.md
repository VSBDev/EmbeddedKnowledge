# Assembling R.'s description from the raw material

The conversation lasted twenty minutes. Here is everything it produced, in the order it arrived, plus the letter that came with the referral. Read it before the worked example, because the example works on exactly this and nothing else.

## What R. said

| | |
| --- | --- |
| S1 | "It started sometime in the spring. March, maybe April. It's every day now." |
| S2 | "I used to fish every weekend. I haven't been since. I don't want to." |
| S3 | "I wake at four and that's it. I lie there till the alarm." |
| S4 | "Most nights. Every night I'm not on a late." |
| S5 | "I'm tired in a way that sleeping doesn't fix." |
| S6 | "I've lost weight. Maybe six kilos. I'm not trying to." |
| S7 | "I'm cold all the time. And I'm blocked up, if that matters." |
| S8 | "Work's fine." Then, a few minutes later: "I've had two write-ups for picking errors. That's never happened to me." |
| S9 | "I stopped answering my friends. It's easier." |
| S10 | "I have a drink most evenings. More the last couple of months. It's the only thing that puts me under." |
| S11 | "Six years back there were about five days where I hardly slept and I was flying. I redid the kitchen in a weekend. I bought a bike I couldn't afford. My mates said I wouldn't shut up. It was a good patch." |
| S12 | "My mum had nerves. She was on lithium for years. I don't know what for." |
| S13 | Asked directly: "Sometimes I think it wouldn't matter if I didn't wake up. I wouldn't do anything about it. I never have." |
| S14 | Asked directly about unusual experiences and beliefs: R. described none. |
| S15 | Asked directly about irritability, and about any days in the four months when the mood lifted: R. described none. |

## What was observed in the room

| | |
| --- | --- |
| O1 | Pauses of about five seconds before most answers. |
| O2 | Speech quiet and slowed; answers short; no spontaneous conversation. |
| O3 | Little change in facial expression across the twenty minutes, including while describing the bike. |
| O4 | Sat still throughout, with little spontaneous movement. |
| O5 | Asked for a question to be repeated twice, and lost the thread mid-answer once. |
| O6 | Kept a coat on throughout, in a warm room. |
| O7 | No tears. |

## What the referral letter says

| | |
| --- | --- |
| L1 | R. attended the practice in March and again in July. |
| L2 | Weight 78 kg in March, 72 kg in July. |
| L3 | The July entry records that R. reported waking early. The March entry records nothing about mood or sleep. |
| L4 | No thyroid function test has been requested or performed. |
| L5 | The letter says nothing about any period six years ago. |

:::{worked-example}
:id: worked-example-assemble-r

**Task.** Replace the referral's one-line summary with a description in which every element states what changed, over what period, and where it came from, and in which the negatives that were established are visible alongside the gaps that were not.

**What you have and what you do not.** Twenty-seven raw items across three sources. Missing: the onset of the fatigue, whether the loss of interest began with the low mood or after it, any account from a person who has watched R. across the four months, and a thyroid result.

**Principle selected.** Every element carries what changed, its time course, and its source, and inference is kept off the finding lines. It fits here because the raw material arrives in three provenances that fail in different ways, and a summary that merges them cannot be checked by anyone downstream.

**Plan.** Sort by source, group by element, write each element with its three tags, write the established negatives and the gaps, then test whether a clinician reading it in three months could tell what had moved.

**Execution, and the decisions that shape it.**

*Sleep (S3, S4, L3).* Direction, pattern, and a dated trace: waking at about 04:00 without returning to sleep, most nights other than nights worked late, since around March on R.'s account, with the practice's July entry recording R. making the same report to the general practitioner. That last clause is worth writing carefully. It establishes that R. was saying this in July, and it is not a second observer confirming it, because the practice recorded R.'s report and not anybody's observation. A record that says "corroborated by the GP" would have upgraded the same source twice. The shift pattern belongs in the line because a sleep description that ignores rotating nights describes the wrong week.

*Energy (S5).* Fatigue that sleep does not relieve, **frequency and onset not established**, R.'s report. The temptation is to write "most days, for four months" and borrow both numbers from the sleep element. Nobody asked, so nobody knows, and the honest line is shorter and more useful than the invented one.

*Weight (S6, L2).* Two versions of the same fact, and the description keeps both. R. estimates about six kilograms without trying. The practice recorded 78 kg in March and 72 kg in July. Only the second version can be compared against a number taken in October, so it is the one that carries the measurement and R.'s account is what tells you it was unintentional.

*Function (S8, S9).* R. says work is fine and also reports two written warnings for picking errors, the first in R.'s account of that job, plus stopping contact with friends. Both halves of S8 go in, unreconciled. Deciding that "work's fine" is minimisation is an inference, and writing it as a finding would delete the only quotation that shows how R. currently talks about work.

*Mood and its expression (S1, S2, O3).* R. describes daily low mood since about March, and loss of interest and pleasure in fishing dated later than the mood, though how much later was not established. Separately, and this is an observation and not a report, facial expression changed little across the twenty minutes, including while R. described the bike. Those are two different elements. Psychiatry keeps two words for them: **mood** is the sustained state the person reports from the inside, and **affect** is the moment-to-moment expression an observer watches, including its range and how it moves. The mental-status outcome earlier in this track owns that distinction, and this lesson uses it in exactly that sense. Keeping them on separate lines is what lets a later reader see when one moves without the other.

*Cognition (O5).* Asked for a question to be repeated twice and lost the thread once, across twenty minutes, observed. R. was not asked about concentration at home, so the element rests on an observation of a short sample and says so.

*Psychomotor (O1, O2, O4).* Long response latency, quiet slowed speech, little spontaneous movement, observed across twenty minutes. This is where the observation row earns its place: none of it came from R., and R. might not have reported any of it.

*Physical (S7, L4).* R. reports feeling cold constantly and being constipated. The letter records that no thyroid function test has been requested. The second sentence is a gap in the record, written as a gap. What to make of the pairing is not this lesson's question and guessing at it here would put an inference into a description.

*Alcohol (S10).* R.'s account: most evenings, increased over about two months, and R.'s stated reason is to get to sleep. Put the two dates side by side and stop. The sleep change is dated to around March and the increase in drinking to around May, so the drinking followed the sleep change in time. Writing which produced which is a causal claim, and a description that makes it has stopped describing.

*The five days, six years ago (S11).* About five days, hardly sleeping, high activity, a kitchen redecorated in a weekend, a bike bought that R. could not afford, and unusual talkativeness as reported to R. by friends at the time. Never assessed by anyone. R.'s framing, "a good patch", stays in the record as R.'s words, because how a person holds a past period is itself information. What does not go in the record is a diagnostic name for it.

*Family (S12).* R. reports that a parent had "nerves" and took lithium for years, and that R. does not know what it was for. Recorded exactly at that strength: R.'s second-hand account of a medicine, with the indication unknown to R.

*Risk (S13).* Passive thoughts that life would not be missed, no plan, no intent, no act, and R. raised none of it until asked directly. That last clause is part of the finding.

*Asked and denied (S14, S15).* R. described no hallucinations and no beliefs that others found strange, no irritability, and no days within the four months when the mood lifted. Three established negatives, written as findings, so the next reader knows which questions were put. The last of them also settles the mixed element, which cannot be described until somebody asks whether the mood ever went the other way.

**Assumptions and limits.** Everything from R. comes from one conversation on one day. The observations sample twenty minutes and are silent about the other twenty-three hours. The two weights were taken on practice scales at visits four months apart.

**Independent check.** Take each line and ask: who would have to be asked to confirm this? If a line has no answer, its source tag is missing. Run it and the description passes, with one instructive result: for seven of the nine elements the answer is "R.", which is a true statement about how thin the evidence is and a reason to seek a second account.

**Interpretation.** The description now supports three things it could not support before. There is a four-month picture with dated elements. There is a documented six-kilogram fall between two weighings. There is an unassessed five-day period six years ago, described at the strength of one person's memory. It supports none of the following: which element produced which, any classification of either period, and the referral's "?bipolar", which entered the record as somebody's conclusion and leaves it again because nothing in the raw material establishes it.

**Self-explanation.** Of the decisions above, which one most changes what a clinician in three months' time is able to do with this record?
:::

A good answer picks the weight decision or the energy decision, and for the same reason. Keeping the practice's two weighings gives a later reader a number to compare against. Refusing to invent an onset for the fatigue leaves a hole that can still be filled by asking. Both decisions are about whether the record can be used again later, which is what separates a description from an impression.

:::{check}
:id: check-what-changed
:kind: application

One line of the finished description reads:

> Waking at about 04:00 without returning to sleep, most nights other than nights worked late, since around March by R.'s account, and recorded independently by the practice in July.

Rewrite it as the referral would have: "poor sleep". Then name three specific questions that a clinician in October can answer from the first version and cannot answer from the second.
:::

The first version answers: has the waking time moved, has the frequency moved, and is R. now getting back to sleep. The second version answers none of them, because it never recorded a direction, a rate, or a baseline. That is the entire practical difference between the two records, and it arrives three months after anyone stopped thinking about the wording.

:::{source-note}
:claims: claim-depressive-episode-definition, claim-retrospective-report-is-evidence
:sources: source-carty-escalona-mdd, source-birk-retrospective-validity

The primary-care review supports the depressive elements the assembly asks about, including the direction of the sleep and weight changes and the two-week duration under DSM-5. The study of retrospectively reported depressive episodes supports treating R.'s account of six years ago as evidence with soft edges: among 68 participants in a long-running Oregon cohort, symptom scores collected at the time were higher during periods later reported as episodes than between them by a medium-to-large margin, and 49.1% of those contemporaneous within-episode reports reached the scale's clinical cut-off. That study examined depressive episodes in one United States cohort, so it supports the general point that a remembered episode carries real signal and imprecise boundaries; it does not measure how accurately anyone recalls a period of raised activity. R., the transcript, the observations, and the letter are inventions of this lesson.
:::
