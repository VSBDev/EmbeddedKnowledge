# A line that nobody can check

A referral reaches the service the day before the appointment. Its clinical summary is one sentence:

> 34-year-old, four-month history of depression. Poor sleep, weight loss, reduced function. ?bipolar.

Nothing there is obviously wrong, and a colleague could read it in two seconds and picture the person. As evidence it is close to empty. Ask one question of each part: how would anybody check this?

- **Four-month history.** Four months since what? Since the mood dropped, since the sleep changed, or since someone first said the word out loud?
- **Poor sleep.** Poor in which direction? Trouble falling asleep, waking through the night, waking early and staying awake, or sleeping far more than usual?
- **Weight loss.** Weighed, or estimated?
- **Reduced function.** Reduced on whose account, measured against what this person was doing before?
- **?bipolar.** No part of that is a description. Somebody's conclusion has been written into the space where the evidence belongs.

In an hour you will meet R., who is 34 and works rotating night shifts in a warehouse. At the end of the conversation you have to produce something better than that sentence. Better does not mean longer. It means that a clinician who was not in the room can tell, for every single element, what changed, over what period, and where the information came from.

## One word to settle before it causes trouble

Depression is two words sharing a spelling, and the two get swapped without anyone noticing.

In ordinary speech it names a low mood: *I was depressed about the interview.* Usually about something, usually passing, and no one means anything technical by it. In clinical use, a major depressive episode names a syndrome. Depressed mood or loss of interest and pleasure has to be present most days for at least two weeks, carrying several further changes alongside it: energy, sleep, concentration, guilt or a sense of worthlessness, movement, weight, thoughts of death. Function suffers across work, home life, and self-care.

The referral used the second sense on evidence that would only support the first, and that happens constantly. This lesson writes **low mood** when it means the state and spells the syndrome out in full when it means the syndrome. Do the same in anything you write, and the ambiguity stops travelling.

## What you are bringing with you

:::{check}
:id: check-source-sorting
:kind: retrieval

Earlier in this track you separated what a person tells you, what someone else tells you, what you see for yourself, and what you conclude. Retrieve it now, before you need it under time pressure. Sort these five fragments into those four kinds:

1. "I wake at four and that's it."
2. R. paused for about five seconds before most answers.
3. The referring practice recorded 78 kg in March and 72 kg in July.
4. R. is withdrawn.
5. "My mates said I wouldn't shut up."

Write your four groups down before reading on.
:::

One and five are R.'s own report, and they are not the same kind of report: the first is R. describing R.'s experience, the fifth is R. reporting what other people said six years ago. Two is your observation. Three is another person's record, and it is the only one of the five with a number that was taken by an instrument. Four is an inference. It is a reasonable one, and it belongs somewhere in your thinking, but it is not a finding and it cannot be checked by anyone who was not there.

That sorting is the whole method of this block. This lesson is where you use it on a full presentation and find out how much work it does.

## Recovery route

Two earlier outcomes in this track carry the ideas above: separating a person's report, another person's report, direct observation, and inference; and describing a time-bound mental-status examination without collapsing it into shorthand. If your groups came out differently from the four above, you have not failed anything. Reread your own answers with this rule in hand: a finding is something a second clinician could have written down in the room, and a conclusion is something you added afterwards. That single rule recovers most of what those outcomes teach, and it is enough to work through this lesson.

Nothing here asks you to disclose anything about yourself. Every case is invented, and you can write your answers, dictate them, or talk them through with a tutor.
