# Nine kinds of change, one person

The outcome this lesson serves names nine kinds of change: depressive, elevated, irritable, mixed, psychotic, cognitive, sleep, energy, and functional. R. hands you some of them across the table, hands you one from memory, and leaves the rest for you to establish or rule out.

Learning the nine as a list is easy and close to useless. What matters is knowing, for each one, what you would be looking at, and what you write when it is not there. The full record of R.'s conversation arrives in the next scene; here it appears in fragments.

## The ones R. gives you directly

Low mood is the element R. came about, so it arrives first and it arrives vaguely: *"It started sometime in the spring. March, maybe April."* Loss of interest and pleasure arrives second, and R. dates it later than the mood: *"I used to fish every weekend. I haven't been since. I don't want to."* Those two carry the weight, because the clinical syndrome needs at least one of them present most days across at least two weeks before anything else counts toward it.

The rest of the depressive picture travels with them and is asked about one element at a time: sleep, appetite and weight, energy, concentration, guilt or a sense of worthlessness, movement, and thoughts of death. R. describes changes in sleep, weight, and energy, and describes thoughts of death only when asked directly. R. says nothing about guilt. The change in movement is something you watch rather than something R. mentions, which already tells you that these elements do not all arrive by the same route.

Two of them are worth separating out because they behave differently from the rest.

**Energy is not sleep.** R. is tired in a way that sleeping does not fix, and R. is also waking at four. Those are two elements with two time courses, and folding them into "fatigue" hides the fact that one of them might move without the other.

**Cognitive change is not just a complaint.** R. asked twice in twenty minutes for a question to be repeated, and lost the thread of an answer once. Depressive episodes carry measurable change in attention, in memory and learning, in executive function, and in the speed of mental and physical processing, and clinical practice guidelines published in the *Indian Journal of Psychiatry* in 2025 record that residual deficits persist after a depressive episode resolves, with a documented effect on occupational and social functioning. So the concentration element needs its own line in the description and its own time course. It is one of the elements most likely to still be there at the next appointment when the mood has lifted.

**Function is what the other elements did to the week.** R. is still going to work. R. has had two write-ups for picking errors. R. has stopped answering friends. Function is a set of specific things that used to happen and now happen differently, each carrying its own date. A single global rating replaces all of that with one number that nobody can take apart again.

## The one R. gives you from memory

Six years ago, by R.'s account, there were about five days of hardly sleeping, of doing a great deal, of unusual talkativeness, and of several purchases R. could not afford. Nobody assessed it. R. calls it a good patch.

What is being described there has a shape. A 2025 seminar on bipolar disorder sets it out as a distinct period of elevated, expansive, or irritable mood together with increased activity or energy, carrying some combination of reduced need for sleep, pressured or increased speech, racing ideas, distractibility, grandiosity, impulsivity, increased goal-directed activity, and involvement in things with a high potential for painful consequences. The conventional duration thresholds are at least seven days for mania, or less if it led to hospital admission, and at least four days for hypomania. Psychotic features occur in mania and are not part of hypomania.

Notice the two directions that period can take. Elevated and irritable are alternatives on the same element, and an episode of raised activity can present as either. R. describes the elevated version. Irritability on its own, with nothing else changed, tells you very little, because it accompanies a great many states; it counts for much more when it arrives with the raised activity, the shortened sleep, and a start date.

Whether R.'s five days clear any threshold, and what it would mean if they did, is the next lesson's question. What this lesson owes that episode is an accurate count of the days, a description of what actually happened during them, and an honest label saying that all of it comes from one person's memory of six years ago.

## The two that change what kind of picture this is

**Mixed features** means depressive and elevated features running inside the same episode. A multicentre Korean chart review of 672 non-psychotic mood episodes found that how often this gets recorded depends heavily on which criteria are applied. On the same 672 episodes, 5.8% met the DSM-5 mixed-features specifier and 31.7% met broader research criteria, a fivefold gap produced by the definition and not by the patients. Among the broader cases, irritable mood was the commonest elevated feature, at 90.8%. That study also found that patients with depression and mixed characteristics were frequently classified as having anxious distress or a personality disorder instead. The bipolar seminar adds that anxiety, agitation, and irritability during mixed states both drive misdiagnosis and raise the risk of suicide, which is why a describer is expected to look for these features instead of waiting for them to announce themselves.

R. was asked about irritability and about any days in the four months when the mood lifted, and described none, and no agitation was observed. Those are findings, and they are worth a line each.

**Psychotic features** means delusions or hallucinations occurring within the mood episode. A review of psychotic depression records that the delusional content is commonly nihilistic, carrying the belief that something bad is about to happen, and that the rate is not small: in the United States Epidemiologic Catchment Area study, 14.7% of people meeting criteria for major depression had a history of psychotic features, a European sample put it at 18.5%, and about a quarter of hospitalised depressed patients met criteria for the psychotic form. Psychotic features are described as mood-congruent when their content fits the mood state and mood-incongruent when it does not, and current classification gives precedence to the incongruent features when both are present.

The reason to ask R. directly sits in the same review. Among 130 diagnoses in a well-characterised sample of people with psychotic depression, 27% were initially wrong, and what the assessing clinicians had missed was the psychosis and not the mood disorder. R. was asked and described nothing of the kind. That sentence goes in the record.

## The nine, and what R.'s description can honestly say about each

| Element | Status for R. | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Depressive (low mood) | Present, most days, since about March | R.'s report |
| Elevated | Reported for about five days, six years ago | R.'s memory, never assessed |
| Irritable | Asked about the four months and denied | R.'s report |
| Mixed | No days of the mood lifting in the four months; no agitation seen | R.'s report, and observation |
| Psychotic | Asked and denied | R.'s report |
| Cognitive | Two requests to repeat a question and one lost thread, in twenty minutes | Observed |
| Sleep | Waking about 04:00, not returning to sleep, most nights other than late shifts | R.'s report |
| Energy | Fatigue not relieved by sleep; frequency and onset not established | R.'s report |
| Functional | Still at work; two picking errors written up; stopped seeing friends | R.'s report |

Seven of the nine rest on R.'s account alone. A description that says so is telling you exactly where it is thin, which is the reason the next lesson wants a second account.
