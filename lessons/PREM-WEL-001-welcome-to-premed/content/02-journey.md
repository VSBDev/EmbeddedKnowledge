# How the subjects prepare you for what follows

The journey has a rough order. Some subjects run alongside each other rather than one after another. Each stage gives you ideas that the next one needs.

1. **Begin with scientific inquiry, laboratory practice, mathematics, and statistics.** You learn to frame a question and compare fairly. You measure with units, read graphs, describe variation, and keep an honest record. The same habits later let you test a chemical prediction, read a genetics result, or challenge a claim about a population.
2. **Study general chemistry and introductory biology side by side.** Chemistry explains particles, bonds, reactions, solutions, and energy. Biology introduces molecules, membranes, cells, transport, and how a cell is organised. Put the two together and you can link a biological outcome to the interactions that might produce it.
3. **Add physics while chemistry and biology deepen.** Forces, energy, pressure, flow, electricity, waves, and light make measurements and body processes easier to model. Equilibrium, acids and bases, inheritance, and gene expression add control and continuity. Together they let you ask whether an explanation is possible at all, chemically, physically, and biologically.
4. **Use organic chemistry to enter biochemistry.** Carbon-based structures, functional groups, three-dimensional shape, and reaction patterns prepare you for proteins, membranes, nucleic acids, and metabolism. Biochemistry then shows how those molecules work together in a cell that controls them, and how evidence can test that account.
5. **Carry those foundations into human structure, physiology, microbiology, and immunology.** Tissues and organs depend on transport, energy, signalling, and control. Infection and defence depend on cells, molecules, evolution, and change across populations. The earlier ideas now do real work on the whole body.
6. **Broaden the account with behaviour, society, and population health.** People live in social and physical settings. Later medical study therefore needs evidence about behaviour, institutions, access, inequality, and populations. This is what lets you tell three things apart: a molecular mechanism, one person's experience, and a pattern across a population.
7. **Finish with integration and capstone work.** A new problem does not announce which subject to use. You have to choose the ideas that matter, connect them through cause or evidence, look hard at the limits, and write a conclusion someone else can check.

The pace can vary, and later lessons will revisit earlier ideas.
