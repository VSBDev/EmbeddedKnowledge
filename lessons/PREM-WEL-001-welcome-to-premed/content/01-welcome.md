# Welcome to Premed

Welcome. Nobody expects you to begin at the end. Curiosity, careful practice, and a willingness to correct your own reasoning are enough to start.

You do not need to know any of these subjects yet. That is what the course is for. This first lesson shows you what you will learn. It shows why each part earns its place, and what you will be able to do once the parts connect. The course starts with careful questions. It moves through matter and living systems. It ends with you using evidence on problems you have not seen before.

Here is what a finished explanation looks like. Take a simple model in which a wider tube carries more liquid than a narrow one. Three subjects each say something different about it. Evidence tests whether the pattern really holds. Physics explains how width affects flow. Physiology asks how living tissue, which can change its own width, alters the picture. None of the three is the whole answer. What turns separate facts into an explanation is knowing what each subject adds, and knowing what each one leaves out.

The route is easier to hold in three stages: **evidence and measurement; matter and living systems; people, populations, and capstone work**. Those three stages are the thing to remember from this lesson. The subject list below is not a vocabulary test. Where a word is new to you, note which subject it belongs to, and note what that subject will later help you explain. You will practise that move near the end.

Here is the journey ahead.

- **Scientific method and laboratory practice.** Ask testable questions. Choose variables and fair comparisons. Measure carefully, keep records that others can repeat, and decide what a result actually supports. These habits keep evidence open to inspection and to challenge.
- **Mathematics and statistics.** Use units, ratios, graphs, probability, averages, variation, and uncertainty ranges. They let you read research, compare groups, check your own answers, and tell a numerical pattern apart from a justified conclusion.
- **Physics.** Forces and motion. Pressure, fluids, and transport. Electricity. Waves, sound, and light. Heat and radiation. These explain movement, blood flow, electrical signals, hearing, vision, measurement, and imaging.
- **General chemistry.** Atoms, bonds, molecular shape, reactions, amounts, gases, solutions, energy changes, reaction rates, equilibrium, acids, and bases. This is the molecular setting that cells work in. It is also the arithmetic behind laboratory, biology, and biochemistry work.
- **Organic chemistry.** How molecular shape, recurring groups, electron movement, and three-dimensional arrangement affect carbon-based molecules. How separation methods and instrument patterns reveal a structure. This prepares you to reason about why the molecules of life bind and change.
- **Biochemistry.** Proteins and enzymes, membranes, carbohydrates, fats, nucleotides, metabolism, and biological information. Together these explain how cells use energy, regulate reactions, communicate, and respond to molecular change.
- **Biology and genetics.** Cell organisation, membranes, division, signalling, inheritance, gene expression, variation, and evolution. These explain how living systems preserve information, develop different functions, and vary between individuals and between populations.
- **Human structure and physiology.** How tissues and organs carry out transport, internal control, nerves, hormones, movement, circulation, breathing, fluid balance, digestion, reproduction, and development. The aim is to explain whole-body mechanisms. It is not to make clinical decisions.
- **Microbiology and immunology.** Microbes and viruses, and how they grow, spread, and evolve. Barriers, fast general defences, and slower targeted responses. Together these explain infection and immunity across an agent, a host, an environment, and time.
- **Behaviour, society, and population health.** Perception, learning, stress, relationships, institutions, inequality, access, environment, and population evidence. These show why biology alone cannot account for every pattern, and why a group average is not one person's experience.
- **Integration and capstone work.** Bring subjects together on an unfamiliar problem that is not a diagnosis. Inspect sources and data, calculate or model, explain the mechanism, say how certain you are, and revise after criticism.

The destination is demanding, and it is concrete. By the time you finish, you should be able to do four things.

**Explain** important scientific relationships. **Model** them in words, quantities, diagrams, or data. **Investigate** questions with honest methods and honest records. And **integrate** several subjects when one on its own is not enough.
