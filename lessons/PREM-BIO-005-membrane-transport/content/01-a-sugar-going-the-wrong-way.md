# A sugar going the wrong way

An absorptive cell near the top of a colonic crypt spends its short working life pulling water and salt out of what is left of a meal. That is what the colon is for: it forms stool from material the small intestine has finished with, by slowly absorbing the water and electrolytes still in it. Reclaiming water is the whole job, and this lesson is about how a cell does that kind of work at all.

## One segment upstream, and back again

The molecule that makes the mechanism visible is glucose, and glucose is mostly gone by the time anything reaches the colon. Sugar uptake is studied one segment upstream, in the small intestine, on a cell built to the same plan as the colonic one: the same columnar shape, the same brush border facing the lumen, the same crypt architecture underneath. So this scene and the four after it work on the small-intestinal absorptive cell, which is where the measurements exist, and the final scene comes back to why any of it matters for fluid. Where a number below belongs to a particular segment or species, the text says so.

Give that small-intestinal cell a meal. Sugar arrives in the lumen, the cell takes it up, and the luminal concentration falls as it does. Keep going and you reach a situation that ought to be impossible: there is more glucose inside the cell than in the fluid outside it, and uptake carries on.

Nothing drifts uphill. If glucose is moving from the dilute side to the concentrated side, something is paying for it. Finding out what, and how much it has to spend, is the first job here.

## What the membrane will and will not do on its own

The previous lesson left you a bilayer two lipid molecules thick, about 5 nm across, liquid in its own plane and effectively solid across it, with a composition that differs between a cell's two faces. It also left one conclusion that matters now: composition sets how tight the barrier is before any protein is involved, and cholesterol among the hydrocarbon chains lowers permeability to small water-soluble molecules. That is [Membrane structure and fluidity (PREM-BIO-004)](https://embeddedknowledge.io/premed/lessons/read/?lesson=PREM-BIO-004). This lesson starts where it stopped.

A bare bilayer is not equally unhelpful to everything. Sort molecules by how readily they cross one unaided and an order appears.

| What is crossing | How it fares in a bare bilayer |
| --- | --- |
| Small nonpolar molecules, oxygen and carbon dioxide | Dissolve in the lipid and cross rapidly |
| Small uncharged polar molecules, water and urea | Cross, but much more slowly |
| Ions, however small | Essentially blocked |

The last row carries a number worth keeping. A synthetic bilayer is about $10^{9}$ times more permeable to water than to an ion as small as sodium or potassium. A billion-fold preference is not mild reluctance. An ion facing a bare membrane is facing a wall, and being small does not help it.

Both molecules this lesson follows sit on the wrong side of that table. Sodium is an ion, so the bilayer refuses it. Glucose is a fair-sized uncharged polar molecule, bigger than water and carrying five hydroxyl groups, so the bilayer is slow with it at best. Neither crosses at a useful rate without help, and one of them has to go uphill as well.

## Two words worth pinning down

**Transport** in ordinary speech means moving goods about, in a vehicle that travels. Almost none of that survives here. In this lesson transport means one solute getting from one side of one membrane to the other, and the thing that moves it is a protein sitting in the membrane, going nowhere. [Organelles and compartmentalisation (PREM-BIO-003)](https://embeddedknowledge.io/premed/lessons/read/?lesson=PREM-BIO-003) uses the word in the other cellular sense, a transport vesicle carrying cargo between compartments, and those are different processes. A vesicle is a container that travels. A transporter is a doorway that stays put.

**Uphill** and **downhill** get used constantly below. Downhill means a solute moving the way it would go on its own. Uphill means the other way, and uphill always has a bill attached.

## The two questions

Everything ahead answers one of two questions.

The first is bookkeeping. When glucose goes uphill, who pays, and how much is there to spend? That is arithmetic you can do, and the third scene does it.

The second is classification, and it is where most of the trouble in this topic lives. Several arrangements move solutes across membranes, convention sorts them into four kinds, and the sorting is worth less than the test that produces it. Someone who has memorised four names still cannot say which one applies to a protein they have not met before. Someone holding the test can.
