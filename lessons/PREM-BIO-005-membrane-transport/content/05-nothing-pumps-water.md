# Nothing pumps water

Everything so far has been about solutes. The reason any of it matters clinically is water, and water works differently enough to need its own scene.

## There is no water pump

Search a cell for a protein that grabs water molecules and pushes them across a membrane against their own gradient and you will not find one. The expert view on this has been close to unanimous for a long time: water movement across epithelia is passive. What moves water is a difference in **effective osmolality** across the membrane, and that is the whole driving force. Osmolality is a count of dissolved particles per unit of water: the side with more dissolved particles has proportionally less free water, and water moves towards it. Effective means counting only the particles that cannot themselves cross, since a solute free to follow the water sets up no lasting difference.

So water cannot be moved directly. It can only be moved by arranging for solute to be somewhere, at which point water follows. Every mechanism in this lesson for shifting sodium and glucose is therefore also, at one remove, a mechanism for shifting water. Absorb solute across an epithelium and water comes after it, because the solute has changed the osmolality on the far side.

That ordering is the point, and it is worth stating in the direction the biology runs: solute first, water second. A cell that has lost the ability to absorb solute has lost the ability to absorb water, whatever solution that water arrives in.

Because water is not absorbed on its own account. Water crosses in response to an osmolality difference, and pure water in the lumen makes that difference point the wrong way, or at least does nothing to create the inward one that drives absorption. Absorbing solute is what creates an inward osmotic gradient, and the water goes where the solute went. Drinking water replaces the volume in the gut without supplying the thing that gets volume across the wall of it.

## Which way water actually crosses is not settled

The passive part is solid. The route is not, and this is a good place to see what an open question in cell biology looks like from inside.

Three candidate paths are currently discussed.

**Through the cells, via aquaporins.** Aquaporins are channel proteins that let water through a membrane along an osmotic gradient. This route has been established for decades and aquaporins have been described in a wide range of tissues.

**Through the cells, carried along by a cotransporter.** SGLT1 and several other carriers are held to contribute to water movement directly, which in the small intestine is reported as significant. The strong version of this idea, that a cotransporter can carry water even against an osmotic gradient, is actively disputed between research groups. Whether cotransporters carry water at all, and how much, remains a matter of debate.

**Between the cells, through the tight junctions.** A paracellular water route was argued over for many years until two molecular components of it were identified, the proteins claudin-2 and claudin-15. That identification moved the question from whether the route exists to how much it carries.

How the total water flux divides between those three has been hard to settle, largely because direct measurements of the split are difficult to make. The honest summary is that water follows solute osmotically, that this is not in doubt, and that the plumbing is.

Notice which parts of this lesson depend on the unsettled bit. Nothing does. The clinical argument in the next scene needs only that water follows absorbed solute, and that claim sits on the settled side. A lesson that told you the route was known would be adding false confidence to an argument that does not need it.

## Back to the colon

The colonic absorptive cell this module keeps returning to absorbs the water and electrolytes left in material the small intestine has finished with. It does that the same way: solute across the epithelium, water after it. The colon is the last chance to reclaim fluid, and a colon receiving far more fluid than usual, or an epithelium whose solute transport has been disturbed, is a colon that cannot keep up. That is the situation the last scene describes.
