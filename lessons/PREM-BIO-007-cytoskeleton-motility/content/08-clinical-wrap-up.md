# Clinical wrap-up: which attachment does a leak implicate?

:::{callout}
:kind: boundary

This case is a teaching example, not medical advice. It carries no diagnosis, no dose, no regimen and no recommendation, and nothing in it should be used to interpret a real person's results. Its purpose is to show what this lesson's model does and does not license a reader to conclude.
:::

Here is a question a clinician actually faces. A colonic epithelium is behaving as though material is crossing it that should not be. Something in the lining has given way. Which of the four attachments does that finding point at, and how confidently?

The lesson answers the first half sharply and the second half with care.

## The reasoning the model supports

Of the three cell-to-cell attachments, only the tight junction seals the space between neighbouring cells. At the tight junction the intercellular space disappears entirely. At the adherens junction and the desmosome the neighbouring membranes stay 15 to 20 nanometres apart, and both of those attachments hold cells together without closing the gap. The tight junction is the rate-limiting step for anything travelling between cells instead of through them.

So a leak along the route between cells points at the tight junction, and specifically at its claudins, occludin and JAM-A and the ZO scaffolds tying them to actin. It does not point at adhesion in general, because adhesion and sealing are different jobs done by different structures.

That is a prediction, and this lesson has already seen it tested from the other direction. In the desmoplakin experiment the desmosome lost its keratin anchor, adhesion between cells was left intact, and the barrier held: a biotin tracer did not leak along the lateral membranes, and the authors state that desmoplakin is not required for the barrier function of the intestinal epithelium. Breaking a non-sealing attachment did not produce a leak. The division of labour is real, and it survived a direct test.

## What the clinical literature adds, and how firmly

Changes in tight junction proteins are reported in several intestinal conditions. Decreased expression and redistribution of claudins 3, 5 and 8 have been described in the intestine of people with Crohn's disease; decreased expression and redistribution of claudins 1 and 4 in ulcerative colitis; decreased ZO-1 in coeliac disease; and decreased claudin 1 in small-intestinal biopsy specimens from people with food allergy after exposure to the relevant allergen.

Read the verbs. Described, reported, observed. These are associations between a disease state and a protein finding, and the review reporting them does not establish whether the junction change causes the disease or results from it. That distinction is not a technicality. If the junction change is a consequence of inflammation, then targeting the junction treats a symptom; if it is a cause, the same intervention treats the disease. The same association supports both stories.

This is the discipline PREM-SCI-007 set out, arriving in a place where it has consequences. The lesson's structural model tells you which component a leak implicates. It does not tell you the direction of causation in a disease, and no amount of confidence about claudins supplies it.

## Working the case

:::{worked-example}
:id: worked-example-barrier-reasoning

**The finding.** In a research setting, colonic tissue from a person with active colitis shows reduced claudin expression and increased movement of a tracer between cells, compared with tissue from people without colitis.

**Step 1: which structure does the model implicate?** The tight junction. It is the only one of the three cell-to-cell attachments that closes the space between cells, and claudins are among its transmembrane proteins. The finding and the model agree.

**Step 2: what is the mechanism of the leak, in structural terms?** Reduced claudin means fewer of the proteins that form the selective pores and seal the space. A paracellular route that was closed is less closed. This part is a structural inference from the composition of the junction and is well supported.

**Step 2a: is the tight junction the only structure that could produce this tracer result?** No, and the model is only entitled to the weaker claim. In an intact epithelium with no gross damage, the tight junction is the rate-limiting step for the route between cells, which is what makes it the first structure to implicate. Inflamed tissue is not that epithelium. A tracer can also cross where cells have been extruded and left a gap, where adhesion has failed enough to open the lateral space, or through changes one junction imposes on another, since these junctions are not independent. So the finding is consistent with a claudin-mediated leak and does not establish that all of the tracer movement is one.

**Step 3: what caused the claudin reduction?** Unknown from this finding. Inflammation in colitis could reduce claudin expression, or reduced claudin could permit luminal material through and drive inflammation, or a third factor could produce both. The finding is a snapshot of two things that co-occur.

**Step 4: what would distinguish them?** Something that establishes order or intervention. A measurement showing the junction change preceding the inflammation, or an intervention that alters only the junction and then changes disease activity. Neither is supplied by an association in tissue sampled once.

**Step 5: what may be concluded?** That the barrier defect is located at the tight junction rather than at the adhesive attachments, which is genuinely useful and follows from the structural model. Not that fixing claudins would treat the colitis.

**Step 6: what the model makes less likely, and what it cannot exclude.** A hypothesis that the leak arises from failed desmosomal adhesion *alone* is a poor fit, because desmosomes do not seal the paracellular space and because removing the desmosome's keratin anchor left the barrier intact in the deletion experiment.

Say exactly how much that buys. It is one deletion, of one linker, in mouse tissue under ordinary conditions and without imposed mechanical stress. It shows that losing keratin's attachment does not by itself break the barrier. It does not show that no desmosomal or adherens-junction failure can contribute to barrier loss in inflamed tissue, and the same review that supplies the junction composition also describes cross-talk between these junctions. So the map tells you which explanation to reach for first and which to demote. Ruling one out would need it tested in the tissue and the condition in question, which this lesson has not done.
:::

## Where the block goes next

Three questions lesson 04 handed forward have now been answered. A microvillus is held up by a bundle of about nineteen actin filaments, guyed to its membrane by myosin cross-bridges and planted in a spectrin-containing terminal web. A junction is a specific set of proteins reaching across to a neighbour and gripping a specific polymer on the inside. And the sealing job, the adhesive job and the load-bearing job belong to different attachments, which is why they fail separately.

One question has been answered honestly rather than fully. What drives the climb is still contested, and the lesson said so.

Lesson 08 takes up the division at the crypt base that supplies every cell making this journey, which is where the proliferation half of that argument gets its machinery. Lesson 10 returns to the end of the journey, where cells are shed, and to what happens when the climb stops ending.
