# A finger of fluid, standing up

Lesson 04 left a puzzle on the table and named this lesson as the place to solve it.

The apical face of a colonic absorptive cell is thrown into thousands of fine projections. Each one is about a micrometre long and about a hundred nanometres across, and together they raise the cell's apical surface area roughly thirtyfold. That much you already have. Here is what lesson 04 could not explain with membranes alone.

The membrane wrapped around one of those projections is two lipid molecules thick, about 5 nanometres, and it behaves as a liquid in its own plane. A lipid in it swaps places with a neighbour millions of times a second. Now try to build a standing cylinder out of that material, twenty times taller than it is wide, and keep it standing while digestive enzymes and bile salts wash past it. Soap film does not do this. A liquid surface pulls itself into the shape with the least area it can reach, which is the opposite of a finger.

So the shape is not coming from the membrane. Something inside is holding it.

## What is inside a microvillus

Cut one across and you find a bundle of actin filaments running its full length. Roughly 19 of them, cross-linked into a rod stiff enough to hold the membrane out against surface tension. The bundle continues down past the base of the projection into the cell, where a layer called the terminal web cross-links neighbouring bundles to each other through a non-erythrocytic spectrin. Each bundle is anchored at the bottom into a shared mat, and the mat is what stops nineteen filaments from simply tipping over.

The bundle does not float free inside the tube. Cross-bridges made of myosin-1A with calmodulin reach out sideways from the outer filaments of the bundle to the membrane itself, two or three from each outer filament. The membrane is held on the frame, at intervals, all the way up.

Three proteins do the cross-linking inside the bundle, and they are not interchangeable. Fimbrin is the main one; a bundle assembled with fimbrin alone is indistinguishable from an intact microvillar core. Villin is a secondary cross-linker that makes looser and less regular bundles, and it has a second setting: when calcium rises, villin stops bundling actin and starts severing it. Small espin is present at about a twentieth of the concentration of the others.

Read that villin fact twice, because it is a real design feature. One protein in the core is a switch that can take the core apart.

## The end that faces the lumen

The filaments in the bundle all point the same way. Every one is unipolar, with its barbed end, the plus end, embedded in a dense plaque at the very tip of the projection, and its pointed end directed down into the cell. New actin subunits are added exclusively at the barbed end, which is the end at the tip.

That places the growing end of every filament in the bundle at the surface facing the gut contents.

Consider what follows. The part of this structure that is being actively extended is the part pressed against the lumen, the least protected position the cell has. A structure that grows from its exposed tip has to keep supplying subunits to that tip, and it can be shortened from there too. The alternative arrangement, growth from the base, would have let the cell build the projection from safety and push it outward. Actin does not offer that option here, because the filament's own chemistry fixes which end grows.

The projections sit in a regular hexagonal array, 115 to 120 nanometres from centre to centre. They are not a random fringe. They are a packed, spaced, individually guyed set of rods, each one built from a polymer whose direction of assembly the cell does not choose.

Before going on, answer in one sentence: why can a lipid bilayer not hold a microvillus in shape by itself?

Then answer a second: if you wanted to shorten every microvillus on a cell quickly, which single protein named above would you go after, and what would you do to it?

**Answer.** A lipid bilayer is a two-dimensional fluid: its molecules exchange places millions of times a second and it has no fixed internal arrangement to resist being pushed out of shape. It will take a shape that is imposed on it and it cannot impose one. Something rigid inside has to hold the projection out, and here that is the actin bundle.

For the second: villin. It cross-links the core bundle at resting calcium and converts to a severing protein as calcium rises, so raising intracellular calcium turns a bundler into a cutter and the core is dismantled from within. Going after fimbrin would remove the primary cross-linker, which is a defensible answer and a slower one; the point of naming villin is that one protein already carries both jobs and the cell switches between them.
:::

The rest of the lesson generalises this. A cell bounded by a fluid gets its shape, its attachments, and its movement from a set of polymers inside it. There are three of them, they have genuinely different mechanics, and the next scene is about why the differences matter more than the names.
