# Four attachments, and which polymer each one grips

Lesson 04 drew the tight junction as a line between two membranes and said plainly that lesson 07 would have to say what the line is made of. Here it is, and there turn out to be four attachments instead of one.

They are not four copies of the same idea. They sit at different heights on the cell, they are built from different proteins, they grip different polymers, and they fail differently. Get the pairing of junction to polymer right and you can predict which of them a given defect implicates. Get it backwards and you cannot.

## Down the side of the cell, in order

In a simple epithelium like this one, the tight junction sits most apically. Below it comes the adherens junction with its associated belt of actin, and below that the desmosomes.

The tight junction is the seal. At the tight junction the space between the two cells disappears altogether; at the adherens junction and at the desmosome the neighbouring membranes stay 15 to 20 nanometres apart. That single geometric fact tells you which of the three closes the route between cells and which two do not. Adhesion and sealing are separate jobs, and only the top one of the three does the sealing. It is the rate-limiting step for anything travelling between cells instead of through them.

Its transmembrane proteins are the claudins, occludin, and JAM-A. On the inside, scaffold proteins ZO-1, ZO-2 and ZO-3 connect those to actin. The claudins are what make the seal selective: they form charge-selective pores, so the tight junction is a regulated gate and not a plug.

The tight junction also does a second job that lesson 04 established and this lesson takes as given. Besides sealing the route between cells, it acts as a fence within the plane of the membrane itself, stopping apical membrane components from drifting round into the basolateral face. The original demonstration, in a polarised dog kidney monolayer, found the tight junction blocked diffusion of a fluorescent phospholipid in the outer leaflet and not in the cytoplasmic one. So one structure holds two separations at right angles to each other: it closes the gap between two cells, and it divides one cell's own membrane into two domains. The seal is what the rest of this scene compares against the other attachments; the fence is why the apical and basolateral surfaces you already know about stay different.

The adherens junction below it uses two adhesion systems reaching across to the neighbour, and they do not share a linker. E-cadherin, a classical cadherin, connects to actin through the catenins. Nectins are a separate immunoglobulin-family system and reach actin principally through afadin. Both end at actin, which is why it is tempting to run them together, and the objective here is which protein makes each link, so the two routes are kept apart. The junction's business is starting and stabilising the contact between two cells.

Then the desmosome, and this is where the polymer changes.

## The one that does not use actin

A desmosome is built from three classes of protein. Desmosomal cadherins, the desmogleins and desmocollins, reach across the gap to the neighbouring cell. Armadillo proteins, plakoglobin and the plakophilins, sit on the cytoplasmic face. And desmoplakin, a cytolinker, anchors keratin-containing intermediate filaments to the membrane.

So the desmosome grips keratin, and keratin is an intermediate filament.

Now recall what the mechanics said about intermediate filaments. They stretch to multiple times their length without breaking and they hold force at higher tensile loads than actin does. The junction whose job is mechanical coupling under load is precisely the junction wired to the polymer that survives load. The pairing is not arbitrary.

This is the place learners most often invert, so state it in both directions. Adherens junctions link to actin. Desmosomes link to intermediate filaments. If you find yourself saying that desmosomes are the actin ones, you have swapped them, and every downstream prediction will be wrong.

## The fourth attachment faces down, not sideways

The three above all join one cell to another. The cell also has to stay attached to what it is sitting on.

Beneath the sheet lies the basement membrane, a mat of laminins, fibronectin and other matrix material. Epithelial cells in the human intestinal crypt bind it through integrins: laminin receptors including the α2β1, α3β1 and α6β4 combinations, and the fibronectin-binding α5β1 among the ones recognising the RGD motif. On the inside, integrin-linked kinase creates a physical link between the integrin **β1** tail and actin.

Read that subunit name, because it limits the claim. The route just described is a β1 route. Three of the four receptors named above carry a β1 subunit; α6β4 does not, and in the hemidesmosomes of other epithelia an α6β4 integrin connects laminin to keratin intermediate filaments through linkers including plectin. So "basal integrins attach to actin" is true of the β1 receptors this lesson's source establishes in this tissue, and is not a statement about every integrin on the basal surface. The map below draws the β1 route for that reason.

With that limit stated, three of the four attachments on this cell converge on actin.

That convergence is worth pausing on. Actin is not the strongest of the three under stretch, and the scene before this one showed intermediate filaments beating it on tensile load. Yet actin gets three of the four attachments. Actin's advantage is that it is polar and carries myosin motors, so an actin-linked attachment can be pulled on, tightened, and released directly, along the filament, by the cell's own motors. Keratin has no motor that walks along it, so a keratin-linked attachment cannot be actuated that way.

That is a statement about motors and not a verdict of passivity, and the difference matters. The desmosome review this lesson uses describes desmosomes and their intermediate filaments participating in the organisation of actin, in Rho-family signalling, in adhesion dynamics, in cell mechanics and in migration. So the keratin-linked attachment is regulated and takes part in how the cell behaves; what it lacks is a motor track of its own. The cell distributes its attachments between a polymer it can pull on directly and a polymer that will not tear, and both are under control.

## Two things this scene leaves out

Gap junctions are absent from the map above on purpose. The organising question here is which cytoskeletal polymer an attachment anchors, and this lesson neither asks nor answers what a gap junction does; lesson 06 of this block, on signalling between cells, is the place that question belongs.

Hemidesmosomes are a harder omission and an honest one. The desmosome review used above names hemidesmosomes alongside integrin-based focal adhesions as the structures through which cell-to-matrix adhesion happens, and says no more about them. The crypt study used above describes focal adhesions and integrin-linked kinase in human intestinal crypt cells and does not address hemidesmosomes at all. So the map shows the actin-linked basal route because that is the route these sources establish in this tissue. Whether the basal surface of a colonic cell also carries a second, keratin-linked attachment is a real question that this lesson does not answer, and the gap is left visible instead of being filled with a plausible guess.
