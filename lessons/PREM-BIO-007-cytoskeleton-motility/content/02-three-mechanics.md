# Three polymers, and what each one's stiffness will let it do

Textbooks introduce the cytoskeleton as a list of three: actin filaments, microtubules, intermediate filaments. Memorising the list buys almost nothing. What each one can do for a cell follows from three measurable properties, and once you have those, most of the jobs assign themselves.

The three properties are how stiff a single filament is, whether the filament has a direction, and how far it can be stretched before it fails.

## How stiff, expressed as a length

Stiffness for a thin filament is usually quoted as a persistence length: the distance along the filament over which it stays roughly straight against the random kicks of the surrounding water. Below that distance the filament behaves like a rod. Far above it, the filament behaves like cooked spaghetti.

| Filament | Persistence length | Same figure, in micrometres |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Microtubule | greater than 1 mm | greater than 1000 µm |
| Actin filament | 3 to 17 µm | 3 to 17 µm |
| Intermediate filament | a few hundred nm to about 1 µm | about 0.2 to 1 µm |

Three orders of magnitude separate the top row from the bottom. That is not a detail of degree.

Now put a cell next to those numbers. A colonic absorptive cell is about 20 to 25 µm tall. A microvillus on its apical face is about 1 µm long. Those are the two lengths that matter, and the table above is only useful once you compare it against them. The next scene does that comparison properly, as a worked calculation. For now, hold the shape of the answer: a microtubule is rigid across anything cell-sized, an actin filament is stiff over a micrometre and floppy over a cell, and a single intermediate filament is floppy over both.

## Whether the filament has a direction

Actin filaments and microtubules are polar. The two ends differ chemically, subunits are added preferentially at one of them, and, decisively, motor proteins can tell the ends apart. Myosins walk on actin. Kinesins and dyneins walk on microtubules. Each motor moves toward a particular end, so a polar filament is a track with a direction on it.

Intermediate filaments are built differently. Their dimers associate in an antiparallel fashion into tetramers that have no net direction, and filaments assembled from those tetramers are apolar. The consequence is not subtle: intermediate filaments carry no molecular motors at all. There is no myosin, kinesin or dynein equivalent for them.

Say what that rules out. Nothing can be carried along an intermediate filament to a chosen destination. No intermediate filament can be walked along to generate directed movement. Whatever intermediate filaments are for, it is not transport and it is not motion.

This is the single most useful distinction in the three-way comparison, and it is the one a list of names hides.

## How far it stretches before it fails

Here the ranking inverts, and the floppiest filament turns out to be the toughest.

Intermediate filaments bear far larger extensional strain than microtubules or actin filaments. Several kinds have been stretched past 200 per cent of their resting length without breaking; single filaments reach about 250 per cent, and desmin filaments up to about 350 per cent. Stretching one to three and a half times its length and finding it intact is not behaviour actin or tubulin offers.

Networks of them behave in a related way. A vimentin network's shear modulus climbs by more than an order of magnitude between 1 per cent and 80 per cent strain: the more you deform it, the harder it pushes back. Intermediate filament networks begin to stiffen at around 10 to 20 per cent strain, while actin networks stiffen at strains of a few per cent. Actin resists early and gives way sooner. Intermediate filaments yield early, then refuse to break. Compared directly against actin, intermediate filaments stretch to multiple times their original length and hold force at higher tensile loads.

## Putting the three properties together

The argument now closes.

A microtubule is effectively a rigid rod on the scale of a cell, and it is polar. Both properties point the same way: use it to define an axis and to run directed traffic along that axis. In an absorptive intestinal cell, microtubules are in fact arranged along the apical-to-basal axis, which is the axis the cell's whole division of labour is organised around.

An actin filament is stiff over about a micrometre and carries myosin motors. Both properties again point one way: use it to build and to move things the size of a surface projection. That is what the previous scene described, nineteen filaments cross-linked into a beam a micrometre long with myosin cross-bridges holding a membrane onto it.

An intermediate filament is floppy, apolar, motorless, and nearly unbreakable under stretch. It cannot define an axis and it cannot carry anything. What it can do is absorb a large deformation and still be there afterwards. So it is the one to reach for when the question is not "what shape should this be" or "how do I move this", but "how does this tissue survive being pulled about".

A sheet of cells climbing out of a crypt while bacteria and digesta scrape past it is being pulled about constantly. The next question is where in that sheet each of these polymers is anchored, and that is what a junction turns out to be.

The persistence-length sources report bending stiffness for purified filaments measured in vitro.
