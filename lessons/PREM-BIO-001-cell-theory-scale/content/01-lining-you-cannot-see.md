# A square centimetre of gut, and what the eye leaves out

Open a length of human large intestine and the inner surface looks like wet pink velvet. Nothing about it announces that it is made of parts. It is even to the eye, it is even under a gloved finger, and for most of the history of medicine that was the entire body of evidence about what a lining is made of.

Put the same piece under a lens and the velvet turns out to be perforated. Narrow pits stand packed side by side across the whole surface, each one a blind-ended tube of living tissue sunk into the wall. Anatomy calls one of those tubes a **crypt**, and this module follows a single one of them from here to the end of the block.

## What the crypt is doing while you read this

At the bottom of the tube sit **stem cells**, meaning cells that keep dividing and whose daughters can either stay as stem cells or go on to become something specialised. Those daughters are pushed upward, and as they climb they change: they stop dividing and take on a job, most of them absorbing water and salt from the gut contents, some of them making mucus. Near the top they work for a few days, then die and are shed into the **lumen**, the open space inside the tube of the gut, and carried away. Signalling from the base falls off as a cell climbs, and how much of that signal a cell is still receiving is one way it registers how far from the base it has travelled. Lesson 06 takes that up properly.

The lining, in other words, is not maintained. It is continuously rebuilt, and it never stops.

A crypt in the descending colon holds roughly 700 cells, stacked about 32 along its length and about 22 around its circumference. The colonic lining renews every three to five days, and the absorptive cells that do most of the work last about four to five days. Whatever is lining your colon at this moment was not there last week.

## Why the arrangement is worth the trouble

A sheet of cells covering a surface or lining a cavity is called an **epithelium**, and the colonic epithelium is one cell thick. On the far side of it is the gut contents. An adult colon carries something like $3.8\times10^{13}$ bacteria; the body's own cells come to about $3\times10^{13}$ by one published estimate, and to roughly 28 to 36 trillion by another that counted differently. Put plainly, the bacteria in one organ are of the same order as every cell you are made of, and a single layer of cells stands between them and your blood.

That sheet has to stay sealed, it has to keep absorbing while it is being replaced, and it has to hand its work over to newly made cells every few days without opening a gap. Doing all three at once is what the crypt's arrangement is for.

They sit at the same order of magnitude: both are a few times $10^{13}$, so their ratio is somewhere near 1 and nowhere near 10. That licenses the statement that the two populations are comparable in number. It licenses nothing about which is larger to within a few per cent, because neither figure is a measurement of a particular person. Both are estimates assembled from assumptions about a reference body, and both sources say so. Reading either as a constant would put precision into a number that was never offering any.

## One word, three meanings

The word **cell** arrives already loaded. A prison cell is a room. A spreadsheet cell is a box in a grid. A battery cell is a power source. All three carry the idea of a small bounded compartment, which is exactly where the biological term came from, and the resemblance stops there. From here on, *cell* means the smallest unit of a living thing that is itself alive: bounded, self-maintaining, and able to give rise to more of its kind. Everything else the word does in English can be set aside.

## What this lesson has to answer

Everything above was unavailable to anyone before the middle of the seventeenth century, and not for want of looking. Physicians had been opening bodies and describing what they found for two thousand years, and they described organs, membranes, vessels, and fluids. Nobody described cells, because nobody could see one.

That is a claim about a number, and the next scene asks you to work the number out.

They disagree by tens of per cent and agree on the order of magnitude, which is the only part this lesson uses.
