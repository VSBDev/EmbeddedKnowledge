# The word came from a piece of dead bark

In 1665 an English polymath named Robert Hooke published a book of engravings made through a microscope. *Micrographia* sold well, it made instruments fashionable, and in it Hooke did something with lasting consequences for medicine: he gave a name to the small walled chambers his lenses showed him. He called them cells, meaning small rooms.

Cork is the material he is remembered for sectioning. Two details of that work usually get lost, and both are worth keeping.

First, he did not treat cork as part of a plant at all. He compared it instead to a mushroom, which tells you how uncertain the classification of living material still was. Second, the chambers he saw in cork were empty. Cork is dead tissue, and what survives in it is the wall. When Hooke examined living plant material he recorded that its chambers were filled with fluid, so he had both observations in front of him. The one that got the name was the empty box.

That is the origin of a word your whole training will use, and it is a slightly unfortunate one. The name points at the container. Two centuries of work were needed to establish that the contents were the point.

## Look at the same tissue

Below is cork bark under an optical microscope. Look first at the lower right, where a labelled bar marks 100 µm. That bar is the same length as the threshold you are about to compare everything against, and it is what makes this a figure you can argue with.

## Check the scale claim instead of accepting it

The previous scene asserted two numbers: an eye separates detail down to about 100 µm, and a cell is roughly 10 to 20 µm. This picture lets you test the second one without trusting anyone, because it carries a bar of known length.

:::{check}
:id: check-read-the-scale-bar
:kind: retrieval

Use the 100 µm bar at the lower right.

1. Estimate how many striations fit inside one bar length.
2. From that count, work out the width of one repeat.
3. Compare your answer with the cell width used in the worked example. Does the picture support it, contradict it, or leave it open?
:::

Counting is rough, and it should be: somewhere around five to ten stripes span the bar. Dividing 100 µm by a count in that range puts one repeat at roughly 10 to 20 µm, which is the band the worked example used. Be careful about what that licenses. Cork is plant tissue and a cork cell is not a colonic cell, so the picture confirms nothing about the gut. What it does confirm is the general size claim the whole argument rests on: the repeating unit of a tissue comes out in tens of micrometres, several times below the threshold you computed, and it does so on a photograph carrying its own ruler. Anyone reporting a cork cell width of 14.3 µm from this image would be inventing digits the method cannot produce.

Two further things follow from the bar. The fragments run many bar-lengths across, so they are well above the eye's threshold; you could have seen a fleck of cork unaided while seeing nothing whatever of its structure. And the chambers are empty, exactly as the account above predicted: no contents, only walls.

## From bark to your gut

A reasonable objection at this point is that cork has nothing to do with medicine. Bark is not gut, plants are not animals, and a chamber in dead cork is not an absorbing cell in a living crypt.

The objection is the right one, and answering it is what turned an observation into a claim about life. Hooke had shown that one dead plant material has a repeating unit structure. Somebody still had to show that living plant tissue does, that animal tissue does, that human tissue does, and that the units in all of them are the same kind of thing. Until that work was done, the honest summary of *Micrographia* was that bark is porous.

The name for the finished claim is cell theory, and the next scene is about the word *theory* in that phrase, because it is the word most likely to mislead you.
