# Clinical wrap-up: what goes in the record, and what gets asked for

:::{callout}
:kind: boundary
:id: callout-wrap-up-boundary

Teaching example, not medical advice. R. and T. are invented. The note below is a model of how differential reasoning is written down, produced by this lesson's method on this lesson's fictional file. It is not a template for any real person's care, it reaches no diagnosis, and it is not a basis for requesting or interpreting anyone's investigations.
:::

The appointment is ending. The clinical question is the one that always arrives at this point and rarely gets taught: what goes in the record, and what gets asked for before the next contact.

## The note

> **Working differential, R., first assessment.** Four months of low mood, loss of interest, waking at four and not returning to sleep, about 6 kg of unintentional weight loss, withdrawal from friends, and slipping performance at work, in a rotating night-shift worker who drinks most evenings and has drunk more over the last two months.
>
> A depressive presentation is established. Which course it belongs to is open, because a depressive episode occurs in a unipolar and in a bipolar-spectrum course alike and separates neither from the other. A unipolar course stays viable exactly as far as the search for a past episode of elevated or activated mood has been adequate, and that search currently consists of one account, from R. alone, of one week six years ago.
>
> A bipolar-spectrum course is live and not established. It rests on one item: R.'s account of about five days six years ago of markedly reduced sleep with increased activity, increased talkativeness and several impulsive purchases, never assessed at the time, described by R. as a good patch. The reported duration clears the usual four-day convention, so what is uncertain here is the report and not the threshold. A second and weaker item points the same way: a parent treated with lithium for years, details unknown to R. Both are confirmable with consent, by asking R. again and by asking someone who knew R. six years ago, and by the parent's prescribing record.
>
> Alcohol is contributing and is unlikely to account for the whole picture, because the mood change and the early waking lead the escalation in drinking by about two months. The current pattern is a strong candidate for holding the early waking open. The start date of the pre-escalation drinking is not recorded. Quantity, dependence severity, previous withdrawal and its complications are also unrecorded; no period of abstinence is proposed here, and none could be contemplated before that assessment.
>
> Thyroid disease. Cold intolerance, constipation and fatigue beyond the sleep loss point towards it; the unintentional weight loss points away; the rest of the feature set was never asked about; and no further history closes it. Thyroid-stimulating hormone and free thyroxine outstanding, which would answer the thyroid question and no other.
>
> Shift work and circadian disruption are plausible contributors of unresolved direction. The shift pattern across the four months is not recorded, and until it is, "wakes at four" cannot be read.
>
> Never asked, and therefore holding no position in either direction: loss, trauma, and neurological enquiry. No medication list was taken, so the medication alternative has nothing on either side and cannot be placed at all.
>
> Risk: passive thoughts that life is not worth the effort, no plan, no intent, no act, raised only on direct questioning. Carried to the safety formulation.

## Five questions and one test

| What | What it buys | What it does not buy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An account of the week six years ago, from R. asked again and, with consent, from someone who was around then | A second line on the highest-weight item in the file, and the first view of that week from outside it | A diagnosis, or agreement. A friend's memory six years on has its own gaps, and the two accounts can differ without either being wrong |
| The parent's prescribing history | Turns "took lithium" into a family history or removes it | Anything about R.'s own course |
| The shift pattern across the four months | Makes the early-waking item readable and separates roster-driven sleep loss from the rest | An explanation for the mood |
| When the evening drinking started | Completes the chronology argument that currently runs on half its evidence | The direction of causation. It is a question about dates and carries no suggestion about stopping |
| A medication list | Lets the medication alternative be considered at all | A cause, since association is not causation and only timing arguments carry weight |
| Thyroid function | An answer to the thyroid question: a result inside the range lowers that hypothesis, on that assay and on R.'s state that day | Anything about the other medical alternatives, which this test was never sent to answer. If mildly abnormal it starts an argument instead of ending one, because raised thyroid-stimulating hormone with a normal free thyroxine is common in tested depressed people and has not been shown to cause or worsen depression |

Five of the six are questions. One is a blood test. Nothing on the list is expensive, and the file has been carrying all six as silence.

## The prospective part, written in advance

Waiting and watching is a channel you can create, and writing the question down before the watching starts is what makes the result readable when it arrives. An observation nobody planned still counts if it was recorded at the time; it arrives with the extra job of saying what was being watched for and what was not. For R. the plan records three things, separately, against dates: mood, sleep, and the shift roster; any period of reduced sleep with increased activity, dated when it happens instead of recalled later; and, if the drinking changes, the mood response and the sleep response noted as two results, because they run on different timescales and anyone treating them as one measurement will misread whatever they find.

Written that way, the next three months produce evidence. Written as "review in three months", they produce another retrospective account with the same weaknesses as the first one.

## What this note refuses to do

It does not choose. Refusing to choose is not the same as being vague, and the difference is visible on the page: every alternative in that note carries what speaks for it, what speaks against it, what nobody has asked, and a named next step. "It could be many things" carries none of those, and it leaves the next clinician doing the whole assessment again.

The two readers from the first scene can both sign that note. They would still bet differently about R., and the note does not ask them not to. It fixes what they are betting on and what would settle it, which is the most a first conversation can produce.

The note also does not say what should be done about any of it. That question is real and it is the next one, and it belongs to the medication and interventional evidence outcome (PSY-250.04) and the psychological and social evidence outcome (PSY-250.05). Deciding between alternatives and deciding what to do are different pieces of reasoning, and the second one gets worse when the first is skipped.

## Accessibility and alternatives

Every task here can be answered in prose, in bullets, or as a table, whichever suits you, and you may type, dictate, or work with a tutor. The tables repeat their logic in the surrounding prose and are read in normal reading order. Nothing depends on colour, position on a page, dragging, sound, motion, or a timed reveal. No task asks for your own health information, medication, diagnosis, or family history, and none requires contact with a patient, a clinic, or a laboratory.

This lesson describes low mood, alcohol use, bereavement, and passive thoughts about life not being worth the effort. If the material meets something in your own life, stop or step away without penalty. EmbeddedKnowledge does not monitor learners and provides no clinical or crisis service; for personal support use an appropriately qualified local service or your current emergency route.

## Recovery route

The assessment is untimed and retakeable, and a missed criterion routes you to a specific reread instead of a repeat of the same case.

- If the ranking came out as a list of labels with no evidence attached, redo the five days with weight and channel separated, then write the two sentences again before ranking anything.
- If each alternative came out with one word against it, reread the three readings. Evidence direction, how much was asked, and what would close the remainder are three answers, and an alternative that carries evidence on both sides with part of it unasked needs all three.
- If an alternative you never asked about ended up at the bottom of your list, take it off the list. A low position is the output of a search, and reporting one you did not run tells the next reader something false.
- If a normal test result made you strike out a whole category, go back to the thyroid bullets. A test answers the question it was sent, under the assumptions of that day.
- If the alcohol reasoning came out as either "it is the drink" or "it is not the drink", go back to the dates. Two mechanisms starting at different times is the answer the timeline supports, and the note has to carry both. If it came out as a plan for R. to stop drinking, reread the safety callout: that is a clinical decision with its own assessment, and this lesson only reads intervals that already happened.
- If you concluded that R. has a bipolar-spectrum disorder, or that R. does not, reread the worked example. The evidence supports neither conclusion, and reaching one is the specific failure this lesson is built to prevent.
- If you are unsure how much confidence to state, that is the skill taught by communicating and updating a formulation (PSY-210.06), which this lesson assumes. Return here after it.

## Where this goes next

Safety and uncertainty in mood presentations (PSY-250.03) is where the risk line in R.'s note gets worked properly. Longitudinal mood formulation (PSY-250.06) is where a differential like this one is revised against what the following months actually showed, which is the only way to find out whether the reasoning here was any good.
