## Completion problem: the thyroid

Most of this one is done for you. Finish the last column.

R. feels cold, is constipated, and is tired past what the lost sleep would explain. Those three sit among the usual presenting features of hypothyroidism, alongside lethargy, dry skin and weight gain. Against them: R. has lost about 6 kg without trying, and weight gain is the direction hypothyroidism usually pushes. Against them again, more quietly: a four-month depressive picture with reduced intake and reduced activity explains the constipation and the weight loss without needing a thyroid at all. Individual symptoms are poor at identifying hypothyroidism, they overlap other conditions, and the diagnosis is made by measuring thyroid-stimulating hormone and free thyroxine.

| What speaks against it | Unintentional weight loss; a competing explanation for two of the three features |
| Which state | ? |
| What would move it, and which way | ? |

The state is *not answerable from history*. No further conversation will settle it, and one blood test will. That is the whole reason the missing result is worth writing in the note as a missing result instead of reasoning past it.

The second answer needs more care than it usually gets. A normal result removes an alternative and does nothing else. A mildly raised thyroid-stimulating hormone with a normal free thyroxine is a common finding when depressed people are tested, and large observational studies together with small intervention studies have not established that it causes or worsens depression; correcting it often leaves the depressive symptoms where they were. So a mildly abnormal result does not hand you an explanation. It hands you a second finding that then has to be argued for.

Two arguments about thyroid testing get run together and they are different arguments. One is about testing everyone with a depressive presentation, where the usual result is the mildly raised thyroid-stimulating hormone described above and the causal reading of it has not held up. The other is about testing a person who has cold intolerance, constipation, and disproportionate fatigue, which is a symptom-driven question. R. is in the second, and the evidence about the first does not settle it.

## Supported problem: the shifts

R. works rotating night shifts. Work through this one with two prompts.

**Prompt one.** Night-shift workers were about 40% more likely to develop depression than day workers in a meta-analysis of eleven studies, individual studies of major depressive disorder specifically have gone both ways, and whether circadian disruption causes mood disorder, follows from it, or accompanies it without causing either is unresolved. Given that, what does the shift work earn on the list?

**Prompt two.** The file says R. wakes at four in the morning and does not get back to sleep. In a rotating-shift worker, what would you need to know before that sentence means anything?

A contextual alternative competes at the level of explanation, not at the level of label, so the useful question is how much of the picture it accounts for and whether changing it would change the course. Here it earns a place as a plausible contributor with an unresolved direction of causation, which is a weaker claim than the association alone might suggest.

The second prompt is the one that pays. Four o'clock means one thing in a person who sleeps from eleven to seven and something else in a person whose sleep window moved twice last week. Until you know R.'s shift pattern across the four months, one of the most-quoted features of the presentation is uninterpretable. That is a free question and nobody has asked it.

## Independent problems: the neurological and the medication alternatives

No prompts. Work both, then read on.

Neurological alternatives, in a 34-year-old with no reported focal signs, no reported cognitive change, and no reported neurological history, sit low on what is in the file. Before recording them as countered, check whether the assessment covered them. If the notes are silent because the questions were not asked, the state is *unassessed*, and the two states get written differently. An absence that has been looked for and an absence that has not been looked for are worth different amounts, and the note is the only place that difference survives.

The medication alternative is starker. The file contains no medication list. More than a dozen classes of general medical drug have been associated with depressive symptoms, an association between taking a drug and becoming depressed is not by itself a causal relation, and the arguments that carry weight are temporal: onset after starting or increasing a drug, and resolution after reducing or stopping it. None of that reasoning can begin without a list. The alternative is not low. It has not been entered.

## Independent problems: grief and trauma

Nothing in R.'s file speaks to either, in either direction, because neither was asked about.

Prolonged grief is descriptively distinct from depression and from post-traumatic stress, with separation distress and yearning for the person who died as its distinguishing features, and the three co-occur often enough that finding one is no reason to stop looking for the others. Longitudinal work in bereaved adults finds their symptoms tracking one another across measurement waves, with the direction of influence unsettled. Trauma exposure is found the same way, by asking.

Both are *unassessed*. Writing "no history of loss or trauma" when the question was not put is the commonest way a differential turns an absence of asking into a negative finding, and it is the one entry on this list that a later reader has no way to detect.
