# Every item of history has two prices

A differential that lists nine categories and stops is a textbook page. A differential that works is a ranked set of alternatives, each one carrying the evidence that put it where it is and the next thing that would move it.

Ranking needs a unit of reasoning smaller than the alternative. That unit is the item of evidence, and each item has two separate properties that are easy to collapse into one.

:::{definition}
:id: definition-weight
:label: Weight

**Weight** is how far an item would move the ranking if it happened exactly as described. It is read off the content of the item against the rest of the file, so a new item elsewhere can raise or lower it. What it never depends on is whether this particular item is true.
:::

:::{definition}
:id: definition-channel
:label: Channel

**Channel** is the route by which the item reached you, and how much that route can be relied on for this kind of content. It is a property of the evidence, and it does not depend on how interesting the content is.
:::

An item with high weight and a weak channel is the ordinary case in a mood history, and it is the one people get wrong in both directions. Treating it as settled overreads it. Discarding it because the channel is weak throws away the most informative thing in the file. The move that works is to state both judgements separately, then name the second channel that would firm the item up.

## The lithium in R.'s family

Take a line from the file that is not the five days.

*A parent had "nerves" and took lithium for years; R. knows nothing further.*

**Weight, if true.** Among the features of a depressive presentation that predict a later bipolar diagnosis, family history of bipolar disorder is the best replicated, alongside early onset, a recurrent course, and subthreshold hypomanic or mixed features, meaning features of an elevated or mixed state that fell short of an episode in duration or in number. A confirmed parental bipolar disorder would move the bipolar-spectrum alternative up substantially, and it is the strongest thing a family history can contribute.

**What the file actually contains.** A family history of long-term lithium use, reported second-hand, attached to a folk word for distress. Long-term lithium is a strong pointer without being a diagnosis: what the file holds is R.'s account of a treatment decision made for someone else, years ago, which R. did not witness and cannot describe. Getting from that to a confirmed parental bipolar disorder takes one inferential step, and the step is not free.

**Channel.** R.'s account of a parent's treatment, unverified, non-specific, and given while R. is depressed.

**Second channel.** Ask R. to ask. Or, with consent, seek the prescribing record. This is a cheap question with a large answer, and it is the kind of thing that gets left out of a first assessment because it is nobody's job in the room.

The item did not resolve. Its rank went from "strong bipolar pointer" to "one confirmable question away from a strong bipolar pointer", which is a different instruction to whoever sees R. next.

## Three kinds of longitudinal evidence

:::{definition}
:id: definition-longitudinal-evidence
:label: Longitudinal evidence

**Longitudinal evidence** is evidence about the shape of a person's illness over time: episodes, their order, their duration, what happened between them, and what changed alongside them.
:::

It arrives by three channels, and they are not interchangeable.

| Channel | What it is | Its characteristic weakness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Retrospective self-report | The person's account of past episodes, given now | Recall bias, and the weakest episodes are the hardest to recall |
| Collateral and records | An informant who was present, or a document written at the time | Availability, consent, and the informant's own vantage point |
| Prospective observation | What you and the person record from today forward | It takes the time it takes |

Only the third is a channel you can create. Writing down in advance what you are watching for, and what each possible outcome would mean, is what makes the result easy to read when it arrives and hard to reshape afterwards. An observation nobody planned still counts if it was recorded at the time; it carries the extra job of saying what was and was not being watched for. Three months of neither produces nothing, because the period then gets reconstructed from memory and you are holding a second retrospective account with the weaknesses of the first.

## Three readings, not one label

The habit this lesson is trying to break is giving each alternative a badge. *Supported*, *countered*, *unassessed*, *needs a test*: these answer different questions, and a live alternative usually answers more than one of them at the same time. Read every alternative three times.

**What the record says, in both directions.** List what speaks for it and what speaks against it, as two entries. Most alternatives worth keeping carry some of each, and one verdict silently drops whichever side is smaller.

**How much of it was asked.** An alternative enquired into thoroughly and one never raised can leave the same short entry in the notes. Whether the question was put is a fact about the assessment, and it is the only thing that tells a later reader what the entry is worth.

**What would close what is left.** Part of the remainder will yield to further conversation. Part of it will not, and needs an informant, a document written at the time, a laboratory result, or a period of observation. Saying which turns an open alternative into a task.

R.'s thyroid question is the case where the three come apart. Cold intolerance, constipation and fatigue past what the sleep loss explains speak for it. About 6 kg of unintentional weight loss speaks against it. Several of the other features nobody has asked about. And the part that decides it is a blood result that no further conversation will produce. Any single label you pick for that alternative will misdescribe two of the three readings.

One more thing follows from the second reading, and it has to be put carefully. An alternative nobody asked about earns no position *from this file*, because a low position reads as the result of a search that was never run. Writing "no history of trauma" when nobody asked is the loud version of that error; quietly ranking it last is the quiet one, and only the loud one is visible to the next reader.

What that does not mean is that an unasked alternative is a blank. Some things are more likely than others before anyone opens their mouth, and a clinician who pretends otherwise is throwing away real information. So keep the two apart on the page: what this file's enquiry has established, and what was already probable independently of it. A ranking may still say which alternative it favours, and should. What it may not do is present a prior as a finding, or let an unasked domain look examined.

:::{check}
:id: check-weight-channel-split

For each of these three lines from R.'s file, say in one sentence what its weight would be if true, and in one sentence what channel delivered it. Rank nothing yet; that comes two scenes from here.

1. "About 6 kg lost over four months, without trying."
2. "Alcohol most evenings, increased over the last two months, to help with sleep."
3. "No thyroid function result available."

One of the three is not the same kind of thing as the other two.
:::

The third line is not an item of history at all. It is the record telling you that one of your alternatives has an outstanding part which no further conversation can close, and that the thing which would close it is cheap. Write that down in those words. The first two lines are worked in the two scenes that follow.
