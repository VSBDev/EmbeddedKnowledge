# Five days, six years ago

Two clinicians read the same notes and disagree. Neither of them disagrees about the four months.

R. is 34 and works rotating night shifts in a warehouse. Four months of low mood and loss of interest. Waking at four in the morning and not getting back to sleep. Still going to work, and slipping at it. No longer seeing friends. About 6 kg lost over the same period, without trying. Alcohol most evenings, more of it over the last two months, which R. says is to get to sleep. Cold, constipated, and tired past what the lost sleep would explain. No thyroid result in the file. A parent had "nerves" and took lithium for years; R. knows nothing further. Asked directly, R. describes stretches when life does not feel worth the effort, with no plan, no intent, and nothing done.

And six years ago, about five days of sleeping very little, being unusually busy and talkative, and buying several things on impulse. Nobody assessed it at the time. R. calls it a good patch.

The first clinician treats those five days as the most informative sentence in the file. The second treats them as one cheerful week in someone's late twenties, described once, six years later, by a person who is depressed now and has every reason to remember an energetic week warmly.

Neither of them doubts that R. is depressed. They differ over what kind of course this episode belongs to, and the episode in front of them cannot settle that on its own. At the moment of the interview a first depressive episode and the depressed phase of a bipolar-spectrum course, meaning a course in which episodes of elevated or activated mood have also occurred, present much alike. What separates them sits in the past, or has not happened yet.

Run the same problem forwards and it looks like this. In a United States population cohort of 6,214 adults with a lifetime diagnosis of major depressive disorder, 4.0% had a manic episode within about three years of follow-up. Some of the people who present as R. presents will look different later, and nothing available at a first appointment says which.

So the five days are load-bearing. The question is how much one sentence of remembered history can be asked to carry, and what it would take to find out.

## What this lesson does

You will work R.'s file as a differential: a set of alternatives, each carrying whatever evidence points its way, whatever evidence points against it, and whatever nobody has looked for. By the end you should be able to:

- work the unipolar, bipolar-spectrum, medical, neurologic, medication, substance, grief, trauma, and contextual alternatives against the file, saying for each what speaks for it, what speaks against it, how much of it was asked, and what would close the remainder;
- judge a single item of past history on two separate properties, how far it would move the differential if it happened as described and how reliable the channel that delivered it is, and say what a second channel would add and what it would still leave open;
- name the evidence, test, or period of observation that would separate two named alternatives, state which way each possible result would move them, keep each result to the question it was sent, and record what stays unavailable.

Two words first. *Depression* here always names the clinical syndrome and never the ordinary sense of feeling low. *Passive thoughts* is the term used later for what R. describes at the end of that summary: thoughts of not wanting to go on, carrying no plan, no intent, and no act.

Three things this lesson leans on and does not teach. Assembling the mood picture itself, with its time course and the source of each observation, belongs to mood phenomenology and function (PSY-250.01). Stating a working formulation with its confidence and inviting correction belongs to communicating and updating a formulation (PSY-210.06). The passive thoughts R. reports are recorded here as part of the picture and are worked as a safety question in collaborative crisis planning (PSY-230.06) and in safety and uncertainty in mood presentations (PSY-250.03). What comes after the differential, meaning anything anyone might do about it, belongs to the treatment outcomes (PSY-250.04 and PSY-250.05) and is out of scope here.

:::{check}
:id: check-what-would-change-your-mind
:kind: retrieval

Before reading on, write two lines and keep them.

1. On what you have read so far, which two alternatives would you put at the top, and what in the file puts them there?
2. Name one thing you could find out this week that would change that order. Say which way the result would move it.

You will compare both lines with your own later answers. The second is what the lesson is about.
