# The drink and the four o'clock waking

:::{check}
:id: check-predict-alcohol-role

Commit to an answer before reading on. R. drinks most evenings, has drunk more over the last two months, and says it is to get to sleep. R. has been waking at four for four months.

Write one sentence saying what you think the alcohol is doing to the sleep, and one sentence saying what would show you were wrong.
:::

One boundary belongs at the front of this scene, because the reasoning below reads periods without drinking as evidence and a reader could take that as an instruction.

:::{callout}
:kind: safety
:id: callout-withdrawal-risk

**Abstinence is not a diagnostic manoeuvre anyone can order up.** In a person who has become physically dependent on alcohol, stopping abruptly can precipitate a withdrawal syndrome whose severe forms include withdrawal seizures and delirium. Those are life-threatening, and they are why severe or impending severe withdrawal is a reason to admit someone. Risk is assessed first, from the quantity and pattern of drinking, the severity of dependence, previous withdrawal episodes and their complications, and physical and psychiatric comorbidity. Where that assessment finds risk, withdrawal is medically assisted and happens where assessment and monitoring are available around the clock.

R.'s file records "most evenings, increased over the last two months" and nothing else. It gives no quantity, no dependence severity, no previous withdrawal, no physical comorbidity. Nothing in it supports proposing that R. stop drinking, and this lesson does not. Everywhere abstinence appears in this scene and later, it appears as a period that has already happened safely: a hospital admission for something else, a documented supported withdrawal, a sober stretch a person describes. The only question put to it is what it shows.
:::

:::{misconception}
:id: misconception-alcohol-explains-the-sleep

**The model to examine.** *R. drinks in the evening. Alcohol breaks up the second half of the night. The four o'clock waking is the drink, so it should be taken out of the depressive picture rather than counted in it.*

**Why it is worth taking seriously.** The mechanism is real and it fits the clock. Evening alcohol shortens the time to fall asleep and sedates through the first part of the night; arousal then rebounds two to three hours after blood alcohol falls close to zero, with rebound REM sleep and more awakenings through the second half. Someone drinking in the evening and waking around three or four in the morning is describing that curve. The hypnotic effect also fades within days of regular use, so the amount needed to get to sleep climbs, which is the loop R. is describing when R. says the drinking has increased and is for sleep.

**The observation the model cannot absorb.** The dates do not line up. The low mood and the four o'clock waking have been there four months. The increase in drinking covers the last two. An increase that began two months after the waking cannot have started the waking. R.'s own account puts the sleep problem first: the drinking is described as a response to it.

**A model that holds both.** Two mechanisms, running in the same direction, starting at different times. Something at four months opened the sleep problem. Alcohol at the current level is a strong candidate for holding it open now, and for having made it worse over the last two months. Those are separate claims, they carry different evidence, and only the second is well supported by the timeline in the file.

**The same test applied to the whole picture.** The question behind the misconception is larger than the sleep: is the depressive picture itself alcohol-related? The discriminator is chronology. Features that appear only during periods of problem drinking and clear during extended sobriety point one way; features that predate the drinking or persist through sobriety point the other. Where a stretch of sobriety has occurred, the convention is to let two to four weeks of it pass before judging the picture, because most symptoms and signs resolve within the first four weeks.

That convention tells you how to read an interval which has occurred. It is never a step anyone proposes, for the reason set out in the callout above.

Applying the chronology test to R. gives a partial answer and exposes a gap. The four-month picture leads the two-month escalation, which argues against the whole presentation being a consequence of the recent drinking. But "most evenings" was true before the escalation as well, and the file does not record when that pattern started. Nobody asked. Until that date exists, the chronology argument runs on half its evidence, and it is a question, not a manoeuvre, so it costs nothing to close.

**One more thing the model gets wrong, in the other direction.** Suppose a period without drinking has occurred and the sleep did not improve across it. That is tempting to read as proof that the sleep belongs to the depressive picture, and it is weaker evidence than it looks. For close to half of people with alcohol dependence, sleep disturbance persists for months after the last drink. Such a period is informative about mood and much less informative about sleep, and anyone treating the two as one measurement will misread whatever they find.

**Recheck, three short cases.** Each describes an interval that has already occurred. For each, say whether chronology favours an independent depressive picture, an alcohol-related one, or neither, and name the one further fact you would want.

1. Low mood began three weeks after the drinking escalated, and lifted over three weeks of abstinence during an admission for something else.
2. Low mood has been present in every one of four episodes over twelve years, two of which fell in periods with no drinking at all.
3. Mood lifted over four weeks of abstinence; the four o'clock waking did not change.

The third is the one that separates readers. Abstinence is one intervention and the two symptoms answer to it on different timescales, so the mood response and the sleep response are two results, not one.
:::

A review of disturbed sleep in alcohol use supports the shape of the night under evening alcohol, the loss of the sedating effect with regular use, and the persistence of sleep disturbance into abstinence for a large minority. A diagnostic review published by the United States National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism supports chronology as the discriminator between independent and alcohol-related psychiatric presentations and the two-to-four-week interval. The safety callout rests on a Royal College of Physicians concise guidance on assessing and managing alcohol dependence and withdrawal, which sets out the risk assessment, the admission criteria, and the requirement for round-the-clock assessment and monitoring, and on an American Thoracic Society statement on severe alcohol withdrawal, which names prior withdrawal, known alcohol use disorder, and heavy recent consumption as the features that mark highest risk. None of these sources describes R., and none supplies a rule for how any individual should manage their drinking; that question belongs to a clinician and the person concerned.
