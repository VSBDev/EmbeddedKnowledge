# Working the five days

:::{worked-example}
:id: worked-example-five-days

**Task.** Decide how far the five days six years ago move the bipolar-spectrum alternative in R.'s differential, and name what would firm the answer. Do not decide what R. has.

**What the file gives.** About five days. Sleeping very little. Unusually busy. Unusually talkative. Several impulsive purchases. Six years ago. Never assessed by anyone. R.'s own word for it: a good patch.

**What the file does not give.** Whether the state was a change from R.'s usual self that other people could see. Whether R. needed less sleep that week or simply got less of it, which are different observations and the file records only the second. What else was going on that week, including R.'s shift pattern, any new medicine, and anything taken to stay awake. Whether the purchases had consequences R. is still carrying. Whether anything similar has happened since. Who else was around to notice.

**Model selected.** Weight and channel, judged separately, because this item scores high on one and low on the other and a single verdict would hide that.

**Plan.** State the weight. State the channel, with the specific bias that applies to this kind of content. Check whether any threshold argument is doing work here. Say what the next channel would be. Write the conclusion in a form the next clinician can act on.

**Weight, if it happened as described.** Potentially high, higher than anything else in the file, and the condition in that sentence is doing real work that the description on hand does not satisfy.

What R. reports is five days of reduced sleep, increased activity, increased talkativeness and disinhibited spending. What a hypomanic or manic episode also requires is a change in mood, elevated or expansive or irritable, running through the period; the file records none. And reduced sleep is not a decreased *need* for sleep. Sleeping little while distressed and needing less sleep while activated read alike in a sentence and are different observations.

So the honest entry is conditional. If the missing phenomenology is established, this becomes the observation that reclassifies the present picture, changing which course R. is understood to be in and which questions matter at every later contact. As it stands it is a lead of high potential weight and unestablished content, and the useful next move is naming what would establish it: the mood change, its duration, whether anyone else noticed, and what it cost.

**Channel, and the bias that applies.** A single retrospective self-report, six years old, given by a person who is currently depressed, about a state that people are known to under-recognise. In a Dutch general-population study, 40 adults who met research criteria for lifetime bipolar disorder were reinterviewed. Nine recognised having had hypomanic or manic episodes, and seven had ever consulted a professional about one. In the same 40 people, 33 recognised their depressive episodes and 30 had consulted someone about those. Roughly a fifth had noticed the elevated side against roughly four fifths for the depressed side. Among those who did not recognise the elevated episodes, the reasons given most often were that the periods had been pleasant and productive, or felt like part of the person's personality.

R. says: a good patch.

R.'s phrasing matches the commonest reason people give for not recognising an episode, and the inference that suggests itself here runs backwards. The match removes one reason to discount the report, namely the assumption that R. would have raised the week as a problem if it had been one. It supplies no positive evidence that the state was hypomanic. A pleasant, productive week is also what a pleasant, productive week feels like. The study found low self-recognition among people who have the condition; it says nothing about how often people who do not have it describe past weeks in those terms, and reading it that way converts a finding about one group into a claim about one person.

**Is a threshold doing any work here?** The convention in widest use sets a minimum of four days for hypomania. R.'s five clears it, so duration is not what limits this case, which is fortunate given what the convention rests on. That four-day minimum entered practice with DSM-IV in 1994. An international group publishing research criteria, its authors including those of the long-running Zurich cohort, states that no empirical evidence supports the particular cut-off and sets the minimum at two days instead, citing studies of shorter episodes; other commentators argue that moving the line down would widen bipolar diagnosis further, and the argument is unsettled. Had R. said three days, the label would have changed and the evidence would not have.

**The next channels.** Two of them, and they are different errands. Several gaps in the list above are R.'s alone to fill: whether the need for sleep was reduced or only the opportunity, what else was running that week, what the purchases cost afterwards, whether anything like it has happened since. Those come from asking R. again and asking better, and no informant supplies them. The one thing R. cannot supply is the view from outside, whether that week looked like a departure from R.'s usual self to anyone who was there. On that question, with R.'s consent, one conversation with someone who knew R. six years ago is worth more than further questioning of R., because there the limit is the channel and not the description. That person arrives with gaps of their own: they saw what they were present for, they are remembering across the same six years, and what they noticed depends on how close they stood. The parent's prescribing history is a third errand and costs about as little.

**Independent check.** Try changing one thing at a time and see what moves.

| Change | What moves |
| --- | --- |
| Five days becomes three | The label under the current convention. Not the evidence, and not what you would do next. |
| R. adds that a friend called it frightening | The channel improves without the content changing, and the ranking moves more than any duration change could. |
| R. adds that the week ran across six night shifts | A competing explanation for the reduced sleep appears, and the item's weight drops until that is unpicked. |
| R. adds two similar weeks in the last two years | Recurrence enters the record, and the item stops being a single retrospective report. |

Three of those four changes are questions you could ask this afternoon.

**Interpretation.** The bipolar-spectrum alternative is live, resting on one retrospective self-report of about five days of reduced sleep with increased activity, talkativeness and impulsive spending six years ago, never assessed, described by R. in positive terms. It is not established. Whether it is the leading alternative depends on the rest of the list, which is not yet worked. It is confirmable in principle, by a fuller account from R., by an informant account of that week, and by the parent's prescribing history, and all three are available.

That is the output. Someone reading it next month can act on it without asking R. to tell the story again.

**Self-explanation.** Which did more to raise the bipolar-spectrum alternative: the content of R.'s five days, or the fact that R. described them warmly? Answer in one sentence, then say what would have to be true for the other answer to be right.
:::

A defensible answer names the content. The warmth of the description is consistent with a hypomanic period going unrecognised and equally consistent with a good week being remembered as a good week, so on its own it moves nothing. It belongs in the note for a different reason: it explains why the episode was never assessed, which tells the next reader why this part of the history is thin instead of leaving them to guess.
