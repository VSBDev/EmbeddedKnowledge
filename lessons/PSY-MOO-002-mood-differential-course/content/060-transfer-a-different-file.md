# Transfer: a file with a second channel sitting in the room

Different person, different shape. The method is the one you have.

T. is 61. T.'s partner died suddenly fourteen months ago. T. had a heart attack ten months ago. A beta blocker was started eight months ago and has continued since. T.'s daughter, who has come to the appointment, dates the change in her father to about nine months ago: low mood, loss of interest, waking early, and a flat quality she says is different from the first months after the death. T. does not drink. Neither T. nor his daughter recalls anything like this before. T.'s own summary is that he is old and sad now.

Four dates, in the order they happened: the death at fourteen months, the heart attack at ten, the mood change at nine, the beta blocker at eight.

:::{check}
:id: check-transfer-tasks

Work these four before reading the discussion. Written answers, five minutes.

1. Build the timeline and say which candidate the chronology supports and which it undercuts.
2. The daughter is in the room. Say what T. can tell you that she cannot, what she can tell you that he cannot, and where each account is limited.
3. Give the three readings to each of: grief-related, depressive, medication, medical, bipolar-spectrum.
4. Name the single item that would move the ranking most, and say which way each possible answer would move it.
:::

**One.** The chronology cuts against the beta blocker as the thing that started this. The mood change is dated a month before the drug began. That is the same tool used on R.'s drinking, pointing the other way: there, an increase two months after the waking could not have started the waking; here, a drug started a month after the mood change could not have started the mood change.

Before crediting yourself with that, check what you retired. Beta blockers carry an old reputation as a cause of depression, and the current evidence does not support it. A randomised trial in T.'s exact situation, adults after a myocardial infarction with preserved left ventricular function, assigned metoprolol or bisoprolol against no beta blocker and measured self-reported depressive symptoms at six to ten weeks and again at a year; the treated group scored about half a point higher on a twenty-one-point subscale at both points, a difference its authors describe as probably below what would matter clinically, with no effect on anxiety. Pointing the other way, a Swedish study of 1.4 million people that compared each person's treated periods against their own untreated periods found hospital admission for a depressive disorder slightly less likely during beta-blocker treatment, with atenolol and metoprolol running with the class. Read together, they put any class-wide effect on depression at somewhere between nothing and very small. So the medication alternative in this file was thin on the drug evidence before the timeline touched it, and the timeline retires the specific version anyway. The heart attack, sitting a month before the mood change, is a temporal association of exactly the shape people find persuasive, and by itself it establishes nothing except that something else was happening then which nobody has yet listed.

**Two.** Ask both, and know what each is for.

T. is the only source for the inside of the last nine months: what changed, in what order, what he made of it, what he has stopped doing. He is also the person to ask, directly, whether there has ever been a spell when he needed less sleep, felt fast, or did things his family remarked on. Being depressed does not remove his ability to answer that. It changes what some of his answers are worth, which is a reason to ask carefully and to ask more than once.

His daughter can supply what he is worst placed to give: an outside view of whether the man in front of you looks different from how her father usually is, and whether she can remember a period when he seemed unlike himself. She saw those from outside; he was inside them. Her account has its own edges. She was not present for all of her father's life and may not have known him during whatever period the bipolar-spectrum question turns on; she is recalling across the same years he is; and she has her own relationship to him, which shapes what she noticed and what she will say. Asking her needs T.'s consent, and asking her while he is in the room changes both accounts.

In R.'s file nobody had been approached at all, and that is the failure worth avoiding here. The repair is two channels with their sources recorded, so a later reader can see which account produced which item.

**Three.** Grief-related is supported, with a real and dated loss. Depressive is supported, on the daughter's account of a change in quality after the early months. Those two are not competitors in the sense that only one may be true: prolonged grief is descriptively distinct from depression and from post-traumatic stress and the three co-occur often, so finding a death in the history is a reason to assess all three and not a reason to stop. Medication has the timeline against it as an initiator and thin drug evidence behind it either way, so what is left is the weak version: a contributor nobody can price. Medical has almost nothing in either column, because a heart attack at ten months means a hospital episode, a medication list, and a set of investigations that nobody in this room has looked at. Bipolar-spectrum has nothing either way and has never been asked in a form that would answer it: "anything like this" asks about low mood, and neither T. nor his daughter has been asked about a period of the opposite kind.

**Four.** Two answers are defensible and it is worth knowing why.

A direct question about past periods of elevated or activated mood, put to T. and, with his consent, to his daughter, is the item that would change the category rather than the weighting, and it costs two questions in one appointment. The full medication and investigation record from the cardiac admission is the item that would populate three alternatives at once, and it costs one request. Choose the first if you want the largest single move; choose the second if you want the largest reduction in how much of the file nobody has asked about. What is not defensible is leaving the appointment having asked T. about his mood and nobody about anything else.

**The trap in this case.** A death, then low mood, then a man who says he is old and sad. The pull is to write "bereavement" and stop, which turns a supported alternative into a conclusion and leaves four other alternatives unassessed on the grounds that one of them is plausible. The presence of a good explanation is not evidence against the others.

The distinctness of prolonged grief from depression and post-traumatic stress, and how often the three occur together, comes from a systematic review of longitudinal studies in bereaved adults. The point that many general medical drugs are associated with depressive symptoms and that attribution rests on temporal argument comes from a review of those drug classes, now seventeen years old, which is why the beta-blocker figures here come from two later studies instead: a pre-specified quality-of-life sub-study of a randomised trial after myocardial infarction, and a Swedish register cohort of 1.4 million people using a within-individual design. The first was open-label and measured its baseline after randomisation; the second counts only depression severe enough to reach hospital. The limits of a person's own account of past episodes come from a review of depression preceding bipolar diagnosis and from the general-population recognition study, neither of which establishes that an informant is a better source than the person; both channels are used here with their limits stated. T. and his family are written for this lesson.
