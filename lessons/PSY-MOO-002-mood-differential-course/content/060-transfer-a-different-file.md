# Transfer: a file where the good channel is the one nobody used

Different person, different shape. The method is the one you have.

T. is 61. T.'s partner died suddenly fourteen months ago. T. had a heart attack ten months ago. A beta blocker was started eight months ago and has continued since. T.'s daughter, who has come to the appointment, dates the change in her father to about nine months ago: low mood, loss of interest, waking early, and a flat quality she says is different from the first months after the death. T. does not drink. Neither T. nor his daughter recalls anything like this before. T.'s own summary is that he is old and sad now.

Four dates, in the order they happened: the death at fourteen months, the heart attack at ten, the mood change at nine, the beta blocker at eight.

:::{check}
:id: check-transfer-tasks

Work these four before reading the discussion. Written answers, five minutes.

1. Build the timeline and say which candidate the chronology supports and which it undercuts.
2. The daughter is in the room. Name two questions you would put to her that you cannot put to T., and say why each one has to go to her.
3. Give a state to each of: grief-related, depressive, medication, medical, bipolar-spectrum.
4. Name the single item that would move the ranking most, and say which way each possible answer would move it.
:::

**One.** The chronology cuts against the beta blocker as the thing that started this. The mood change is dated a month before the drug began. That is the same tool used on R.'s drinking, pointing the other way: there, an increase two months after the waking could not have started the waking; here, a drug started a month after the mood change could not have started the mood change. Neither result clears the substance. Beta blockers sit among the general medical drug classes associated with depressive symptoms, association is not causation in either direction, and the arguments that carry weight are temporal ones about starting, increasing, reducing and stopping. What the timeline does is retire one specific version of the medication claim and leave the weaker version standing. The heart attack, sitting a month before the mood change, is a temporal association of exactly the shape people find persuasive, and by itself it establishes nothing except that something else was happening then which nobody has yet listed.

**Two.** The daughter is the best channel in this file, and she is the only route to two questions T. cannot answer about himself. Whether the current state is a change from T.'s usual self, which is a comparison T. is poorly placed to make from inside it. And whether there has ever been a period of elevated, driven, or unusually active mood that T. would not think worth mentioning. In R.'s file that second question had no informant available and the assessment was limited by it. Here an informant is sitting in the room, and asking T. alone would reproduce R.'s limitation by choice.

**Three.** Grief-related is supported, with a real and dated loss. Depressive is supported, on the daughter's account of a change in quality after the early months. Those two are not competitors in the sense that only one may be true: prolonged grief is descriptively distinct from depression and from post-traumatic stress and the three co-occur often, so finding a death in the history is a reason to assess all three and not a reason to stop. Medication is entered, chronology against it as an initiator, open as a contributor. Medical is entered and thin: a heart attack at ten months means a hospital episode, a medication list, and a set of investigations that nobody in this room has looked at. Bipolar-spectrum is unassessed through the only channel that would answer it.

**Four.** Two answers are defensible and it is worth knowing why.

An account from the daughter of any past period of elevated or activated mood is the item that would change the category, not the weighting, and it costs one question. The full medication and investigation record from the cardiac admission is the item that would populate three alternatives at once, and it costs one request. Choose the first if you want the largest single move; choose the second if you want the largest reduction in how much of the file is unassessed. What is not defensible is leaving the appointment having asked T. about his mood and nobody about anything else.

**The trap in this case.** A death, then low mood, then a man who says he is old and sad. The pull is to write "bereavement" and stop, which turns a supported alternative into a conclusion and leaves four other alternatives unassessed on the grounds that one of them is plausible. The presence of a good explanation is not evidence against the others.

The point that many general medical drugs are associated with depressive symptoms and that attribution rests on temporal argument comes from a review of those drug classes. The limits of a person's own account of past episodes come from a review of depression preceding bipolar diagnosis and from the general-population recognition study. T. and his family are written for this lesson.
