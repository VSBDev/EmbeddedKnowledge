# Clinical wrap-up: shape the dinner-timing comparison

This is how the dinner-timing question earns a shape in time.

> **Illustrative teaching example—not medical advice.** This case tests study-design reasoning; it does not claim that dinner timing changes glucose or recommend a dinner schedule.

In the running teaching case about adults with type 2 diabetes, PREM-SCI-003 fixed dinner timing as the independent variable and next-morning fasting glucose as the dependent variable, while flagging possible confounders such as evening activity; carry that setup forward without re-deriving it.

## Contrast the candidate designs

**Habitual-times comparison.** Researchers recruit eligible participants, record whether they habitually eat dinner earlier or later, and then record next-morning fasting glucose. The participants' habits create the exposure difference; the research team does not assign it. Participants enter through an eligibility rule rather than through their glucose outcome, and the comparison runs from observed dinner timing to the next-morning outcome. This is an ordinary observational comparison with cohort timing. If the groups differ, the strongest warranted inference is an association between habitual dinner timing and next-morning fasting glucose in this sample, not a causal effect of changing dinner time.

**Within-person experiment (crossover).** The research team assigns each participant to eat earlier on some randomly ordered nights and later on others, then records the next-morning outcome after each assigned night. The research team creates the comparison, eligible participants enter before assignment, and time runs from assigned dinner timing to the later outcome. Each participant supplies both sides of the within-person comparison.

## Choose the best fit under the stated constraints

For this teaching case, choose the within-person experiment. The case states that dinner timing is manipulable and reversible, the outcome follows the next morning, and each participant can serve as their own comparison. Those constraints make repeated assigned conditions within each person a closer fit to the question than comparing different people's unassigned habits.

## Calibrate the inference

If the assigned dinner-time conditions produce different next-morning outcomes, the experiment can support a bounded causal inference about changing dinner timing for these participants under the studied conditions. The ordinary observational design supports the narrower association stated above because the research team did not create its exposure difference.

The material boundary is reach: a small within-person study speaks to these participants under these conditions, not automatically to all adults with type 2 diabetes.

The next lesson, **Sampling, randomization, and blinding**, decides who gets in, how nights are allocated, and who is kept unaware.
