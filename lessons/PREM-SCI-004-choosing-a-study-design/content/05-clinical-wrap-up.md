# Clinical wrap-up: shape the dinner-timing comparison

This is how the dinner-timing question earns a shape in time.

> **Illustrative teaching example—not medical advice.** This case tests study-design reasoning; it does not claim that dinner timing changes glucose or recommend a dinner schedule.

Use this fictional teaching setup: dinner timing is the exposure, next-morning fasting glucose is the outcome, and evening activity or other time-varying factors could differ across study nights.

## Contrast the candidate designs

**Habitual-times comparison.** Researchers recruit eligible participants, record whether they habitually eat dinner earlier or later, and then record next-morning fasting glucose. The participants' habits create the exposure difference; the research team does not assign it. Participants enter through an eligibility rule rather than through their glucose outcome, and the comparison runs from observed dinner timing to the next-morning outcome. This is an ordinary observational comparison with cohort timing. If the groups differ, the strongest warranted inference is an association between habitual dinner timing and next-morning fasting glucose in this sample, not a causal effect of changing dinner time.

**Within-person experiment (crossover).** The research team assigns each participant to eat earlier on some nights and later on others, randomly orders the assigned conditions, and records the next-morning outcome after each assigned night. The research team creates the comparison, eligible participants enter before assignment, and time runs from assigned dinner timing to the later outcome. Each participant receives both conditions and supplies both sides of the within-person comparison.

## Choose the best fit under the stated constraints

For this teaching case, the within-person experiment is the best fit only if assigning dinner times is ethically acceptable, clinically supervised, and feasible. The protocol would need to randomize condition order, support and assess adherence, measure the outcome comparably after each condition, and keep relevant co-interventions stable or address their changes. It would also need enough separation between conditions to make residual effects implausible, or another design-and-analysis plan for carryover, period, and sequence effects.

If those conditions cannot be met, investigators should choose another ethically acceptable design or narrow the inference. The habitual-times comparison remains available as an observational alternative.

## Calibrate the inference

If the assigned conditions, order, adherence, carryover and period plan, co-interventions, and outcome measurement are handled as stated, a difference in next-morning outcomes can support a bounded causal inference about changing dinner timing for these participants under the studied conditions. If those conditions fail, the inference must be narrower. The ordinary observational design supports the association stated above because the research team did not create its exposure difference.

The material boundary is reach: a small within-person study speaks to these participants under these conditions, not automatically to all adults with type 2 diabetes.

The next lesson, **Sampling, randomization, and blinding**, decides who gets in, how nights are allocated, and who is kept unaware.
