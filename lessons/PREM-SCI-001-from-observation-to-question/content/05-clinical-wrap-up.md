# Clinical wrap-up: from a bedside observation to a question

This is how a bedside observation becomes a structured scientific question.

**Teaching example—not medical advice.** This case is invented for reasoning practice. It does not claim that dinner timing changes glucose in real patients and must not be used to guide care.

In this teaching example, a clinician notices that, among several adults described as having type 2 diabetes, morning fasting glucose records tend to be higher on mornings after a late dinner than after an early dinner.

## 1. Keep the observation separate from the explanation

The raw observation is the pattern in the teaching record: dinner time was recorded as later or earlier, and next-morning fasting glucose was recorded as higher on several of the later-dinner mornings.

A tempting explanation would be, “Eating later caused the higher next-morning value.” That sentence adds a cause that the observation itself does not contain. Keep it separate from the record.

## 2. Construct one bounded, testable question

> Among the adults with type 2 diabetes described in this teaching example, how does recorded next-morning fasting glucose differ between mornings after a dinner recorded as later and mornings after a dinner recorded as earlier?

The question names the cases covered by the teaching record, the dinner-time comparison, the glucose record, and the next-morning boundary. It asks about a difference without claiming that dinner time caused it.

## 3. Evaluate testability without designing the study

- **Measurable variables:** recorded dinner time and recorded next-morning fasting glucose. “Later,” “earlier,” and “next morning” would need explicit recording rules.
- **Feasible comparison:** the question identifies two cases that could be compared—mornings after later dinners and mornings after earlier dinners.
- **Open outcome:** the records could show higher values, lower values, or no clear difference. More than one answer is possible.

Stop here. The lesson has produced an empirically answerable question, not a study plan or a clinical conclusion.

Later lessons turn this question into hypotheses, a design, and endpoints.
