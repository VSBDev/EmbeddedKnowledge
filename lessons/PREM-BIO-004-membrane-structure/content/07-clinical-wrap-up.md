# Clinical wrap-up: a drug that can only tell two membranes apart by their sterol

:::{callout}
:kind: boundary
:id: callout-teaching-boundary

Teaching example, not medical advice. Nothing here is a dose, an indication, a contraindication, a monitoring plan, or a recommendation about any patient. No dose, threshold, regimen, or duration appears anywhere in this lesson. This is a reasoning exercise about membrane composition, worked with the tools the lesson has already built.
:::

The target of amphotericin B is a lipid, and it is the lipid the third scene spent half its length on.

Fungi build their membranes with **ergosterol** where human cells build theirs with **cholesterol**. Both are sterols, both sit among the hydrocarbon chains, and both do the same structural job. They are not the same molecule, and the difference is enough to hang a drug on.

## Step one: what is the target

Amphotericin B interacts with ergosterol rather than with an enzyme. Two accounts of what follows are current. In one, the drug and the sterol together form pores through which ions leak. In the other, the drug assembles into large aggregates sitting outside the membrane and pulls ergosterol out of the bilayer into them, which is described as a sterol sponge. The disagreement is not only about the route. In the pore account the drug and the sterol build an ion-conducting structure together and the sterol stays in the membrane as part of it; in the sponge account the sterol leaves. Only the second ends in a membrane that no longer has the sterol it was built around.

Take the sponge account for a moment, because it is the one this lesson's model speaks to. A membrane stripped of its sterol has lost the component that restricts chain movement, obstructs the phase transition, and holds down permeability to small water-soluble molecules. Those consequences are consequences of membrane behaviour, and you can predict their direction from the third scene without knowing anything else about fungal biology.

The pore account arrives at leakage by a different road: the structure the drug and the sterol form is itself the hole. Both accounts predict a membrane that leaks, which is why the disagreement has been hard to settle from the outcome alone. They do not predict the same membrane. Keep the two apart when you reason forward from either, because a prediction that starts from sterol removal is a prediction about only one of them.

## Step two: why it is more toxic to a fungus than to us

Two reasons, and they are different in kind.

The first is chemical. Three interactions bind the drug to ergosterol: van der Waals forces, a network of hydrogen bonds, and an electronic interaction between the ergosterol side chain and the drug's polyene tail. That third attachment point does not form when the drug binds cholesterol.

The second is compositional. Fungal membranes carry a higher ratio of ergosterol to phospholipid than mammalian membranes carry of cholesterol to phospholipid. Even a drug with no chemical preference would find more target per unit area in the fungus.

:::{check}
:id: check-selectivity-is-a-ratio
:kind: transfer

The drug's affinity for ergosterol is about ten times its affinity for cholesterol.

1. Does a tenfold preference mean human membranes are unaffected? Answer before reading on, and say what a tenfold preference *does* mean.
2. Human cells are exposed to this drug. Predict, from the third scene alone, one direction in which their membrane behaviour changes.
3. What would have to be true of a sterol for a drug like this to be entirely selective?
:::

A tenfold preference means the drug binds ergosterol about ten times more readily. It does not mean it leaves cholesterol alone, and non-selective disruption of mammalian cell membranes does occur. A ratio is not a switch, and reading a selectivity figure as though it were one is the single most common error in thinking about drug toxicity.

For the second part: removing cholesterol from a membrane removes what was holding down permeability to small water-soluble molecules, so that membrane leaks more than it did. Predicting the direction is available to you; predicting which tissue suffers most is not, and that is a question about exposure, which this lesson does not cover.

For the third part, the drug would need a binding requirement that the human sterol cannot satisfy at all, rather than one it satisfies less well. The electronic interaction with the ergosterol side chain is close to that: it is described as absent with cholesterol. What stops the drug being entirely selective is that the other two interactions are not.

## Step three: where the toxicity lands, and why formulation helps

The kidney takes the worst of it. Two contributions are described: the drug constricts the afferent arterioles that feed the filtering units, which lowers renal blood flow and filtration; and cells take the drug up through low-density-lipoprotein receptors, which raises how much of it they see. Separately, mononuclear immune cells recognise the drug through two surface receptors, which is the route to the reactions that can accompany an infusion.

Notice that only the first of those is a membrane-composition story. The second is a delivery story, and it is why formulation changes the picture. Preparations in which the drug is associated with lipid show less kidney toxicity than the older deoxycholate preparation, and the liposomal one also produces fewer infusion reactions. Nothing about the drug's affinity for either sterol has changed. What changed is where the drug goes and what it is presented in.

That distinction between *what a molecule binds* and *where a molecule ends up* is worth carrying forward. This lesson can predict the first and cannot predict the second.

## Step four: resistance, which turns the same dial

The commonest way a fungus escapes this drug is by altering the sterol composition of its own membrane. Loss of function in the genes of the ergosterol pathway, *ERG11* and *ERG3* among them, replaces ergosterol with other sterols: lanosterol, eburicol, 4,14-dimethyl-zymosterol.

The fungus is doing exactly what the third scene described a cell doing, and for a different reason. Composition is the adjustable part of a membrane, so composition is what changes under selection, and a drug that targets a compositional feature is a drug whose target can be composed differently.

This is where the lesson leaves you with a question instead of an answer. Sterols are not interchangeable structurally; ergosterol and lanosterol are different molecules and this lesson has given you every reason to expect them to behave differently among the hydrocarbon chains. Whether a resistant strain pays for its substitution in altered membrane behaviour is a real research question and the review cited here does not answer it. Being able to formulate that question precisely, from a sterol swap to a prediction about chain packing, is what the last five scenes were for.
