# One cell, two membranes, and a fence between them

An absorptive cell in the crypt has a lumen on one side and the rest of the body on the other. Those are different problems, so the cell solves them with different membranes.

A note on where the measurements come from before they are used. Most of the detailed work on brush border lipid composition was done on small-intestinal epithelium, where the microvilli are longest and the tissue purifies cleanly, and some of it in pig. The colonic absorptive cell this block follows carries a shorter version of the same apical specialisation. The argument below is about how a polarised cell organises two membranes, and that argument is not specific to a segment or a species. Where a figure comes from another tissue, the text says so.

## The two faces

:::{diagram} ../diagrams/enterocyte-two-faces.diagram.json
:alt: One absorptive intestinal cell with an apical membrane facing the lumen, a basolateral membrane facing the tissue beneath, and a tight junction between them acting as a fence in the outer layer of the membrane.
:longdesc: The map places the gut lumen at the top, carrying bile salts, pancreatic digestive enzymes and bacteria, and the tissue side at the bottom with neighbouring cells and the basement membrane. Between them sits one cell with two different membranes. The apical membrane faces the lumen, is thrown into microvilli about one micrometre long and about one hundred nanometres across, and its brush border raises apical surface area roughly thirtyfold. Its lipid is unusually rich in glycolipids, over thirty per cent in pig against roughly five per cent in a typical cell membrane, and those glycolipids with cholesterol and sphingomyelin form liquid-ordered domains in the outer layer, which is what gives the membrane stability against the lumen. The basolateral membrane faces the tissue, carries a different lipid and protein set, and handles contact with neighbouring cells and the basement membrane. The tight junction between them acts as a fence in the outer layer only: a labelled lipid in the outer layer of the apical membrane does not reach the basolateral side, while the same label in the inner layer redistributes immediately. Measured bilayer thickness differs between the faces of a polarised cell, about 42.5 angstroms apical against about 35.6 angstroms basolateral, measured phosphate to phosphate in rat liver, with membrane protein, and not cholesterol, accounting for the difference.
:::

The apical and basolateral membranes of an intestinal epithelial cell are biochemically and functionally distinct, each with its own set of proteins and its own set of lipids. The apical membrane faces the lumen and does two jobs: it keeps pathogens out, and it processes and takes up nutrients. The basolateral membrane faces the neighbouring cells and the basement membrane, and its business is with them.

## The apical membrane is built for a chemically violent neighbourhood

Look at what is on the other side of it. The lumen delivers bile salts, which are detergents, along with the digestive enzymes of the pancreas and bacteria pressed against the surface in enormous numbers. A membrane made to the standard specification would not last there.

The brush border is not made to the standard specification. Its lipid is extraordinarily rich in glycolipids, which exceed 30 per cent of brush border lipid in pig against roughly 5 per cent of total lipid in a typical cell membrane. Glycolipids, cholesterol and sphingomyelin together promote liquid-ordered domains in the outer layer, and in the brush border those domains are thought to be relatively large and stable. That organisation gives the membrane physical and chemical stability against exactly the agents the lumen delivers.

Read that against the previous scene and the picture completes itself. The layer facing the lumen is the layer stiffened by ordered lipid; the layer facing the cytoplasm is the one carrying the signalling lipids. One membrane, two layers, two different jobs, held apart by physics that resists mixing.

The geometry is worth a moment too. A microvillus is about a micrometre long and about a hundred nanometres across, and thousands of them raise the apical surface area of the cell about thirtyfold. The membrane wrapped around one of those cylinders is 5 nanometres thick: twenty times thinner than the cylinder is wide. Lesson 07 takes up what holds a microvillus upright, which is a cytoskeletal question and not a membrane one.

## How two fluid membranes stay different

Here is the difficulty. Both membranes are fluid, both are continuous with each other around the top of the cell, and a lipid in the plane crosses a cell in about two minutes. Nothing computed so far explains why the apical composition does not simply flow into the basolateral one.

The answer is a fence, and the experiment that found it is clean enough to reconstruct.

:::{investigation}
:id: investigation-tight-junction-fence

**Setup.** Take a polarised epithelial monolayer, in this case a kidney cell line grown as a sheet. Deliver a fluorescent phospholipid specifically into the apical membrane by fusing a liposome with it, then watch whether the label reaches the basolateral surface.

**The variable that mattered.** Run it twice with liposomes built differently. In the first, the fluorescent lipid sits mostly in the liposome's outer layer, so fusion delivers it to the outer layer of the apical membrane. In the second, the label is in both layers, so fusion delivers it to both layers of the apical membrane.

**Result.** From the first, the label did not move to the basolateral side. From the second, redistribution to the basolateral membrane happened immediately.

**What it shows.** The tight junction blocks diffusion of that phospholipid in the outer layer of the plasma membrane and does not block it in the cytoplasmic layer. The barrier is one-sided. That single asymmetry explains how a composition difference survives in a structure whose components are otherwise free to move.

**What it does not show.** One labelled lipid in one cell line is not every lipid in every epithelium, and a fence that holds the outer layer says nothing about how proteins are delivered to the correct face in the first place. Sorting during delivery is a separate mechanism from retention after arrival.
:::

Protein is held apart the same way. Some plasma-membrane enzymes and transport proteins are confined to the apical surface and others to the basal and lateral surfaces, and that separation of both protein and lipid is attributed at least in part to the tight junction. Lesson 07 is where a junction stops being a line on a diagram and becomes a set of proteins.

## The two faces are physically different, and still one membrane

Earlier in this scene the two faces were continuous with each other around the top of the cell, and that has not changed: apical and basolateral are domains of one plasma membrane, not two membranes. What the domains are is physically different, and the strongest single piece of evidence for that is that you can measure the difference.

Solution X-ray scattering across purified membranes from rat liver gives the distance from the phosphate groups on one side to those on the other. Apical plasma membrane came out at 42.5 ± 0.3 Å, basolateral at 35.6 ± 0.6 Å, with the Golgi at 39.5 ± 0.4 Å and the endoplasmic reticulum at 37.5 ± 0.4 Å. The two faces of one polarised cell differ in bilayer thickness by around 7 Å, which is close to 20 per cent. A continuous sheet whose two ends differ by a fifth in thickness is a strong result: it says the difference between the domains is structural and not only a matter of which proteins are parked where.

Two cautions come with that number. It is a hepatocyte measurement, so the apical membrane in question is a bile canalicular one and not an intestinal brush border; the general finding is that a polarised cell's faces are physically different, and the intestinal specifics come from the composition work quoted above. And the phosphate-to-phosphate distance is smaller than the membrane's overall 5-nanometre thickness because it stops at the phosphates instead of at the outer edge of the head groups. When a source gives a number, check where it put the edge.

The same study found that removing cholesterol changed thickness by at most 1 Å, while removing protein changed it by 4 to 5 Å. Membrane protein modulates the thickness of these membranes; cholesterol, at least in this measurement, does not. That is a useful corrective to the previous scene, where cholesterol was doing all the work. Cholesterol governs how the chains move. Protein governs how far apart the two sets of head groups sit.

## Where this leaves the block

The membrane is the barrier for everything, and it is organised rather than merely present. Every one of those questions assumes what this lesson established: two molecules of lipid, fluid in the plane, effectively solid across it, and sorted.
