# Stiffer, looser, and the one that works both ways

Fluidity is not one fixed property that membranes either have or lack. It varies with composition and with temperature, it varies between one membrane and another in the same cell, and a cell that loses control of it is a cell in difficulty. This scene names what moves it and in which direction.

A caution about the word first. *Fluidity* here is a qualitative summary of how easily the hydrocarbon chains move and how loosely they pack. The measurable quantities behind it are viscosity and chain order, and those are what researchers report; no instrument returns a number called fluidity. Treat the word as a direction of travel, which is all this outcome asks you to predict.

## Cool a bilayer far enough and it freezes

A bilayer held above a certain temperature is liquid: chains bending, lipids sliding past one another at the rate the previous scene computed. Cool it past that temperature and it changes into a rigid crystalline state, with the chains packed straight and parallel. That change of state is called a **phase transition**, and the temperature at which it happens depends on what the chains are like.

Two features of the chain set it, and they push the same way.

**Chain length.** Shorter chains have less surface along which to interact with their neighbours, so they hold together less strongly and the bilayer stays liquid to a lower temperature. Longer chains raise the transition temperature.

**Unsaturation.** A *cis* double bond puts a kink in an otherwise straight chain, and kinked chains do not pack tightly against each other. More *cis* double bonds means a lower transition temperature and a membrane that stays liquid in the cold.

| Change | Effect on the membrane | Mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Longer hydrocarbon chains | Less fluid at a given temperature | More chain-to-chain interaction along a longer contact |
| More *cis* double bonds | More fluid at a given temperature | Kinks obstruct tight packing |
| Lower temperature | Less fluid, and below the transition the bilayer freezes | Less thermal motion in the chains |
| More cholesterol | Two-sided; see below | Rigid ring system among flexible chains |

## Cholesterol does two opposite things and neither is a compromise

Cholesterol is the awkward entry in that table and the one worth getting right, because a learner who memorises a single direction for it will answer half the questions backwards.

Wedged among the hydrocarbon chains, cholesterol's rigid ring system restricts their movement, so a fluid bilayer with cholesterol in it is less fluid than the same bilayer without. That is the first effect and it is the one most people remember.

At the concentrations actually found in eukaryotic plasma membranes, cholesterol also gets in the way of the chains coming together and crystallising. It obstructs the phase transition itself. A membrane loaded with cholesterol is therefore harder to freeze as well as harder to disturb, and across a range of temperatures its behaviour changes less than the same membrane without cholesterol would.

The resolution is that cholesterol is not a fluidity dial with a direction. It is a buffer against change in either direction. Ask what it does and the honest answer is a question back: change relative to what starting state, and at what temperature.

One consequence matters later. Cholesterol makes the bilayer less deformable in the region where it sits, and a less deformable bilayer is less permeable to small water-soluble molecules. Composition therefore sets barrier tightness, before any transport protein is involved at all. Lesson 05 picks that up.

## Cells hold their membranes at a working viscosity

Organisms do not let membrane viscosity float with the temperature. They adjust the lipids they build so that viscosity stays roughly where it works, a homeostatic behaviour named **homeoviscous adaptation**. The adjustments are the ones already in the table: the proportion of chains that disrupt packing, such as unsaturated or branched ones, and the length of the chains.

The clearest demonstration is bacterial. An *Escherichia coli* strain lacking the enzyme that extends fatty-acid chains grew badly in one growth medium because its membrane fluidity fell, and growth recovered when the missing lipid was supplied. Take that as evidence that the regulation is load-bearing. The study tested no human cell, so it says nothing about one. The ability to adjust membrane composition with temperature is broadly conserved across living things, which is the general statement the evidence supports.

Notice which dial a crypt cell actually holds. It does not set the temperature it works at. What it can change is what it builds its membranes out of, which is why every regulatory mechanism in this area is compositional.

For each change, say whether the membrane becomes more fluid, less fluid, or whether the answer depends on something you should name.

1. Replacing a saturated tail with one carrying two *cis* double bonds.
2. Dropping the temperature by 15 °C.
3. Doubling the cholesterol content.
4. Replacing a 16-carbon tail with an 18-carbon tail.

The first makes it more fluid: kinks stop the chains packing. The second makes it less fluid, and if the drop crosses the transition temperature the bilayer freezes instead of merely stiffening. The fourth makes it less fluid: a longer chain interacts with its neighbours along more of its length.

The third is the one that depends. Above the transition temperature more cholesterol restricts chain movement, so the membrane is less fluid. Approaching or below the transition, cholesterol obstructs crystallisation, so the membrane is more fluid than it would otherwise have been. Naming the starting state is part of the answer, and an answer that gives one direction without naming it is incomplete even when the direction happens to be right.
