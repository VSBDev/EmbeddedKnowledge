# The lumen is on the other side of five nanometres

Conventional light microscopy separates detail down to about 250 nanometres across the specimen. The boundary that makes a crypt cell a cell is about 5 nanometres thick, fifty times finer than that. Anyone who draws a cell outline from a light micrograph is drawing a line finer than the instrument can resolve, and what that line is made of has to be argued from chemistry and from measurements taken by other means.

Five nanometres is two molecules thick. Two facing layers of lipid, and on the far side of them, in the gut, the lumen.

The word **membrane** arrives from ordinary English already meaning something, and the ordinary meaning is not wrong so much as thin. A drum skin is a membrane; so is the film inside an eggshell. All of them are sheets that separate one space from another, which is the part biology keeps. From here on the word carries three things the everyday sense does not: the sheet is exactly two molecules of lipid thick, it behaves as a liquid within its own plane, and it decides what crosses it. The **plasma membrane** is the one enclosing the whole cell, which is the membrane this lesson is about.

## What this lesson assumes, and where it comes from

In the course graph this outcome sits downstream of **Lipids and membranes (PREM-09.05)**, which is where lipid chemistry is taught. No lesson has been written for that outcome yet. What follows therefore carries its own short version and does not build a phospholipid from first principles; when PREM-09.05 exists, read it first and this section becomes a reminder.

Three things are assumed. A **phospholipid** has a water-attracting head containing a phosphate group and two water-avoiding hydrocarbon tails, which makes one molecule with two incompatible halves. A tail is **saturated** when every carbon-to-carbon bond along it is single, and **unsaturated** when it carries one or more double bonds; a *cis* double bond puts a permanent kink in the chain. **Cholesterol** is a small, rigid, largely water-avoiding molecule carrying a single hydroxyl group at one end. Nothing later in this lesson needs more lipid chemistry than those three sentences.

## Nothing holds one lipid to the next

There is no bond between neighbouring phospholipids in a membrane. The sheet is held together by the water on either side of it.

A water-avoiding molecule placed in water forces the surrounding water molecules to reorganise into ordered cage-like arrangements, and forming those cages costs free energy. The cost falls when the water-avoiding parts cluster together, because clustering leaves the smallest number of water molecules affected. Phospholipids therefore aggregate without being told to, tails buried inward and heads exposed to water, and a bilayer is the shape that aggregation takes for a molecule with a bulky head and two tails.

Three consequences follow, and between them they are most of what a membrane can do.

**A bilayer cannot have an edge.** A free edge exposes buried tails to water, which is the arrangement the whole assembly is avoiding. The only way to have no edge is to close on itself and form a sealed compartment. A cell is a bag because a flat square of bilayer is not a stable object.

**A bilayer repairs itself.** A small tear creates that same free edge, so the lipids rearrange spontaneously to remove it. No signal, no enzyme and no repair programme is involved. The hole closes because an open hole is the expensive state.

**A bilayer can be taken apart and put back together.** Pinching a vesicle off one membrane and fusing it into another leaves nothing broken, because there was no bonded lattice to break. Vesicular transport in lesson 05 depends on that.

Lipid is not the bulk of the membrane by mass. Protein accounts for about half the mass of a typical plasma membrane, dropping below a quarter in the myelin that insulates axons and rising to about three quarters in the inner membranes of mitochondria. Read those three figures next to what each membrane does: the one whose main job is electrical insulation carries the least protein, and the one running ATP production carries the most.

The lipid itself is not one substance either. Sterols alone can be up to 40 per cent of the total lipid in a plasma membrane, and the phospholipids run to hundreds of chemically distinct species. The diagram above draws one repeating lipid because a diagram has to; treat that as notation.

Numbers help here. There are roughly $5\times10^{6}$ lipid molecules in a one-micrometre square of bilayer, and about $10^{9}$ in the plasma membrane of a small animal cell. A billion components, no bonds between them, and a barrier that has to hold for the four or five days an absorptive cell spends climbing the crypt before it is shed.

That is the problem the rest of the lesson is about.
