# Older, simpler, lesser: three words that do not follow

Commit to an answer before reading on. True or false, and why?

1. Prokaryotic cells are an earlier form that eukaryotic cells improved on.
2. Prokaryotic cells have no internal compartments.
3. If a cell is large, it is eukaryotic.

Most people who meet this topic answer true, true, true. The wording of the vocabulary encourages it, and so does the order textbooks present things in. Each answer is wrong, and the third is wrong in a way that reveals what the other two are doing.

## The model to take apart

Here is the idea in its most reasonable form, stated as someone holding it would state it. Life began simple. Early cells had no internal structure, just DNA and enzymes loose in a bag. Later, a compartment appeared around the DNA, and that innovation made everything else possible: bigger cells, more genes, specialisation, multicellularity. Prokaryotes are what remains of the first stage; eukaryotes are the second stage. The prefix says so.

Nothing about that story is stupid. It has a beginning, a middle, and a mechanism, and parts of it are even true. What it gets wrong is the shape of the claim: it converts a structural description into a ranking, and a ranking implies a ladder that the evidence does not support.

## Three things the ranking cannot account for

**A group defined by an absence need not be a branch.** *Prokaryote* covers two collections of organisms, bacteria and archaea. In 1990 Woese, Kandler and Wheelis argued from molecular sequence data that the living world divides primarily into three **domains**, Bacteria, Archaea and Eucarya, and that neither the five-kingdom scheme nor the prokaryote–eukaryote dichotomy captures that primary division. A domain in this sense is a rank in classification, sitting above kingdom; it is a different use of the word from the domain of a function in [Functions and graphs (PREM-QNT-007)](https://embeddedknowledge.io/premed/lessons/read/?lesson=PREM-QNT-007), and the two senses share nothing but the spelling. Work since has gone further: phylogenomic analyses place eukaryotes as emerging from within the Archaea, with the Asgard archaea as their closest prokaryotic relatives. Read that carefully. If eukaryotes arose from inside one of the two groups that "prokaryote" collects, then "prokaryote" is not a limb of the tree with eukaryotes on a different limb. It is a description of cells that lack a particular feature, and organisms lacking a feature can sit anywhere.

**Prokaryotic cells build compartments.** Bacteria are routinely described as having organelles. Some are membrane-bound: anammoxosomes, magnetosomes, chromatophores, chlorosomes. Others are built from protein rather than lipid, and the best studied is the carboxysome, which encloses the enzymes of carbon fixation behind a selectively permeable protein shell. Concentrating a reaction, keeping a toxic intermediate away from the rest of the cell, controlling what diffuses in and out: prokaryotes do all of it. The thing they lack is specifically an envelope around the chromosome.

**Size is a tendency with a spectacular exception.** *Candidatus* Thiomargarita magnifica reaches about 2 cm in length and 50 µm across. Two centimetres is roughly ten thousand times the length of the rod modelled in scene 2, and longer than a great many animals. Read the second half of that description too: in this organism, one or more chromosomes sit inside membrane-bound compartments called pepins.

That last fact should bother you, and it is supposed to. A criterion stated as a bright line has met an organism that sits on the line, which is what happens to structural criteria when biology is sampled widely enough. What biologists did in response is the instructive part. They did not reclassify it as a eukaryote. It is a bacterium because of what its genome and ribosomal RNA say about its ancestry, and ancestry is settled by sequence comparison rather than by counting membranes. The architecture criterion from scene 1 describes how a cell is built. It was never the thing that decides who is related to whom, and treating a description of construction as a verdict on lineage is the root error inside all three false statements above.

## What the criterion actually licenses

:::{diagram} ../diagrams/what-the-criterion-licenses.diagram.json
:alt: A single structural test branches into two cell types, then into consequences that follow directly, a branch for what the arrangement makes possible rather than causes, tendencies that usually hold, one conclusion that does not follow at all, and a branch for what the test does not settle.
:longdesc: The test at the left is the working criterion: is a cell's DNA enclosed by a membrane envelope of its own? Yes gives a eukaryotic cell with its nuclear DNA inside a nuclear envelope; no gives a prokaryotic cell with its DNA in the cytoplasm as a nucleoid. Two consequences follow directly, because they concern where transcription and translation can happen: in a eukaryotic cell the two sit in separate compartments for the genes in the nucleus, so a transcript cannot be read until it has crossed the envelope, and in a prokaryotic cell they share one compartment so a ribosome can read a transcript still being made. A separate branch off the eukaryotic side is labelled made possible rather than follows, and holds processing, quality control and selective export, which the separation opens up but does not cause, since each needs machinery the envelope does not supply. A branch off the test itself records what the test does not settle at all: which groups a cell belongs to, decided by ancestry, and the bacterium whose chromosomes sit inside membrane-bound compartments. Two further boxes are marked as tendencies with exceptions rather than rules: prokaryotic cells are usually smaller, with a centimetre-long bacterium as the counterexample, and usually divide faster, with the tuberculosis bacillus as the counterexample. A final box, reached from both cell types along edges labelled does not follow, holds the conclusions the test cannot support: older, simpler, or less capable. The tiers are the point. How far a statement sits from the test is how much weight it can carry, and two of the branches exist to mark statements that sit close to the test and still do not follow from it.
:::

Use it as a sorting rule. When you meet a claim about the two cell types, ask which tier it belongs to.

- **Does it follow from where the DNA sits?** Then it is safe.
- **Is it something the arrangement makes possible, needing machinery of its own?** Then it holds where somebody has checked that the machinery is there. Transcript quality control is the example from the last scene: the envelope opens the possibility and does not do the work.
- **Is it a tendency?** Then it holds until you meet the exception, and you have now met two.
- **Is it about rank, age, or worth?** Then the criterion has nothing to say, and neither should you.

And one thing sits outside the tiers altogether, which is what group an organism belongs to. That is ancestry, and the giant bacterium above is the proof: the test answers *enclosed* for it and it is a bacterium anyway.

:::{misconception}
:id: misconception-prokaryote-means-primitive

**The wrong model.** Prokaryotic cells are the primitive first draft; the nucleus is the upgrade that produced everything since.

**What it cannot explain.** Eukaryotes arise from within the Archaea, so the two groups the term collects are not a single lineage that eukaryotes departed from. Bacteria build compartments, including membrane-bound organelles and protein-shelled ones. A bacterium exists that is 2 cm long with chromosomes in membrane-bound compartments.

**The model that survives.** The prokaryote–eukaryote distinction describes one architectural feature: whether a membrane envelope encloses the DNA. It predicts where transcription and translation can happen. It correlates loosely with cell size and division rate. It says nothing about age, complexity, sophistication, or evolutionary rank, and it is not how relatedness is established.
:::

:::{check}
:id: check-classify-the-new-organism
:kind: retrieval

A constructed case, written for this exercise rather than taken from a real report. A microbe recovered from deep-sea sediment is described as follows.

- Cells about 8 µm across.
- A single circular chromosome lying free in the cytoplasm.
- An internal compartment with a protein shell, concentrating one enzyme.
- A doubling time of about 30 hours.

Classify it, then say which of the four descriptions you used and why the other three were set aside.
:::

It is prokaryotic, and only the second description was needed: the chromosome lies free in the cytoplasm, so no membrane envelope encloses it.

The other three are distractors of three different kinds. The 8 µm width is a tendency running the wrong way, and tendencies do not classify; the centimetre-long bacterium already showed that a large prokaryotic cell is possible. The protein-shelled compartment is not a nucleus and never was, so it is irrelevant to a criterion about the DNA specifically; carboxysomes are exactly this. The 30-hour doubling time is another tendency running the wrong way, and the next scene is built around a real organism that behaves the same way. If you found yourself weighing the four facts against each other and taking a majority verdict, that is the habit to break. One of them is the one the criterion reads and three of them are noise. Note the careful wording: the criterion reads the DNA's location, and that is what makes it the relevant fact here. It is not that the location defines what the organism is, which is the thing the giant bacterium settled.

The three-domain proposal and the statement that neither the five-kingdom taxonomy nor the eukaryote–prokaryote dichotomy reflects the primary division of the living world come from the 1990 paper that named Archaea, Bacteria and Eucarya. The placement of eukaryotes within the Archaea, with Asgard archaea as their closest prokaryotic relatives, comes from a 2018 phylogenomic paper defending the two-domain topology; that placement remains an active research area rather than a closed question, and the paper is written as a reply to a competing interpretation. A 2021 review of the carboxysome supplies the list of membrane-bound bacterial organelles and the description of the carboxysome's selectively permeable protein shell. The maximum size of about 2 cm in length and 50 µm across for *Candidatus* Thiomargarita magnifica, and the statement that one or more of its chromosomes sit in membrane-bound organelles called pepins, come from a 2023 review of giant bacteria; the primary description of that organism was published in 2022 and is cited by that review. The structural criterion itself, and the contrast between a nucleoid and a nucleus, come from a cell-biology textbook chapter. The deep-sea organism in the final exercise is constructed for teaching and describes no real report.
