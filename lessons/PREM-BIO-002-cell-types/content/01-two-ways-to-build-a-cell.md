# Two ways to build a cell, in contact all day

Scrape the inside of a colon and two populations come off on the same slide. One is the lining: tall cells joined edge to edge, each roughly 20 µm from base to tip. The other is whatever was in the tube, and it is mostly bacteria, rods and spheres about a tenth that length. Seconds earlier they were touching. A colon carries something like $3.8\times10^{13}$ bacteria, the same order of magnitude as the number of cells in the whole body, and the only thing between that population and your tissue is an epithelium one cell thick.

[Cell theory, scale, and emergence (PREM-BIO-001)](https://embeddedknowledge.io/premed/lessons/read/?lesson=PREM-BIO-001) built the crypt that supplies those lining cells and worked out why nobody could see any of this before lenses. This lesson stays at the same surface and asks a different question. Those two kinds of cell are built to different plans. What is the difference, what does each plan make easy, and what does each one cost?

## One test, and everything else is downstream

Textbooks list a dozen differences between the two kinds of cell. Almost all of them are consequences or correlations. One is definitional.

:::{definition}
:id: definition-two-architectures
:label: Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells

Ask a single question of any cell: **is its DNA enclosed inside a membrane envelope of its own, separate from the rest of the cell's interior?**

A **eukaryotic** cell answers yes. The envelope and the DNA it encloses are together called the **cell nucleus**. Your colonic lining cells are eukaryotic, as is every cell in every animal, plant, fungus, and protist.

A **prokaryotic** cell answers no. Its DNA sits in the same continuous interior as everything else, in a dense region called the **nucleoid** that is not walled off by a membrane. Bacteria are prokaryotic, and so are archaea, a second and separately named group you will meet again in this lesson.

One thing to fix before you use it. This is the working criterion, not a definition of what these groups *are*. It is how the two words are applied to almost every cell you will meet, it is reliable there, and it is what the rest of this lesson reasons from. It is also known to fail. One bacterium keeps its chromosomes inside membrane-bound compartments and is a bacterium anyway; scene 4 takes that case seriously, because what it shows is that membership is settled by ancestry while this test describes construction. So use the test to reason about how a cell is built. Do not use it to decide what a cell is related to, and do not expect it to answer for every cell in a body.

Everything else you will read about the two kinds of cell either follows from this answer, tends to go with it, or has nothing to do with it, and telling those three apart is most of the work here.
:::

Two ordinary words shift meaning as you cross into this vocabulary, and both will catch you out if nobody says so.

**Nucleus** in everyday English means the middle, the core, the important bit. In a cell it means a specific membrane-bounded compartment with DNA inside it. A prokaryotic cell has a middle and has DNA; it has no nucleus.

**Prokaryote** is worse, because its etymology is an argument. It was assembled from Greek roots meaning *before* and *kernel*, so the word itself asserts that these cells came first and that the nucleus was the upgrade. Hold that claim loosely. A later scene takes it apart properly, and it is the single most common wrong idea in this topic.

## What the inside of a prokaryotic cell looks like

:::{figure} ../assets/prokaryote-cell-labelled.png
:alt: Cutaway drawing of a rod-shaped bacterial cell with the cytoplasm opened, showing a tangled loop of DNA labelled nucleoid, scattered ribosomes, a small plasmid loop, and layered capsule, cell wall and plasma membrane, plus pili and a flagellum.
:longdesc: A rod-shaped cell is drawn cut open along its length. Three layers wrap the outside: an outer capsule, a cell wall beneath it, and the plasma membrane innermost. The interior is one continuous space, labelled cytoplasm, with no compartment inside it. A long tangled loop labelled nucleoid, and annotated as circular DNA, lies free in that cytoplasm, surrounded by the same fluid as everything else and separated from it by nothing. Small dots scattered throughout are labelled ribosomes, and they sit in the same space as the DNA rather than in a compartment of their own. A separate small closed loop is labelled plasmid. Short projections around the outside are labelled pili, and one long whip at one end is labelled flagellum. The teaching point is what is absent: no membrane runs between the DNA and the ribosomes, so a ribosome can reach a transcript the moment it is made.
:::

That is a generic prokaryotic cell, opened along its length, with the nucleoid and the ribosomes sharing one continuous interior. Read it for the negative space. The DNA is a loop, usually circular, lying loose in the cytoplasm. The ribosomes, which are the machines that build protein from a transcript, are scattered through the same fluid. Nothing separates them. Compare that with a lining cell, where a transcript made in the nucleus has to be finished, checked, and moved out through the envelope before any ribosome touches it.

The other labels are there so the drawing is readable, and this lesson sources none of them beyond what it names. The **cell wall** outside the membrane is rigid, and that is the only one of them this lesson uses. A **capsule** is the layer outside the wall, a **plasmid** is a separate small DNA loop, **pili** are the short surface projections, and the **flagellum** is the long whip at one end. What each of those does, and how a plasmid moves between cells, is microbiology and is not established anywhere in this pack. Take the words as labels for now.

## What this lesson does and does not cover

This lesson works on the contrast and its consequences: size, exchange, mixing, division rate, and what a compartment is worth. It does not open the eukaryotic interior. The nucleus is named here and left as a box with a membrane around it. Lesson 03 of this module is where the endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi, mitochondria, and the rest get their proper treatment, and lesson 04 does the membrane itself. Wait for those instead of half-learning them here.

:::{check}
:id: check-recall-crypt-numbers
:kind: retrieval

Before going on, recover three things from PREM-BIO-001 without looking them up.

1. How thick is the colonic epithelium, in cells?
2. Roughly how long does a colonic absorptive cell work at the surface before it is shed?
3. Which side of the epithelium does the bacterial population sit on?
:::

The epithelium is one cell thick. Much of the colonic lining turns over on a three-to-five-day timescale, with absorptive cells lasting about four to five days. The bacteria are in the lumen, the open space inside the tube, on the far side of that single layer. Hold all three: a sheet one cell thick, replacing itself every few days, with a bacterial population of order $10^{13}$ pressed against it. By the end of this lesson you will be able to say why the two sides of that sheet run on such different clocks.
