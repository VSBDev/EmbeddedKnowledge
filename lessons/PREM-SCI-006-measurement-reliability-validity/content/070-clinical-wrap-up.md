# Clinical wrap-up: make two diabetes measures earn their names

This is how “dinner time” and “fasting glucose” become numbers that earn their names.

**Teaching example—not medical advice.** This case and its measurement records are invented for reasoning practice. It does not claim that dinner timing affects glucose in real patients and must not be used to guide care.

PREM-SCI-005 already set entry, allocation, and blinding for the fictional crossover: the exposure is dinner timing, and the outcome is next-morning fasting glucose. Here, do not re-audit that design. Audit only how those two quantities become records.

## Repair the two operational definitions

Start with **dinner time**.

- **Target:** choose the clock time of a named event, such as the start or end of the designated dinner. “When dinner happened” is too vague because it does not choose the event.
- **Observable procedure:** a timestamped self-log could record when the participant reports that event. A timestamped photo would instead record when the image was made, so the study must choose what the timestamp is meant to represent rather than treating the two procedures as interchangeable.
- **Omission:** a dinner-only record leaves out late snacks. That omission matters if the intended exposure record is supposed to cover all eating after the designated dinner.
- **Repair:** name the dinner event, the recording tool, the permitted time window, and the rule for incomplete records. Add a separate timestamped entry for any late snack and state in advance whether that entry changes the dinner-time record or remains a second quantity.

Now audit **next-morning fasting glucose**.

- **Target:** the glucose quantity at the study's specified next-morning time under its stated fasting condition.
- **Observable procedure:** choose one operation, such as the first reading from a named fingerstick meter under a written protocol, or a laboratory draw collected under a written protocol. A meter reading and a laboratory result are different operations; the record must identify which produced the value.
- **Irrelevant influence:** meter miscalibration can move the recorded value even though it is not part of the intended glucose quantity.
- **Repair:** state the collection window, fasting rule, device or laboratory method, handling of failed readings, and reference-check procedure. Record the measuring system with each result so a change of system is visible.

These repairs do not decide whether dinner timing affects glucose. They make the exposure and outcome records inspectable.

## Separate scatter from shift

Suppose a supplied fictional record contains several readings made under the same stated conditions. Reading-to-reading scatter represents a random error component. Averaging can reduce the influence of that scatter when the varying component is suitably centered and the readings are sufficiently independent.

Now suppose the same meter reads consistently high when checked against an appropriate reference. That shared shift represents a systematic error component. Averaging the shifted readings does not remove the shift; a reference check can reveal it and direct investigation or correction.

Keep the judgments separate:

> close together → evidence about precision under stated conditions  
> shifted together from a reference → evidence about systematic error

Neither judgment answers what the fasting-glucose record means as an indicator of the intended construct.

## Name what stayed the same and what changed

For a **repeatability** check in this teaching record, compare readings with the same meter, person, procedure, and day. The question is how closely the operation agrees when the named conditions are held fixed.

For a **reproducibility** check, deliberately change a condition and name it. Compare results from different meters or from different laboratories using the specified procedures. Agreement across those changes adds reproducibility evidence; disagreement identifies a condition that needs investigation. It does not, by itself, identify a cause or validate the broader study.

## Ask whether one reading represents the intended construct

The proposed interpretation is broader than “this operation produced this value.” The study wants a single next-morning fasting reading to stand for an intended overnight-glucose construct. Audit that interpretation before accepting it.

- **Coverage:** state exactly what the construct includes. If it concerns a pattern across the overnight interval, one reading samples only one point and may leave part of the intended construct unrepresented.
- **Convergent evidence:** predict how the reading should relate to another defensible operation for the same or a closely related construct, such as a paired result from the alternative specified glucose procedure.
- **Discriminant evidence:** predict a weaker or distinct relationship with a defensible measure of a different construct. Name that comparison before inspecting the result.
- **Setting evidence:** check whether the proposed interpretation remains supported in each setting where the study will use it, such as the specified home-recording and laboratory-collection settings.

A reading can be highly repeatable and still provide too narrow or contaminated a representation of the intended construct. Consistency is useful evidence, but it is not sufficient for construct validity.

## Bounded measurement judgment

The repaired plan names both targets, makes both operations observable, separates random scatter from systematic shift, distinguishes same-condition repeatability from changed-condition reproducibility, and asks what evidence supports the single-reading interpretation.

Stop there. Do not reopen study design or sampling, and do not add confounding or a bias taxonomy to this audit.
