# The cells that stop and stay

The loop in scene 1 offers a cell two futures. Divide, or be removed. There is a third, and it is not on the diagram because it is not part of the loop: stop dividing permanently and stay where you are, alive.

That state has a name, it is common, and almost everything a learner arrives believing about it is wrong.

## What senescence is

:::{definition}
:id: definition-senescence

**Cellular senescence** is an essentially irreversible arrest of cell proliferation, entered when a cell experiences stress of a kind that could otherwise make it cancerous. A senescent cell stays viable and metabolically active. It has stopped dividing and it has not been removed.
:::

Three parts of that definition need separating out, because each one contradicts a common assumption.

*Essentially irreversible.* You already know from lesson 01 that not dividing and being unable to divide are different things: the definition of a stem cell given there was about capacity rather than current activity, and a quiescent stem cell can be activated. Senescence is the other case. The arrest is a durable one, and its permanence is the property that makes the rest of the story work.

*Viable and metabolically active.* The cell is not dying, not shrinking, not being cleared. It is present, running its metabolism, and as the next section shows, secreting.

*Stress that could make it cancerous.* This is the part that dismantles "senescent means old". The triggers are damage and inappropriate signalling: telomere shortening that leaves the telomeres unable to do their job, breaks through both strands of the DNA, activation of an oncogene, disturbance of the epigenome, and too much mitogenic signalling.

Three of those names belong to machinery this lesson does not own, and it is parking them rather than teaching them. A telomere is part of a chromosome's structure; the epigenome is the set of chemical marks on DNA and its packaging that help set which genes a cell reads; a mitogenic signal is one whose message is to divide. Take those as vocabulary at dictionary level, because nothing in this lesson turns on their detail, and expect the chromosome and cell-cycle material to be where they are properly developed.

What the list is for is its shape. Every entry is either damage or a wrong signal. Telomere shortening is one item among five, and it is the one that made everyone think the word senescence meant elapsed time. The category is defined by the trigger being oncogenic stress, not by the cell having been around a long while.

Take the oncogene entry seriously, because it is the cleanest result in the field. Switch on an oncogene in a cell and the cell may respond by arresting permanently. The change that ought to make it divide without limit instead stops it dividing at all. This is called oncogene-induced senescence, and it means a cell has a response available to a dangerous mutation that neither divides nor dies.

## What it buys

The permanence is the point. A cell that has sustained oncogenic stress and can never divide again cannot found a growing population, whatever else is wrong with it. That is why the senescence response is read as having evolved at least partly to suppress the development of cancer, and why it acts as a strong constraint on the formation of malignant tumours.

One word in that sentence needs marking. *Malignant* is the source's word and it does specific work: it separates growths that stay where they arose from those that do not. What makes a growth capable of leaving its site of origin is not this lesson's material, and the lesson uses the word as a label without unpacking it.

Put it beside scene 2 and you have two different solutions to the same problem. A cell that must not be allowed to divide can be removed from the tissue by apoptosis, or held in the tissue permanently arrested by senescence. Both stop the lineage. They leave the tissue in different states, and that difference is where the trouble starts.

## What it costs

A senescent cell is still there, and it is not quiet. Senescent cells display what is called the senescence-associated secretory phenotype: they secrete numerous proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines, growth factors and proteases into the tissue around them.

That secretion is the mechanism behind the second half of the account. Senescence may act as a basic ageing process that drives ageing phenotypes and age-related pathology later in life. And the secretions can go the other way on cancer too: by sustaining chronic inflammation and making the local environment permissive, the same secretory phenotype that follows from a cancer-suppressing arrest can promote the development of cancer.

Read that last sentence again, because it is not a contradiction to be resolved. The same response, in the same cell, in the same tissue, both constrains tumours and can encourage them, depending on when in a life it happens and what else is present.

## Do not tidy this up

The framework for holding two opposed effects of one mechanism is antagonistic pleiotropy: a trait selected for its benefit at one stage of life carries costs at another, and selection is weaker on late-life costs. Senescence fits it. Arresting a damaged cell early in life prevents a growing population of damaged cells; the same arrested cells accumulating and secreting across decades contribute to age-related pathology.

So resist three tidy statements, all of which you will meet.

"Senescence is what happens to old cells." No: it is a response to oncogenic stress, and elapsed time is not its definition.

"Senescence is a failure of the cell." No: it is a functioning response with a demonstrated protective role, which is why it is described as having evolved for that purpose.

"Senescence is good, or senescence is bad." Neither, and the field does not claim either. It is a mechanism with effects in both directions, and the honest position is to say which effect you are talking about and over what timescale.

A learner who can hold that shape will handle the rest of this lesson, because cancer has the same shape. One change, several possible consequences, decided by context.

:::{check}
:id: check-three-fates

A colonic crypt cell sustains DNA damage severe enough that it must not divide again. Three routes are available: apoptosis, senescence, or continuing to divide.

Describe what the tissue is left holding in each case, and say which of the three leaves a cell in the tissue that is secreting into it.

**Answers.** With apoptosis, the tissue is left holding nothing from that cell. It condenses, breaks into membrane-bounded fragments, is cleared by neighbours or shed from the surface, and provokes no inflammatory response. The lineage ends and the cell is gone.

With senescence, the tissue is left holding a living, metabolically active cell that will never divide again and that secretes proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines, growth factors and proteases into its surroundings. The lineage ends and the cell stays. This is the route that leaves a secreting cell in the tissue.

With continued division, the tissue is left holding a growing population of cells carrying the damage. The lineage does not end, and the number of cells in the crypt begins to depart from the balance scene 1 established.

The comparison is worth making because the first two are both successful outcomes for the tissue in the short term and they are not equivalent in the long term. A tissue that solves every dangerous cell by senescence accumulates secreting cells; one that solves them all by apoptosis has to replace them.
:::
