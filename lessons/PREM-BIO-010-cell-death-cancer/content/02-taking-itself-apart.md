# A cell that takes itself apart

Two cells die in a tissue section. The first has swollen, its plasma membrane has given way, and its contents have gone out into the space around it. Neighbouring cells look normal; a rim of inflammatory cells has arrived. The second has shrunk, its chromatin has condensed, and it has broken into several compact fragments, each one still wrapped in intact membrane. There is no inflammatory rim. A neighbouring epithelial cell has already engulfed two of the fragments.

Both cells are dead. Almost nothing else about them is the same, and the difference is not the amount of damage. The second cell did that to itself.

## The observation that named it

In 1972 Kerr, Wyllie and Currie collected these tidy deaths from a range of tissues and argued that they were one phenomenon. They proposed the name apoptosis for a mechanism of controlled cell deletion, and made a claim about what it is for: it plays a complementary but opposite role to mitosis in regulating animal cell populations.

Set that beside the loop from scene 1. Mitosis at the base, apoptosis at the top, and a cell number that stays constant because the two are matched. The 1972 paper reached that reading from morphology alone, meaning from what the cells looked like under a microscope and nothing else, and it holds.

The structural changes they described come in two stages. First the nucleus and cytoplasm condense and the cell breaks into a number of membrane-bound fragments whose internal structure is still well preserved. Then those fragments, which they called apoptotic bodies, are either shed from epithelial-lined surfaces or taken up by other cells. They read the morphology as evidence of an active, inherently programmed process, one that environmental stimuli can start or hold off, and they reported it in the ordinary turnover of many healthy adult tissues.

Notice the phrase *shed from epithelial-lined surfaces*. The crypt is an epithelial-lined surface, and one of the two disposal routes in the original description is the one this block has been calling shedding since lesson 01.

:::{definition}
:id: definition-apoptosis

**Apoptosis** is a form of cell death that the dying cell carries out itself, using machinery it already contains, with a characteristic result: the cell condenses, breaks into membrane-bounded fragments, keeps its contents inside them, and is cleared by neighbouring or phagocytic cells without provoking an inflammatory response.

The word names a *programme*, not a degree of injury. A cell running it has not been overwhelmed; it has been switched on.
:::

## Why the tidiness is the point, not a detail

The colonic epithelium is one cell thick, and its job is to keep the lumen on the lumen side. Now put the two death styles into it.

An apoptotic cell keeps its plasma membrane intact even late in its disassembly, so its cytoplasmic contents are not released into the interstitium. It is cleared quickly, and there is essentially no inflammatory reaction associated with the process. The cell that has lost membrane integrity does the opposite: its contents go out into the surrounding tissue, chemotactic signals follow, inflammatory cells are recruited, and inflammation is usually present.

That second outcome is manageable as an occasional accident. As a routine disposal method it would not be. Around 175 cells a day leave one crypt, and a tissue disposing of them by spilling their contents under a sheet that has to stay sealed would be running a permanent inflammatory response as the price of ordinary maintenance. Whether the tissue could tolerate that is not something this lesson has measured, and the reasoning here is the lesson's own and not a quoted finding. What the sources establish is narrower and enough: the tidy route exists, it is the one the tissue uses for turnover, and it does not provoke inflammation.

Lesson 04 already gave you the signal that makes the clearance work. Phosphatidylserine is normally held on the cytoplasmic side of the plasma membrane. On an apoptotic cell it appears on the outer surface, where it is a recognition ligand for phagocytes. A molecule the outside world never sees becomes visible, and that visibility is the message. The cell does not merely die neatly; it labels itself for collection.

## How much machinery you actually need

Enough of the machinery to reason with, and no more. The syllabus for this module excludes memorising every signalling intermediate, and this is the place that exclusion bites hardest.

Apoptosis is a coordinated and often energy-dependent process that runs through a family of proteases called caspases. They divide into two functional groups: initiators, which respond to the signal that starts the process, and executioners, which do the disassembly. Two routes reach them. An extrinsic route starts at death receptors in the plasma membrane, which belong to the tumour necrosis factor receptor family, so the signal to die can arrive from outside the cell. An intrinsic route starts inside, at the mitochondrion, which releases proteins from its intermembrane space into the cytosol; cytochrome c is one of them, and it assembles with Apaf-1 and procaspase-9 into a complex called the apoptosome. Both routes converge on the executioners. Once caspases are activated there seems to be an irreversible commitment, and the whole process can be over in two to three hours.

What to hold: two routes, one from outside and one from inside, converging on a shared execution step that runs quickly and does not reverse. What not to hold: the numbering. Which caspase carries which number is a lookup, and no question in this lesson turns on it.

## The word "necrosis" is doing less work than it looks

Here the vocabulary needs care, because the clean pairing of apoptosis against necrosis is a simplification and the field has retired it.

The Nomenclature Committee on Cell Death draws its main line somewhere else: between accidental cell death, the immediate and catastrophic end of a cell exposed to a severe physical, chemical or mechanical insult, and regulated cell death, which relies on dedicated molecular machinery and can therefore be delayed or accelerated by a genetic or pharmacological intervention. Programmed cell death, in the committee's usage, names the regulated forms that run in ordinary physiology without any outside perturbation. And the committee deliberately classifies death on genetic, biochemical, pharmacological and functional grounds instead of on how the cell looks, because there are regulated pathways whose end result looks necrotic. Necroptosis and pyroptosis are two.

So "necrosis" describes an appearance: a cell that swelled and burst. It does not by itself tell you whether machinery was running. The contrast that survives is between a death the cell executed and a death that happened to it, and that contrast is testable, because you can interfere with machinery and see the death change.

:::{check}
:id: check-programme-not-damage

A researcher applies a compound that blocks caspase activity to a monolayer of epithelial cells, then applies a stimulus that normally kills them. Cell death is delayed by several hours.

Two questions. What does that result establish about the death being studied? And would the same experiment distinguish accidental cell death from a regulated pathway with a necrotic appearance?

**Answers.** First, it establishes that the death depends on machinery the cell contains, because interfering with one component changed the timing. That is the defining property of regulated cell death, and it is why the committee draws its line at machinery and not at appearance. It does not on its own establish that the pathway is apoptosis, since caspases participate in more than one regulated route.

Second, it would separate accidental death from regulated death, because accidental death by definition does not depend on a dedicated pathway and so a caspase inhibitor should not delay it. It would not reliably separate apoptosis from a regulated pathway that ends up looking necrotic; for that you need the biochemical and genetic evidence the committee's scheme is built on, and appearance in a section is not enough.
:::
