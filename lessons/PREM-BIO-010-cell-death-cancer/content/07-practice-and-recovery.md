# Practice: work the balance, then say what you cannot tell

Three tasks, with the support falling away. Attempt each one before reading its answer, and for the last one write your uncertainty down as part of the answer, where it belongs.

## Task 1, with the steps supplied

A crypt holds about 700 cells and produces about 175 a day. Suppose the shortfall in removal is much smaller than the one per cent used in scene 5, and the crypt's population takes ten years to double instead of a little over a year.

**Step 1.** Convert ten years to days.

**Step 2.** Work out how many extra cells per day accumulate if 700 extra cells arrive over that period.

**Step 3.** Express that daily excess as a percentage of the 175 cells a day being produced.

**Step 4.** Say in one sentence what the number means for anyone trying to detect the imbalance.

**Answers.** Step 1: ten years is about 3650 days. Step 2: 700 cells divided by 3650 days is about 0.19 cells per day. Step 3: 0.19 divided by 175 is about 0.0011, so roughly 0.11 per cent, a little over one part in a thousand. Step 4: a mismatch of one part in a thousand between production and removal is enough to double a crypt's population within a decade, which means no measurement of production or removal accurate to a few per cent could distinguish a crypt in balance from one that is not.

Check the direction before moving on. A smaller shortfall gives a longer doubling time, and 0.11 per cent is smaller than the one per cent that gave 400 days. The two results are consistent.

## Task 2, with less help

Three findings from three different tissue preparations. For each one, decide which of scene 5's three failure modes it points at, and name the mode.

**Finding A.** Cells in the upper part of a crypt are still expressing the gene programme characteristic of cells at the base, and are still dividing. Cells reaching the surface are being removed normally.

**Finding B.** An epithelial monolayer is crowded well past its normal density. Cell number does not come back down, and no extrusion events are seen.

**Finding C.** Detached epithelial cells recovered from a culture medium are alive, and a proportion of them go on to divide.

**Answers.** Finding A is the second failure mode: the climb stops ending. The population arriving at the top is too large because cells that should have stopped dividing and differentiated are still running the base programme, which is the consequence lesson 06 identified for a cell whose position reading has been decoupled. Removal is explicitly stated to be working.

Finding B is the third mode, removal failing, and specifically the extrusion route rather than the death programme. Crowding normally drives extrusion and brings cell number back down; here it does not.

Finding C is also the third mode, and also removal, but by a different route: these cells have lost the attachment-dependence of their survival, so detachment is no longer sufficient to start apoptosis. Distinguishing B from C matters because they are different repairs to the same imbalance, and because a section through a tissue would not tell them apart.

## Task 3, unscaffolded

Two preparations of the same epithelium.

**Preparation 1.** Cell number is above normal. Apoptotic cells are present at the surface at the usual frequency, with condensed nuclei and intact membranes, and are being cleared without any inflammatory infiltrate.

**Preparation 2.** Cell number is above normal. Apoptotic cells are almost absent. Scattered cells have swollen and lost membrane integrity, and there is an inflammatory infiltrate.

Say for each preparation which controls appear to be operating and which do not, then state the two most important things neither description lets you decide.

**Answer.** Preparation 1: the apoptotic programme is operating and so is clearance, since the cells show the condensation and membrane integrity of scene 2 and are being removed without inflammation. So a failure of the death programme is the one explanation this description argues against.

Now resist finishing the sentence. It is tempting to conclude that the imbalance must therefore be on the production side, and the description does not license that, because it says nothing about the other removal route. Scene 3 established that extrusion is separable from death: a sheet can fail to extrude live cells when it becomes crowded while its apoptotic machinery works perfectly. Nothing here reports extrusion events at all. So preparation 1 is consistent with more cells being made, with cells failing to stop dividing on the climb, and with failed live-cell extrusion, and it separates none of the three.

Preparation 2: the apoptotic route is not visibly operating, and the cells that are dying are dying with membrane rupture and an inflammatory response, which is the accidental-appearing death of scene 2 and not the programme. So removal by apoptosis looks impaired here, and cell number is high, which is consistent.

The two things neither description settles. First, whether the raised cell number arose from increased production or from failed removal, in either preparation: a single snapshot of cell number and dying cells gives you a state and not a rate, and the whole argument of scene 5 was about rates. You would need the production rate and the loss rate, measured separately, to attribute the imbalance. Second, in preparation 2, whether the ruptured cells died accidentally or by a regulated pathway with a necrotic appearance. Scene 2's point was that appearance does not settle that question; you would need to interfere with the machinery and see whether the death changed.

If you did conclude "production side" for preparation 1, that is the most instructive wrong answer in this lesson, and it comes from treating apoptosis as the whole of removal. It is one of the two removal routes this lesson has described, and a sheet can lose the other one on its own.

If you wrote only the first of those two, that is the more important one and the answer is a good one. If you wrote neither and instead named a mutation, reread scene 6: none of these descriptions contains any information about a genome.

## Accessibility and alternatives

Everything this lesson assesses can be done without seeing either diagram. Both carry a long description that states their nodes, every declared relationship, the direction of each one, and the conclusion the picture is meant to deliver, and the surrounding prose states the same relationships in sentences. The renewal loop is five stages joined in a closed ring, and its teaching point is a single relationship: the rate of loss sets the rate that must be replaced. The driver-mutation map is five ideas with two limiting links, and its teaching point is that the same mutation sits on both paths.

The arithmetic in scene 5 and in task 1 is worked in words and full sentences as well as in symbols, and the one displayed equation is read aloud in the prose immediately below it: the number of cells in a crypt rises exactly as fast as production exceeds loss. No result in this lesson depends on reading a figure, on colour, on position on a page, or on any interaction. No task asks a reader for any information about their own body, health, family history or tissue, and none ever will.

## Recovery route

If this lesson has been hard going, the difficulty is almost always in one of three places, and each has a specific repair.

**The loop and its arithmetic did not land.** Reread scene 1 of this lesson, then lesson 01 of this block, scene "What seven hundred cells do that one cannot", which is where the 700 cells, the three-to-five-day renewal and the production-equals-loss statement were established. You need three things from it and nothing else: the cell count, the renewal interval, and the sentence that cells must leave at the same average rate they are made.

**The position-reading argument in scene 5 did not land.** Go to lesson 06 of this block, scenes 2 and 9. Scene 2 builds the destruction complex and how a signal arriving as an inhibitor of an inhibitor raises the output; scene 9 draws the consequence for a cell with damaged APC. If you take one sentence, take that lesson's own: the gradient outside the cell is unchanged, and what has broken is the reading.

**The attachment argument in scene 3 did not land.** Go to lesson 07 of this block, scene 4 for the junction map and the basal integrins, and scene 6 for the statement that an enterocyte never leaves the sheet and is shed only at the end of its journey. Anoikis is what makes that last clause do work: leaving the sheet and staying alive are ordinarily incompatible for this cell.

None of these is a prerequisite you were supposed to have memorised. Retrieving a fact you half-remember is a slower route to the same place, and taking it is not a sign that this lesson was too advanced.
