# Attachment as permission to stay alive

Lesson 07 was firm about one thing. An enterocyte never leaves the sheet. It stays joined to its neighbours by every attachment in its junctional map for the whole journey up the crypt, and it is shed only at the end of that journey. There is no free leading edge, no crawling, no turning around.

Which raises a question lesson 07 did not answer. If the cell is held on the whole way, what makes the end the end?

## An experiment on cells that had nowhere to sit

In 1994 Frisch and Francis took epithelial cells, the MDCK line derived from dog kidney, and disrupted the interactions between them and the extracellular matrix. The cells died, and they died by apoptosis. The authors named the phenomenon anoikis, from the Greek for homelessness.

Read what that result says. These cells were not injured, starved or poisoned. They were denied a surface to attach to. Attachment was supplying something the cell needed in order to keep running, and removing it started the programme from scene 2.

:::{definition}
:id: definition-anoikis

**Anoikis** is apoptosis triggered by the loss of, or by inappropriate, attachment between a cell and the extracellular matrix. It is the same execution machinery described in scene 2, reached by a different starting condition: a cell finds itself somewhere it should not be, and the response is to run the programme.

The name is Greek for homelessness, which is unusually apt. What the cell has lost is not a nutrient or a signal from another cell. It has lost its address.
:::

Two details from the 1994 work matter more than the headline. First, sensitivity was regulated rather than universal: anoikis did not occur in normal fibroblasts, so this is a property of some cell types and not a general consequence of floating free. Second, it could be switched off. Two of the interventions that switched it off used *oncogenes*, which are genes whose activation contributes to a cell's growth advantage; putting one into a cell and changing how the cell grows is what *transformation* means here. Introducing either of two such genes, v-Ha-ras or v-src, abolished anoikis in epithelial cells. So did a chemical treatment with a phorbol ester, and so did making the cell produce extra quantities of a protein called bcl-2. The authors suggested that getting around anoikis accompanies the acquisition of anchorage independence, meaning the ability to grow without a surface to sit on, or of motility. A later review adds the reason anyone cares: losing anoikis is *thought* to make it easier for cells to survive away from where they started and settle somewhere else, which is what metastasis means.

Hold that second point. A cell that survives detachment is doing something the experiment says takes a specific change to achieve.

Scope the finding honestly before carrying it anywhere. This is a cell line in culture, from dog kidney, in 1994, with attachment removed abruptly by the experimenter. It establishes that epithelial survival can be attachment-dependent and that the dependence can be broken. It does not by itself establish what happens at the top of a human colonic crypt.

## What the crypt actually supplies, and what it does not

Lesson 07 gave you the attachment in question. Epithelial cells of the human intestinal crypt bind a basement membrane of laminins and fibronectin through integrins, with integrin-linked kinase joining the integrin beta1 tail to actin inside the cell. That is the grip whose loss anoikis responds to, in the tissue this block follows.

And the connection is not this lesson's guess. The same review of the human intestinal crypt states that cell survival is tightly regulated by RGD-dependent interactions between matrix and integrins, and that integrin receptors including alpha5beta1 and the alphaV integrins have a central role in controlling anoikis, meaning apoptosis on loss of attachment. So attachment-dependent survival is documented in this tissue and not only in a dog kidney line, and the receptors doing it are the ones lesson 07 already put at the base of the cell.

Now the honest part, and it is the most important paragraph in the scene.

The best-studied version of shedding in the intestine is at the tip of a small-intestinal villus, where cells are lost from a region called the extrusion zone. A 2015 review of that process states plainly that it is not known whether cell death precedes the start of detachment or whether detachment itself causes the death, the second of those being anoikis. The same review reports evidence pointing the other way, that the apoptotic pathway appears to be activated before cells reach the extrusion zone at all.

So the order of events is open. Both stories are live: a cell might detach and then die, or die and then be pushed out. This lesson is not going to close that question, and neither should you when you meet it in an exam or a paper. Anoikis is established as a real phenomenon and a real capability of epithelial cells. Anoikis as *the* trigger for shedding at an intestinal surface is a candidate mechanism with contrary evidence beside it.

The mechanics of the removal itself are better settled. Tight junction protein ZO-1 redistributes about fifteen minutes before a cell is shed, and after the cell leaves, the gap it opens is sealed by neighbours re-forming tight junctions within about twenty minutes. The sheet closes behind the departing cell. Barrier and disposal are solved together, which is what a one-cell-thick lining has to do.

One number, carefully scoped: an estimated 1400 mature enterocytes are shed from a single small-intestinal villus tip every 24 hours. That is a small-intestinal measurement from a review about small-intestinal villi, and this lesson does not carry it over to the colon, whose surface is arranged differently. Use it for the sense of scale it gives and nothing more.

## The tissue decides, and crowding is how

Here is the finding that shifts the whole account, and it is why this scene is where the phrase *tissue context* first earns its place.

Epithelia extrude cells in response to crowding. When a monolayer becomes too dense, cells leave, and the number comes back down. In 2012 Eisenhoffer and colleagues showed that most of the cells leaving this way were still alive when they left: they were caspase-negative in 80 per cent of extrusions from human colon sections, 88 per cent in zebrafish epidermis and 67 per cent in cultured monolayers. Crowding was doing the selecting, and a stretch-activated ion channel, Piezo1, was required. Block the stretch-activated channels with gadolinium, or knock Piezo1 down in zebrafish, and extrusion falls off.

:::{definition}
:id: definition-extrusion

**Extrusion** is the squeezing of a single cell out of an epithelial sheet by its neighbours, which close the gap behind it. Lesson 07 used the word once without defining it; this is the sense meant. Extrusion is a mechanical removal, and it is separable from death: a cell may be dead before it goes, or alive as it goes and die afterwards.
:::

The order of cause and effect flips in this account. In anoikis as usually pictured, a cell loses attachment and therefore dies. In crowding-induced extrusion, the tissue removes a living cell because there are too many cells, and the cell then dies because it is outside. The authors' own reading is that most extruded cells die from anoikis after the fact, from loss of survival signals from the matrix they no longer touch. They present that as the likely outcome rather than something they demonstrated in the animal, and the honest summary is that the mechanical step is established and the death that follows it is inferred.

What is established is enough to change how you think about the loop. Removal is not a private decision by a worn-out cell. It is a property of the sheet, responding to how many cells the sheet is carrying. Position and density decide who leaves. Nothing about the cell's genome has entered the story yet.

## What blocking removal did

The last part of the 2012 result is the bridge to the rest of the lesson. When extrusion was blocked, cells did not simply stay put and behave. Zebrafish treated with gadolinium developed epidermal cell masses at the fin edges, and Piezo1 morphants developed the same masses at fin edges and other sites of tissue strain. Cell density stayed elevated where control tissue's density had recovered.

The authors go one step further and suggest that such masses may set the stage for tumourigenesis by allowing defective cells to be retained, noting that colon polyp sections showed increased cell density and no clearly identified extrusions compared with control sections. Weigh that carefully. The masses in zebrafish are a demonstrated consequence of blocking extrusion. The link to tumour formation in a human colon is a suggestion supported by a difference between polyp and control sections, which is an association in tissue examined once. It is a reasonable hypothesis and it is not a demonstrated causal chain, and the paper says as much.

:::{check}
:id: check-detachment-survivor

A cultured epithelial cell line is detached from its substrate and kept in suspension. Most cells die within hours. One subpopulation survives, divides in suspension, and forms colonies.

What has that subpopulation lost, in the terms of this scene? And name one thing this observation does *not* establish about it.

**Answers.** It has lost anoikis: the attachment-dependence of its own survival. Detachment is no longer sufficient to start the programme in these cells, which is exactly what the 1994 work produced deliberately by transforming epithelial cells with v-Ha-ras or v-src, and what overexpressed bcl-2 also achieved.

What it does not establish: that these cells would form a tumour, or spread to another site, in an animal or a person. Surviving in suspension is one capability. The 1994 authors put it as circumvention of anoikis accompanying anchorage independence or motility, and a later review puts the metastasis connection as something anoikis loss is *thought* to facilitate. Both are careful with the verb, and so should you be. Growing in a flask without a floor is a long way short of a disease.
:::

The 1994 Journal of Cell Biology paper by Frisch and Francis supplies the induction of apoptosis by disruption of interactions between normal epithelial cells and extracellular matrix in MDCK cells, the naming of the phenomenon anoikis, the absence of anoikis in normal fibroblasts, its abrogation by transformation with v-Ha-ras or v-src or by phorbol-ester treatment, the protection given by bcl-2 overexpression, and the authors' suggestion that circumvention of anoikis accompanies the acquisition of anchorage independence or cell motility. A 2008 review supplies the Greek meaning of the name as homelessness, the description of anoikis as a specialised form of apoptosis following loss of attachment, the statement that inappropriate loss of anoikis is thought to facilitate the movement of cells to remote sites as occurs in metastasis, and the bypassing of epithelial anoikis by oncogenic Ras or Src.

A 2012 review of the human intestinal crypt supplies the integrin attachment to a basement membrane containing laminins and fibronectin and the integrin-linked kinase connection to actin, carried forward from lesson 07. A 2015 review of small-intestinal villus-tip shedding supplies the extrusion zone, the explicit statement that it is not known whether cell death precedes detachment or detachment causes death, the contrary evidence that the apoptotic pathway appears to be activated before cells reach the extrusion zone, the redistribution of ZO-1 approximately fifteen minutes before shedding, the sealing of the resulting gaps by neighbouring cells re-forming tight junctions within about twenty minutes, and the estimate of about 1400 mature enterocytes shed from a single villus tip in 24 hours. Those figures are small-intestinal and are used as such.

The 2012 Nature paper by Eisenhoffer and colleagues supplies crowding-induced extrusion of live cells, the caspase-negative proportions of 80 per cent in human colon sections, 88 per cent in zebrafish epidermis and 67 per cent in cultured monolayers, the requirement for stretch-activated channels shown with gadolinium and for Piezo1 shown by morpholino knockdown in zebrafish, the epidermal cell masses at fin edges and sites of tissue strain when extrusion was blocked, the elevated cell density that failed to recover, the authors' proposal that most extruded cells die from anoikis through loss of matrix survival signals, and their suggestion that such masses may set the stage for tumourigenesis alongside the observation of increased density and no clearly identified extrusions in colon polyp sections.

Two limits are stated in the scene because the sources state them. The order of death and detachment at an intestinal surface is unresolved in the review that examines it most closely, and this lesson leaves it unresolved. The death of extruded cells by anoikis, and the link from blocked extrusion to tumour formation, are the 2012 authors' interpretation and their suggestion respectively, not results they demonstrated, and the scene marks each as such. The judgement that removal is a property of the sheet rather than of the individual cell is this lesson's reading of the crowding result.
