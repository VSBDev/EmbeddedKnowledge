# Clinical wrap-up: reading a growth without diagnosing anyone

:::{callout}
:id: callout-case-boundary
:kind: boundary

This case is a teaching example and not medical advice. The tissue description below is constructed for this lesson from features its sources establish; it is not a real specimen, a real report or a real person, and nothing in it is a diagnostic criterion. The scene carries no diagnosis, no prognosis, no staging, no treatment, no screening recommendation and no personal risk estimate. Clinical oncology management is outside this course's scope by design. What the scene shows is which conclusions this lesson's model licenses about a tissue, and which it does not.
:::

A growth is removed from the wall of a colon and sectioned. The reasoning question is not what to call it. It is this: which parts of the renewal loop have failed here, which are still working, and what does the section refuse to tell you?

That question is answerable with this lesson and nothing else, which is the point of asking it.

## Working the case

:::{worked-example}
:id: worked-example-reading-the-growth

**The description.** A benign growth: an abnormal proliferation of cells, driven by at least one mutation. Compared with control colonic tissue, cell density is increased, and no extrusion events can be clearly identified. Proliferating cells are present well above the region where proliferation normally stops. The cells are still joined into an epithelium and still sitting on their basement membrane.

**Step 1: locate the finding on the loop.**

Cell density is up, so production has exceeded loss and stayed there. That is the whole content of the balance relation from scene 5: the population rises exactly as fast as production exceeds loss. The section tells you the imbalance happened. It does not tell you how fast, because a density is a state and the imbalance is a rate.

**Step 2: which failure modes does the description implicate?**

Two of scene 5's three, and they are visible separately here.

Proliferating cells above the normal proliferative region is the climb failing to end. Cells that should have stopped dividing and finished differentiating have not. That is what lesson 06 predicted for a cell whose position reading has been decoupled from the gradient: it runs the crypt-base programme at every height, and the crypt-base programme is the programme of a dividing, undifferentiated cell.

No clearly identifiable extrusion events, with density raised, is removal failing at the mechanical step. In control colonic tissue, extrusion is what brings density back down when a sheet becomes crowded. Here density is up and extrusion is not being seen.

**Step 3: which controls are demonstrably still operating?**

This step matters more than step 2 and is the one usually skipped.

The cells are still attached to the basement membrane and still joined into an epithelium. So the attachment-dependence of scene 3 has not been tested by this tissue, because nothing here has detached. Whether these cells could survive detachment is unknown from this description, and it is a different question from whether they are dividing too much.

The growth is confined and organised as an epithelium, which means the tissue-level constraints of scene 6 have not all given way. A clone can expand a long way inside a functioning epithelium. Scene 6's skin result is the reference case: about a quarter of the cells in normal eyelid epidermis carried NOTCH mutations, some clones carried two or three drivers, and the epidermis went on working.

**Step 4: what the description does not settle, stated as a list because each item is a separate gap.**

Whether the imbalance arose from raised production or from failed removal. Both are consistent with a raised density, and separating them needs the two rates measured, not a section.

Whether the apoptotic programme is intact. Nothing in the description reports apoptotic cells, their morphology, or their clearance. An absence of a report is not a report of absence.

How many driver mutations are present. A typical tumour carries two to eight, and common adult tumours of the colon often three to six, while normal cells average well under one. The description names at least one and stops there, and the difference between one and six is the difference the whole of scene 6 was about.

What will happen next. Progression from normal epithelium to carcinoma is described as stepwise and takes decades, and no single section establishes where along such a course any tissue sits, or that it will continue along it at all.

**Step 5: what may be concluded.**

That the imbalance is real, and that the differentiation control has given way, while attachment and epithelial organisation are still holding. That much is a genuine and useful reading, and it comes entirely from the loop.

Removal needs more care than that. A single fixed section cannot measure a rate. Extrusion is an event, so not seeing one in a section is consistent with a mechanism that has failed and equally consistent with one working too slowly to catch in a snapshot. The raised density is a reason to suspect removal is not keeping pace, and the reduced extrusion reported in colonic polyps is an association, which this lesson said when it introduced it. Showing that the removal control had failed would take a measurement over time, which this case does not have. So the reading is: one control demonstrably given way, one under suspicion, two still holding.

What may not be concluded is anything about a person. The reading describes a tissue's control state. It does not name a disease, predict a course, or bear on what anyone should do, and this lesson stops where that boundary is.

**Step 6: the self-explanation prompt.** Which step in this analysis would you have skipped if nobody had asked for it?

Almost everyone skips step 3. The trained instinct is to catalogue what has broken, and a tissue is more informative about its intact controls than about its broken ones, because the intact ones tell you which failures have *not* happened. Scene 6's whole argument depends on that asymmetry: a quarter of a normal epidermis carrying driver mutations is a fact about controls that were still working.
:::

## What the loop bought, and what it cost

Lesson 01 of this block opened with a question about architecture. Why is the lining of the colon built from an enormous number of small units instead of a few large ones?

This lesson supplies half of the answer that lesson could not give. A tissue made of small, individually replaceable units can be maintained by replacing units one at a time, continuously, without ever taking the sheet out of service. Removal is per cell. Manufacture is per cell. The barrier stays sealed throughout, because neighbours close the gap a departing cell leaves. The interval measured for that closing, about twenty minutes, comes from work on the small-intestinal villus tip rather than the colon, so read it as the order of magnitude for how fast the sealing is and not as a colonic figure.

The cost arrives with the benefit and is not separable from it. A tissue that maintains itself by continuous replacement is a tissue running division continuously, in one of the fastest-renewing structures the body has, for a lifetime. About 175 divisions a day per crypt, every day, for decades. Each of those divisions is an opportunity for the control on division to fail, and the arithmetic in scene 5 showed how small a standing failure has to be before it matters. Lesson 01's closing observation was that everything about the arrangement depends on division being started, stopped and positioned correctly over decades. This lesson is what that sentence means.

Colorectal cancer is the clinical form of that failure, and it is the reason this block opens and closes on the same structure.

## What the block promised and what remains open

Five earlier lessons named this one. Each promise is now discharged, and it is worth saying which scene did it.

Lesson 01 asked what happens when a cell in the crypt stops obeying the arrangement, and said colorectal cancer is the clinical form of the failure. Scenes 5, 6 and this one. Lesson 02 said this lesson would return to the crypt when division stops being controlled. Scene 5. Lesson 04 established that phosphatidylserine on the outer surface of a dying cell is read as an instruction to engulf it, and handed forward how that removal is controlled. Scenes 2 and 3. Lesson 06 identified the joint that fails when a cell's position reading is decoupled, and left what happens next to this lesson. Scene 5. Lesson 07 said this lesson would return to where cells are shed and to what happens when the climb stops ending. Scenes 3 and 5.

Three questions leave this lesson unresolved, and they are unresolved in the literature rather than merely omitted here.

Whether shedding at an intestinal surface is caused by detachment or preceded by death is not known, and the review that examines it most closely says so and reports evidence on both sides. Which of p53's three responses does the tumour-suppressing work is not settled, and depends on the tissue. And the clearest evidence that normal tissue carries driver mutations while working normally comes from skin; this lesson has no colonic dataset of that kind, so its extension of that finding to the colon is a use of the model and not a measurement.

A lesson that closed those three would be a more satisfying lesson and a less accurate one.
