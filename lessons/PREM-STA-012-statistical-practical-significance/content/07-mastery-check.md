# Show that the two senses stay apart

The mastery check runs on two studies you have not met, so nothing can be answered from memory of the dinner-timing numbers. Six items, retakeable, feedback after the attempt. A calculator is enough.

Both studies are invented for teaching. Neither describes a real trial, clinic or patient, and neither supports any claim about bedtime routines or walking programmes.

A diabetes service has agreed, before reading either paper, that it will change its advice only for an effect on fasting glucose of at least **5 mg/dL**. The service wrote down how it reached that figure and attached the reasoning to its minutes.

| Study | Per group | Difference in mean fasting glucose | Standard error | Two-sided *p* | 95% interval |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| S: structured bedtime routine | 400 | 4.5 mg/dL lower | 1.131 mg/dL | < 0.001 | 2.3 to 6.7 mg/dL |
| T: community walking programme | 2500 | 1.8 mg/dL lower | 0.453 mg/dL | < 0.001 | 0.9 to 2.7 mg/dL |

The two *p*-values are equal to the precision printed. Both round to 0.00007.

The six items ask you to:

1. compute the lower limit of study S's interval from its difference and standard error;
2. classify study S against the service's threshold and say what it supports;
3. say what the two equal *p*-values do and do not let you conclude about the two programmes;
4. repair a sentence that has slid from the technical sense of the word into the everyday one;
5. identify which of four ways of obtaining the 5 mg/dL threshold is defensible;
6. decide what a measurement problem in study T does to the threshold comparison.

Every item is scored on the reasoning as much as the arithmetic. A correct position with the wrong justification earns nothing, and the rubrics say so.

## Recovering from a miss

Each item's feedback names the idea behind the right answer and points at the scene that teaches it.

A miss on item 1 or 2 sends you to the five positions in scene 4, and specifically to the worked example that runs the four steps in order. A miss on item 3 sends you to the three-row table in scene 2, where the same *p*-value appears beside two very different effects. A miss on item 4 sends you to the definition at the top of scene 2. A miss on item 5 sends you to the anchor-based and distribution-based methods in scene 3. A miss on item 6 sends you to the closing scene, where a biased estimate is laid against a threshold and the comparison falls apart.

Retake as often as you like. Nothing is timed.

## Holding on to this

The two senses of the word drift back together with time, and they drift in a predictable way: the definition survives and the reflex does not, so a year from now you will still be able to define statistical significance correctly and will still feel a small lift when a paper reports one. Three returns will hold the separation in place.

- **Within two days.** Take the dinner-timing line, 9.0 mg/dL with an interval of 0.9 to 17.1, and write the one-sentence verdict for a threshold of 3 mg/dL and again for a threshold of 20 mg/dL. The data never change. Watch the verdict move.
- **Within two weeks.** Find any paper reporting a statistically significant clinical result. Write down the effect estimate, the interval, and the threshold the authors used. If there is no threshold in the paper, write down that fact, because its absence is the finding.
- **When you next read a press release.** Check whether the number quoted is a level or a change, and whether it is absolute or relative. Those two questions catch most of what goes wrong before any statistics are involved.
