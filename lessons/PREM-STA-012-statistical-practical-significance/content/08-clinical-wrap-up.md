# Clinical wrap-up: the meeting where the block ends

**Teaching example, not medical advice.** The service, the meeting, the patient, the study and the 5 mg/dL threshold are invented for teaching. Nothing here reports a finding about real patients or supports any change to anyone's care. The fasting glucose diagnostic ranges quoted below are real and are attributed where they appear.

Two blocks ago, in the first lesson of the science block, a clinician noticed something at a bedside. People who ate late seemed to come in with higher morning readings. That observation became a question, the question became a study, the study was designed across the ten lessons that followed, and this block has spent eleven more analysing what it produced.

The service is now sitting with the result, and the meeting has one item on it.

> Difference 9.0 mg/dL. 95% interval 0.9 to 17.1. *p* = 0.029.
>
> Do we change what we tell people?

Four things have to be said before anyone answers, and the block has taught all four.

## Magnitude

The effect is 9.0 mg/dL, about 0.5 mmol/L. That is the estimate, and it is the number the whole discussion is actually about.

Getting it stated first matters, because a meeting that opens with *p* = 0.029 spends the next twenty minutes on whether the finding is real and never returns to how big it is. The *p*-value has already been extracted for everything it holds: the data sit awkwardly with a true difference of zero. It contributes nothing else and it should be set down.

## Uncertainty

The interval runs from 0.9 to 17.1 mg/dL and it is 16.2 mg/dL wide. Against the service's threshold of 5 mg/dL, this is position C.

Someone in the room will point out that the result is statistically significant. The reply is that the threshold the result cleared is zero, and zero is not the number in the service's policy. On the number that is in the policy, the study is compatible with an effect a fifth of the threshold and with an effect three times it.

So the result has statistical significance and its practical significance is undetermined. Those two verdicts sit side by side without contradiction, and a meeting that can hold both at once is a meeting that will make a defensible decision.

That is worth stating in the minutes in exactly those terms, because it is a specific and useful finding. The study has established a direction and left the magnitude open, and a service that knows which of those it has is in a much better position than one that has a *p*-value and a feeling.

## Bias

Everything above assumes the 9.0 is pointing at the right place. The design block spent a great deal of effort on the reasons it might not.

Bias, in the sense **PREM-SCI-008** established, is a systematic distortion of an estimate introduced through design, conduct, measurement, analysis or reporting. It behaves differently from the uncertainty an interval describes. Width comes from how far a repeat of the study would wander; bias moves every repeat the same way. So a biased study can produce a narrow interval sitting confidently around the wrong value, and the arithmetic gives no warning at all.

This particular comparison was observational. People chose their own dinner times. Whatever else differs between people who eat at seven and people who eat at ten, shift work, household composition, sleep, other eating through the day, rides along with the comparison and lands inside the 9.0.

The consequence for this lesson's technique is blunt. Laying an interval against a threshold is only as good as the estimate at its centre. If the estimate is displaced by 6 mg/dL of confounding, the whole band moves with it, the position changes, and the four-step procedure returns a confident answer to the wrong question. The threshold comparison is the last step in a chain, and it inherits every weakness above it.

## Real-world relevance

The last consideration is the one that unsettles people most, and it survives even if the estimate is perfect.

Fasting glucose is a laboratory measurement. Nobody's life is made better or worse by a number on a meter. What the number stands in for is what happens over the following decade to eyes, kidneys, nerves and arteries, and the link between a small persistent shift in morning glucose and any of those outcomes is a separate question this study never asked.

The cohort's mean fasting glucose was 142 mg/dL. The national institute that publishes diagnostic ranges gives 126 mg/dL or above as diabetes, so these are people already diagnosed and already being managed. The decision in front of the service is about management. Moving someone from 146 to 137 changes a category boundary for nobody in this cohort, and whether it changes anything they would notice is the question that no column in the dataset answers.

## What the service actually does

Three decisions, and only the first is about the study.

**The advice does not change.** The study supports the direction and leaves the magnitude open against the threshold the service set. Changing advice on that evidence would mean asking several thousand people to reorganise their evenings for an effect that may be a fifth of the size the service itself judged worth the trouble.

**The larger study gets funded, with the threshold written into the protocol.** The arithmetic in scene 5 gives about 123 people per group for an interval that could clear 5 mg/dL if the effect really is 9.0. That number came out of the threshold, which means the threshold has to exist before recruitment. A trial designed without one produces a result that has to be argued about afterwards, and by then the arguing is contaminated by the answer.

**The threshold itself goes back for review.** The 5 mg/dL was reached by a small group in a room. The people it most affects were not in that room, and the literature on minimal important differences is clear that the reference point is what patients perceive and value. Asking them is a piece of work the service has not done.

## The patient at ten past nine

The consultant from scene 1 still has an appointment. What he says is shorter than any of the above.

He says there is some evidence that eating later is associated with slightly higher morning readings, that the study is small and cannot say how much of a difference it makes, and that if shifting dinner earlier is easy then it is a reasonable thing to try, while it is not something worth losing sleep or a family meal over. Then he moves on to the things that are established.

That is what an honest reading of 9.0 mg/dL, 95% interval 0.9 to 17.1, *p* = 0.029 sounds like at a bedside. It is less than the sentence appeared to promise, and it is what the sentence actually contained.

## Where the block stops

The arithmetic in these twelve lessons is finished. It has done what arithmetic can do: it has taken a bedside observation, turned it into a question, given the question a design, and given the design a number with its uncertainty attached.

The last step is a judgement, and it belongs to people. Whether 9 mg/dL matters depends on what else the person is already carrying, what the change would cost them, what the alternatives are, and what they want. No test returns that. The block ends here deliberately, one step short of the answer, because the step that remains is the one that has to be taken by someone who is accountable for it.
