# Clinical wrap-up: the meeting where the block ends

**Teaching example, not medical advice.** The service, the meeting, the patient, the study and the 5 mg/dL threshold are invented for teaching. Nothing here reports a finding about real patients or supports any change to anyone's care.

Two blocks ago, in the first lesson of the science block, a clinician noticed something at a bedside. People who ate late seemed to come in with higher morning readings. That observation became a question, the question became a study, the study was designed across the ten lessons that followed, and this block has spent eleven more analysing what it produced.

The service is now sitting with the result, and the meeting has one item on it.

> Difference 9.0 mg/dL. 95% interval 0.9 to 17.1. *p* = 0.029.
>
> Do we change what we tell people?

Four things have to be said before anyone answers, and the block has taught all four.

## Magnitude

The effect is 9.0 mg/dL, about 0.5 mmol/L. That is the estimate, and it is the number the whole discussion is actually about.

Getting it stated first matters, because a meeting that opens with *p* = 0.029 may spend the next twenty minutes treating the result as a yes-or-no truth claim and never return to how big the estimate is. The *p*-value has already been extracted for what it contributes here: under the stated model, the data sit awkwardly with a true difference of zero. It does not determine magnitude or practical importance.

## Uncertainty

The interval runs from 0.9 to 17.1 mg/dL and it is 16.2 mg/dL wide. Against the service's threshold of 5 mg/dL, this is position C.

Someone in the room will point out that the result is statistically significant. The reply is that the threshold the result cleared is zero, and zero is not the number in the service's policy. On the number that is in the policy, the study is compatible with an effect a fifth of the threshold and with an effect three times it.

So the result has statistical significance and its practical significance is undetermined. Those two verdicts sit side by side without contradiction, and a meeting that can hold both at once is a meeting that will make a defensible decision.

That is worth stating in the minutes in exactly those terms, because it is a specific and useful reading. Under the stated model and confidence procedure, the data support the positive direction while the interval leaves the threshold comparison open. A service that keeps those statements calibrated is in a better position than one that has a *p*-value and a feeling.

## Bias

Everything above assumes the 9.0 is pointing at the right place. The design block spent a great deal of effort on the reasons it might not.

Bias, in the sense **PREM-SCI-008** established, is a systematic distortion of an estimate introduced through design, conduct, measurement, analysis or reporting. It behaves differently from the random uncertainty represented by a conventional interval. Bias can shift the centre of the repeat-sampling distribution without making the reported interval wider, so a narrow interval does not protect against a systematically displaced estimate.

This particular comparison was observational. People chose their own dinner times. Whatever else differs between people who eat at seven and people who eat at ten, shift work, household composition, sleep, other eating through the day, rides along with the comparison and lands inside the 9.0.

The consequence for this lesson's technique is blunt. Laying an interval against a threshold is only as good as the estimate at its centre. If the estimate is displaced by 6 mg/dL of confounding, the whole band moves with it, the position changes, and the four-step procedure returns a confident answer to the wrong question. The threshold comparison is the last step in a chain, and it inherits every weakness above it.

## Real-world relevance

The last consideration survives even if the estimate is unbiased.

The reported 9 mg/dL is a difference between two invented group means. The pack provides no individual-level values, no paired changes, and no model for how effects vary between people. It therefore cannot say that an individual person's glucose changed by 9 mg/dL, how many participants lie near any diagnostic cut-point, or whether anyone crossed a category. Those are different questions requiring different data.

The service must also decide which patient-relevant outcomes, costs and alternatives belong in its rule. This study reports only a group-level fasting-glucose comparison. It cannot supply those missing judgements, and the invented 5 mg/dL line is a service decision threshold rather than a demonstrated MCID.

## What the service actually does

Three decisions, and only the first is about the study.

**The advice does not change.** The study supports the direction and leaves the magnitude open against the threshold the service set. Changing advice on that evidence would mean asking several thousand people to reorganise their evenings for an effect that may be a fifth of the size the service itself judged worth the trouble.

**The larger study gets funded, with the threshold and operating probability written into the protocol.** The arithmetic in scene 5 gives about 252 people per group for an 80% probability that the lower 95% limit will exceed 5 mg/dL if the true difference is 9.0 mg/dL, under the stated known-spread normal approximation. The target probability, assumed effect, spread and threshold all have to be declared before recruitment. The calculation does not guarantee position A in the realised study.

**The threshold itself goes back for review.** The 5 mg/dL was reached by a small group in a room. The people it most affects were not in that room. Eliciting their preferences could inform the service rule, but it would not by itself be an anchor-based MCID estimate; that would require relating observed change on the target outcome to an independent external anchor. The service must record which construct it is using and how it connects to the between-group effect.

## The patient at ten past nine

The consultant from scene 1 still has an appointment. The invented observational result does not license individual advice: it is a group-level association, its interval crosses the service threshold, and the pack contains no evidence about effects for this patient.

An honest explanation would keep those limits visible and return individual care to established clinical evidence, the patient's circumstances and accountable professional judgement. The lesson does not recommend changing dinner time or any other part of care.

## Where the block stops

The arithmetic in these twelve lessons is finished. It has done what arithmetic can do: it has taken a bedside observation, turned it into a question, given the question a design, and given the design a number with its uncertainty attached.

The last step is a judgement, and it belongs to people. Whether 9 mg/dL matters depends on what else the person is already carrying, what the change would cost them, what the alternatives are, and what they want. No test returns that. The block ends here deliberately, one step short of the answer, because the step that remains is the one that has to be taken by someone who is accountable for it.
