# No map, no address

Pick one cell out of the lining of the large intestine, halfway up a crypt. Counting from the bottom of a tube about 32 cells tall, it is roughly the seventeenth. The cells below it divide. The cells above it have stopped dividing and are finishing the absorptive machinery they will use at the surface. Our cell has to behave like neither the base nor the top, and in a few days it will be at the top itself, doing something different again.

Ask how it knows which of those things to be.

It cannot look. It has no map of the crypt and no coordinates written anywhere inside it. It cannot count the cells below, because it has never been in contact with most of them and there is no wire running down the column. It carries no record of the climb either: it was produced by a division partway up, and nothing was handed to it saying how far its parent had travelled. Lesson 04 of this block (PREM-05.04) established what the whole of its contact with the outside world amounts to: a sheet of lipid two molecules thick, fluid in its own plane, with proteins sitting in it. Anything the cell learns about where it is has to arrive at that sheet as a molecule and be recognised there.

That narrows the question to something answerable. What substance, present in what amount, could tell a cell how far it is from the bottom of a tube?

The crypt's answer is a family of proteins called Wnt. Mesenchymal cells sitting near the crypt base secrete them; mesenchymal cells further away do not. Wnt signalling therefore decreases progressively as you go up the crypt axis, and the stem cells at the base are the cells with the highest Wnt activity. A cell deep in the crypt is bathed in a lot of it. A cell near the surface has almost none. Between those two ends the amount falls off with height, and that falling-off is the quantity this lesson follows: the best-characterised thing in the crypt that varies with position and that a single cell can read at its own surface.

A graded amount of one substance, standing in for a position. That idea is worth pausing on, because it is how a great deal of the body is laid out, and because it is not obvious that it can work at all.

## The word signal is already in use

**Signal** arrives from ordinary English carrying a shape. A traffic signal, a phone signal, a hand signal: in each case something is sent so that something else will change what it does. Biology keeps that. What it adds is that the sender and the receiver are cells, the message is a molecule, and the message has no content beyond how much of it there is and where it binds. A Wnt protein does not say *you are at position seventeen*. It arrives, or it does not arrive, in some quantity, and the cell's machinery does the rest.

Guard against one nearby confusion. Lesson 03 of this block (PREM-05.03) used **signal sequence** for a stretch of a protein's own amino-acid chain that tells the cell where to deliver that protein. A signal sequence is part of the cargo and addresses the inside of a cell. A signal in this lesson's sense is a separate molecule released by one cell and read by another. The two are unrelated mechanisms that happen to share a word.

## What this lesson assumes

Two outcomes sit upstream of this one in the course graph, and they are in different places.

**Membrane structure and fluidity (PREM-05.04)** is lesson 04 of this block, already written. Everything here about receptors sitting in a membrane, and about the apical and basolateral faces of a polarised cell, is that lesson's work and is used without being rebuilt.

**Protein structure and folding (PREM-09.03)** sits in the Biomolecules module, not in this block, and no lesson has been written for it yet. That is a genuine hole underneath this material, because every actor in this lesson is a protein. Instead of promising that almost nothing is needed from it, here is the list of what this lesson actually borrows, with the one-line version of each. When PREM-09.03 exists, read it first and these lines become a reminder.

:::{callout}
:kind: note
:id: callout-borrowed-protein-vocabulary

**What this lesson borrows from the unwritten protein lesson.**

**A protein has a definite shape, and binding something can change it.** This is what lets a receptor turn an outside event into an inside one. How a sequence of amino acids produces a particular fold, and how binding at one end moves the other end, belongs to PREM-09.03.

**One protein can have separable parts that do separable jobs.** A long protein is not a single indivisible lump; different stretches of it bind different partners, so losing one stretch can cost one function while the rest of the molecule survives. The final scene of this lesson turns on this, because a shortened version of one protein keeps some jobs and loses another.

**A scaffold is a protein whose job is to hold other proteins together.** It need not do the chemistry itself. It brings the reacting partners into one place so the reaction happens at a useful rate.

**A protein can be tagged for destruction.** Cells attach a small marker protein, ubiquitin, to a protein they intend to dispose of, and a barrel-shaped machine called the proteasome takes tagged proteins apart. "Marked for ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation" means labelled, then shredded.

**A transcription factor is a protein that changes whether particular genes get read.** It does this by binding DNA, or by binding something already attached to DNA.

**A conserved amino acid** is one that has stayed the same at that position across many species, which usually signals that it matters. Scene 7 refers to a conserved serine; serine is one of the twenty amino acids proteins are built from.
:::

Where this lesson would need more protein chemistry than those lines, it says so and stops.
